## Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF)

**Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF)** is one of the most widely used statistical techniques for converting textual data into numerical feature vectors.

TF-IDF consists of two complementary components:

- **Term Frequency (TF):** Measures how frequently a term appears within a document.
- **Inverse Document Frequency (IDF):** Measures how rare or informative the term is across the entire document collection.

The main objective of TF-IDF is to:

- assign **higher weights to informative and distinctive words**, and
- reduce the influence of **very common words** that carry little discriminative information.

---

### Term Frequency (TF)

Term Frequency simply measures how often a particular word appears in a document.

It is calculated as

​​$$
TF_{t,d}=\frac{f_{t,d}}{|d|}
$$​

where:

- $f_{t,d}$ denotes the number of occurrences of term $t$ in document $d$.
- $|d|$ represents the total number of words in document $d$.

Normalizing by the document length prevents longer documents from receiving artificially larger scores simply because they contain more words.

---

### Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

While TF measures local importance within a document, **Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)** measures the global importance of a word across the entire corpus.

Words appearing in only a few documents receive higher weights because they are generally more informative.

The basic IDF formula is

$$ IDF(t,D)=\log\left(\frac{N}{n_t}\right) $$

where:

- $N$ is the total number of documents.
- $n_t$ is the number of documents containing term $t$.
- $D$ denotes the complete document collection.

A word that appears in every document has little power to distinguish one document from another, and therefore receives a very small IDF value.

---

### Combining TF and IDF

The TF-IDF score is simply the product of TF and IDF:

$$
\mathrm{tfidf}(t,d,D)=\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\times\mathrm{idf}(t,D)
$$

A high TF-IDF score is obtained when:

- the term appears frequently in the current document (high TF), and
- the term appears in relatively few documents across the corpus (high IDF).

Consequently, TF-IDF emphasizes words that best characterize a document while suppressing common, less informative terms.

---

### Example

Consider the following two documents:

**Document A**

```
apple apple apple orange
```

**Document B**

```
apple banana banana banana banana
```

#### Step 1: Compute TF for **apple** in Document A

Since

- $f_{\text{apple},A}=3$
- $|A|=4$

we obtain

$$
tf=\frac{3}{4}
$$​

---

#### Step 2: Compute TF for **apple** in Document B

Since

- $f_{\text{apple},B}=1$
- $|B|=5$

we obtain

$$
tf=\frac{1}{5}
$$​

---

#### Step 3: Compute IDF

The word **apple** appears in both documents.

Therefore,

$$
\Rightarrow n_{\text{apple}} = 2,\quad N = 2
$$

Thus,

$$
idf=\log\left(\frac{2}{2}\right)=0
$$

---

#### Final TF-IDF

Because

$IDF=0,IDF=0,IDF=0,$

the TF-IDF score for **apple** is zero in both documents.

Although the word appears multiple times, it provides no discriminative information because it occurs in every document within the corpus.

This example illustrates one of TF-IDF's key strengths: it combines **local frequency** with **global rarity**, allowing descriptive and distinguishing terms to receive greater importance while common words are automatically down-weighted.

## Implementing TF-IDF in Python

Python's **scikit-learn** library provides a convenient implementation through the `TfidfVectorizer` class located in `sklearn.feature_extraction.text`.

Suppose we have the following corpus:

```python
corpus = [
    "The cat sat on the mat.",
    "The dog barked at the mailman.",
    "Cats and dogs are common pets.",
    "The mailman delivers letters every day."
]
```

Next, create a TF-IDF vectorizer and fit it to the corpus.

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

# Create an instance of TfidfVectorizer
vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer()

# Learn the vocabulary and transform the corpus
X = vectorizer.fit_transform(corpus)
```

After fitting the model, we can inspect the resulting TF-IDF values.

```python
# Extract feature names
feature_names = vectorizer.get_feature_names_out()

# Convert sparse matrix to dense format
dense = X.todense()

# Display TF-IDF scores
for doc_idx, doc in enumerate(dense):
    print(f"Document {doc_idx + 1}:")
    for word_idx, score in enumerate(doc.tolist()[0]):
        if score > 0:
            print(f"  {feature_names[word_idx]}: {score:.4f}")
```

The output is:

Although TF-IDF is computationally efficient and performs remarkably well in many information retrieval and recommendation tasks, it has an important limitation: **it treats every word as an independent token and does not understand semantic relationships between words**.

For example, TF-IDF cannot recognize that the words **book** and **library** are semantically related. Likewise, it cannot infer that **car** and **automobile** refer to nearly the same concept.

Because of this limitation, TF-IDF may perform poorly in applications where semantic similarity plays a critical role. To address this issue, more advanced embedding techniques such as **Word2Vec** were developed.

## Word2Vec

Unlike TF-IDF, which represents words using statistical frequency measures, **Word2Vec** is a machine learning approach that learns dense, continuous vector representations of words. Its objective is to embed words into a high-dimensional vector space in such a way that words with similar meanings are positioned close to one another. As a result, Word2Vec captures not only word occurrences but also semantic and syntactic relationships between words.

Word2Vec is trained using two neural network architectures:

- **Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW)**
    
- **Skip-Gram**
    

Both approaches learn word representations by analyzing the context in which words appear. They process text using a sliding context window and learn either:

- to predict the target word from its surrounding context (CBOW), or
    
- to predict surrounding context words from a target word (Skip-Gram).
    

Through this learning process, the model gradually discovers meaningful relationships among words rather than simply counting their occurrences.

Consequently, the vectors learned by Word2Vec encode semantic similarities and syntactic relationships, making them highly valuable for recommendation systems, information retrieval, and numerous natural language processing tasks.

### Semantic Relationships in Word2Vec

One of the best-known demonstrations of Word2Vec's capabilities is its ability to capture analogical relationships between words.

For example, if we consider the words **King**, **Queen**, **Man**, and **Woman**, the learned word vectors approximately satisfy the following relationship:

## $$  
\mathrm{Vector}(\text{King})

\mathrm{Vector}(\text{Man})  
+  
\mathrm{Vector}(\text{Woman})  
\approx  
\mathrm{Vector}(\text{Queen})  
$$

This remarkable property illustrates that Word2Vec learns meaningful geometric relationships within the embedding space rather than merely memorizing words.

## Implementing Word2Vec in Python

Word2Vec is readily available through the **Gensim** library.

If Gensim is not already installed, it can be installed using:

```bash
pip install gensim
```

---

### Preparing the Data

For this example, we use the tokenization utilities provided by the **NLTK** library.

```python
import nltk
nltk.download('punkt')

from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize

# Sample text
text = "This is a simple example of training Word2Vec using Gensim."

# Tokenize the text
tokens = word_tokenize(text.lower())

print(tokens)
```

---

### Training the Word2Vec Model

Once the text has been tokenized, the model can be trained. Although pre-trained Word2Vec models released by Google are widely available, this example trains a model directly from the sample data.

```python
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

# Train the model
model = Word2Vec(
    [tokens],
    vector_size=100,
    window=5,
    min_count=1,
    workers=4
)
```

The parameters have the following meanings:

- **vector_size** — Dimensionality of the word embeddings.
    
- **window** — Size of the context window used by the CBOW and Skip-Gram architectures.
    
- **min_count** — Minimum number of occurrences required for a word to be included in the vocabulary.
    
- **workers** — Number of CPU cores used for parallel training.

## GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation)

**GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation)** was introduced by researchers at Stanford University in 2014 and is one of the most influential unsupervised methods for learning distributed word representations.

Unlike Word2Vec, which learns embeddings by predicting neighboring words, **GloVe leverages global word co-occurrence statistics** extracted from a large text corpus.

Specifically, GloVe first constructs a **word co-occurrence matrix**, where each element records how frequently two words occur together within a predefined context window. It then factorizes this matrix to obtain vector representations, such that the dot product of two word vectors approximates the logarithm of their co-occurrence frequency.

As a result, GloVe combines the statistical advantages of traditional count-based methods with the expressive power of neural word embeddings, producing vectors that effectively capture semantic relationships between words.

The embeddings learned by GloVe are capable of modeling complex semantic structures as well as linear relationships between words. For example, they naturally preserve analogical relationships such as

$$  
\text{King} - \text{Man} + \text{Woman} \approx \text{Queen}.  
$$

Because of these properties, GloVe has been widely applied to numerous natural language processing tasks, including:

- Sentiment analysis
    
- Semantic similarity measurement
    
- Word analogy tasks
    
- Information retrieval
    
- Recommendation systems
    

Furthermore, pre-trained GloVe embeddings are incorporated into several NLP frameworks, including **spaCy**, where they serve as semantic word features.

## Python Implementation

Pre-trained GloVe models can be conveniently loaded in Python using the **Gensim** library. The following example demonstrates how to load a pre-trained GloVe model, retrieve semantically similar words, and obtain the embedding vector of a specific word.

```python
import gensim.downloader as api

# Load a pre-trained GloVe model
model = api.load("glove-wiki-gigaword-100")

# Find similar words
similar_words = model.most_similar("king", topn=5)
print(similar_words)

# Display the embedding vector for a word
vector = model["king"]
print(vector)
```

In this example, the **`glove-wiki-gigaword-100`** model is loaded. This model represents each word as a **100-dimensional embedding vector**. After loading the model, the code retrieves the five words that are most semantically similar to **"king"** and then prints the corresponding embedding vector for the word **"king"**.

For a more comprehensive understanding of GloVe and its implementation, refer to the official Stanford GloVe documentation and the Gensim documentation.

## Comparison of TF-IDF, Word2Vec, and GloVe

Having introduced three popular text representation techniques, it is useful to compare their characteristics and understand when each method is most appropriate.

### TF-IDF

TF-IDF is a **statistical feature extraction method** that represents documents using sparse vectors based on word frequencies. It is computationally efficient, easy to implement, and performs well on small and medium-sized datasets. However, it does not capture semantic relationships between words because each term is treated independently.

### Word2Vec

Word2Vec learns **dense vector representations** using shallow neural networks trained to predict contextual relationships between words. The resulting embeddings capture semantic and syntactic similarities, allowing the model to recognize relationships such as synonyms, analogies, and contextual similarity. However, training high-quality Word2Vec embeddings generally requires a sufficiently large corpus.

### GloVe

GloVe also produces dense semantic embeddings but differs from Word2Vec by leveraging **global co-occurrence statistics** collected from the entire corpus rather than relying solely on local context prediction. As a result, GloVe often produces embeddings that preserve both local contextual information and global corpus statistics, making it particularly effective for many downstream NLP applications.

The key differences among these methods can be summarized as follows.

|Feature|TF-IDF|Word2Vec|GloVe|
|---|---|---|---|
|Representation type|Sparse vectors|Dense vectors|Dense vectors|
|Learns semantic similarity|✗|✓|✓|
|Captures contextual relationships|✗|✓|✓|
|Based on word frequency statistics|✓|Partially|✓ (global statistics)|
|Neural network training required|✗|✓|✓|
|Computational complexity|Low|Moderate|Moderate to High|
|Suitable for small datasets|✓|Limited|Limited|
|Suitable for large corpora|Limited|✓|✓|

Each technique has its own advantages and limitations. In practice, the most appropriate representation depends on the recommendation task, the size of the available dataset, and the computational resources. Although modern recommendation systems increasingly employ contextual embeddings generated by transformer-based language models such as **BERT**, TF-IDF, Word2Vec, and GloVe remain fundamental techniques whose concepts underpin many contemporary representation learning methods.
