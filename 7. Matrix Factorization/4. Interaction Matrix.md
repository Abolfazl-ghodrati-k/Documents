# Interaction Matrix

In the sections on collaborative filtering, we saw that the central idea is to use users’ past behavior—namely, which items they have liked or interacted with—to predict their future preferences. To represent this **large volume of user–item interaction data** in a structured form that can be processed by algorithms, we use the concept of a **User–Item Interaction Matrix**.

In simple and intuitive terms, an interaction matrix is a large table in which:

- **Rows represent the users** of the system.
    
- **Columns represent the items** available in the system.
    
- **The entries of the table** indicate what kind of **interaction** each user has had with each specific item.
    

This matrix is the central input representation for many collaborative-filtering algorithms, including the matrix-factorization methods studied in this section. It is the same matrix $R$ introduced in the previous lesson, and our goal is to decompose it into the latent-feature matrices $P$ and $Q$.

## Types of Interaction Data: Explicit and Implicit

The values stored in the entries of the interaction matrix may come from two main types of feedback: **explicit feedback** and **implicit feedback**, both of which were discussed in detail in earlier sections.

Matrix-factorization algorithms can be applied to both types of data, although the modeling strategy and the loss function may differ. In this section, our primary focus is on **rating data**, which is a form of explicit feedback, because it is easier to interpret and clearly illustrates the fundamental ideas of matrix factorization.

## Interaction Matrices in the Real World

The next step in understanding interaction matrices is learning how to construct them from raw data. In real-world systems, interaction data is usually stored in files or databases and therefore requires preprocessing.

Efficient construction of an interaction matrix involves steps such as mapping real user and item identifiers to numerical indices suitable for computation and using specialized libraries such as `scipy.sparse` to manage the high degree of sparsity efficiently. The practical details of this process—including data loading, preprocessing, identifier mapping, and sparse-matrix construction—will be covered in the next lesson, _Interaction Matrices in Practice_.

For now, let us examine the main properties of interaction matrices once they have been constructed.

## Challenges and Considerations Related to the Interaction Matrix

The sparse nature of the interaction matrix introduces several challenges that matrix-factorization methods are designed to address:

- **Cold Start:** New users or new items may have very few interactions or no interactions at all. Consequently, their corresponding rows or columns in the interaction matrix are largely empty. Similarity-based methods struggle in this situation because they cannot compute reliable similarities from insufficient interaction data. When at least a small number of interactions are available, matrix-factorization methods can learn approximate latent representations for these users or items and position them in the latent space, making prediction possible.
    
- **Scale and Sparsity in Practice:** Although sparsity may appear relatively moderate in small examples, interaction matrices in real-world systems such as Netflix or Amazon can contain millions of users and millions of items. The proportion of nonzero entries, representing observed interactions, is often below 1% and may even fall below 0.1%. This extreme sparsity is one of the primary reasons for using efficient data structures such as `scipy.sparse` and algorithms such as matrix factorization, which can extract useful patterns from a relatively small number of observed interactions.

More advanced matrix-factorization algorithms often model these biases explicitly by adding user-bias and item-bias terms to the rating-prediction function.

A common bias-aware prediction model is:

$$(\hat{R}_{u,i} = \mu + b_{u} + b_{i} + P[u,:] \cdot Q[:,i])$$

where:

- $\mu$ is the global average rating;
- $b_{u}$ is the bias associated with user $u$;
- $b_{i}$ is the bias associated with item $i$;
- $P[u,:]$ is the latent-feature vector of user $u$;
- $Q[:,i]$ is the latent-feature vector of item $i$.
