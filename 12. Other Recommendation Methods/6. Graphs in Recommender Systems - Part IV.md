## Temporal and Dynamic Graph Learning in Recommender Systems

So far, our discussion has focused on **static graphs**, in which the graph structure and relationships between entities are assumed to remain unchanged over time.

Static Graph Neural Network (GNN) models, such as NGCF and LightGCN, can effectively aggregate neighborhood information. However, they are not inherently designed to capture temporal changes in graph structure, evolving user–item interactions, or changes in node characteristics.

In real-world applications, many networked systems, particularly recommender systems, are inherently **dynamic**.

For example:

- New users continuously join a platform.
- New items are introduced into the catalog.
- Users repeatedly interact with existing and newly introduced items.
- Relationships between users and items evolve as new interactions occur.

These changes introduce a **temporal dimension** into the graph structure.

Accurately modeling this temporal dimension is essential for generating relevant, personalized, and up-to-date recommendations.

## Why Temporal Modeling Matters in Recommender Systems

Several characteristics of real-world recommendation environments make temporal modeling particularly important.

### 1. Changes in User Preferences

User preferences are not static.

An item that interests a user today may no longer be relevant tomorrow.

For example, a user who previously preferred action movies may gradually develop an interest in comedy films.

If a recommender system ignores these temporal changes, it may continue generating recommendations based on outdated preferences.

Temporal modeling enables recommendation algorithms to adapt to evolving user interests and distinguish between historical preferences and more recent behavioral patterns.

### 2. Item Dynamics

The popularity and relevance of items can change substantially over time.

An item may suddenly become popular and subsequently lose its appeal.

Examples include:

- Breaking news articles.
- Trending music.
- Seasonal products.
- Time-sensitive entertainment content.

A recommender system must recognize and respond to these changes to maintain recommendation relevance.

Static representations may fail to capture such rapid shifts because they summarize interactions without explicitly considering when those interactions occurred.

### 3. Emergence of New Relationships

New user–item interactions continuously introduce additional edges into the interaction graph.

These new relationships contain valuable information about user preferences and item characteristics.

Consequently, newly observed interactions should influence the learned embeddings of the corresponding users and items.

Dynamic graph learning provides mechanisms for incorporating these relationships as the graph evolves.

### 4. Dynamic Collaborative Signals

Many traditional **Sequential Recommendation** approaches focus primarily on modeling the interactions within each individual user's sequence.

This is commonly referred to as **intra-sequence modeling**.

However, these approaches may overlook dynamic collaborative relationships between the interaction sequences of different users.

Such relationships are known as **Dynamic Collaborative Signals**.

They capture higher-order connectivity patterns among users and items across different points in time.

For example, two users may exhibit related preferences even when their interaction sequences differ, because their behaviors are connected through shared items or indirect paths in the interaction graph.

Modeling these evolving collaborative relationships can provide additional information for predicting future user behavior.

### 5. Time-Dependent Predictions

In many recommendation tasks, the objective is not simply to estimate whether a user will interact with an item.

Instead, the system must predict future interactions while accounting for the chronological order of previous events.

For example, a sequential recommender system may attempt to predict the next product a user will purchase within a particular time interval.

In such scenarios, the ordering and timing of previous interactions can significantly influence the prediction.

Temporal graph models are designed to incorporate this information directly into the representation-learning process.

---

## Challenges of Learning from Temporal and Dynamic Graphs

Modeling dynamic graphs introduces several challenges beyond those encountered in static graph learning.

### 1. Data Volume

Dynamic graphs often contain enormous amounts of interaction data.

In addition to recording the existence of edges, these datasets must preserve temporal information associated with interactions.

For example, an interaction record may contain:

- The user identifier.
- The item identifier.
- The interaction timestamp.
- Additional contextual or interaction-specific attributes.

As interactions accumulate, both the volume of data and the computational requirements of graph learning increase.

### 2. Structural Complexity

Static graph learning primarily focuses on the relationships represented by a fixed graph structure.

Dynamic graph learning must additionally model how these relationships evolve over time.

This introduces two closely related challenges:

1. Representing continuously changing user–item interactions within a graph.
2. Preserving the chronological order of interactions.

A temporal graph representation must therefore capture both structural connectivity and temporal dependencies.

### 3. Scalability

Real-world recommendation graphs may contain millions or billions of nodes and edges.

Furthermore, their structures may change continuously as new interactions occur.

Temporal graph learning algorithms must process these changes efficiently without requiring prohibitively expensive recomputation.

Scalability is particularly important for large recommendation platforms that receive substantial volumes of interaction events.

### 4. Connecting Historical Information to Future Predictions

An important challenge is determining how to summarize historical interactions in the current representations of users and items.

The learned embeddings should preserve information from previous events that remains useful for predicting future behavior.

At the same time, they must adapt to recent interactions and avoid assigning excessive importance to outdated information.

This requires an effective mechanism for combining historical knowledge with newly observed behavior.

### 5. Encoding Dynamic Collaborative Signals

Another challenge is explicitly representing collaborative relationships that evolve across different users' interaction sequences.

These relationships may involve multiple users and items connected through higher-order paths at different points in time.

A temporal recommendation model must identify and encode such signals so that they can contribute to the prediction of individual user preferences.

## General Approaches to Temporal Graph Learning

Several approaches have been developed to address the challenges of temporal graph learning.

These methods can be broadly classified into two categories:

1. **Snapshot-Based Methods**, also known as Discrete-Time Dynamic Graphs (DTDGs).
2. **Event-Based Methods**, commonly associated with Continuous-Time Dynamic Graphs (CTDGs).

## 1. Snapshot-Based Methods: Discrete-Time Dynamic Graphs

In snapshot-based approaches, a dynamic graph is represented as a sequence of graph snapshots corresponding to different time intervals.

Time is divided into discrete periods, and each snapshot represents the graph structure associated with a particular period.

For example, a recommender system may construct separate interaction graphs for each day, week, or month.

Each snapshot can then be processed using conventional graph learning techniques.

### Learning from Graph Snapshots

A GNN or graph embedding algorithm can be applied to individual snapshots to learn node representations.

To capture temporal dependencies, information from previous snapshots may be incorporated into subsequent ones.

Two common strategies are:

- **Embedding Initialization:** Embeddings learned from one snapshot are used to initialize the embeddings for the next snapshot.
- **Recurrent Modeling:** Recurrent neural network architectures combine embeddings or graph representations across successive time intervals.

These strategies allow information to propagate not only through graph neighborhoods but also across time.

### Advantages

The primary advantage of snapshot-based methods is their compatibility with existing static graph learning techniques.

Since each snapshot can be treated as a static graph, many established GNN and graph embedding algorithms can be reused.

This simplifies model development and implementation.

### Limitations

Snapshot-based approaches also have important disadvantages.

**Loss of fine-grained temporal information:** Changes occurring between snapshots may not be represented accurately.

For example, if a system constructs daily snapshots, interactions occurring at different times within the same day may be treated as belonging to the same temporal interval.

**Limited suitability for rapidly changing graphs:** When graph structures change frequently, coarse snapshots may fail to capture important short-term dynamics.

**High storage and computational costs:** Maintaining and processing multiple snapshots of a very large graph can be expensive, particularly when substantial portions of the graph structure are repeated across snapshots.

These limitations motivate approaches that represent interactions as individual temporal events.

## 2. Event-Based Methods: Continuous-Time Dynamic Graphs

Event-based methods model graph evolution as a continuous stream of interaction events.

These approaches are commonly associated with **Continuous-Time Dynamic Graphs (CTDGs)**.

Rather than dividing time into discrete snapshots, each interaction is represented as an individual event with an associated timestamp.

A basic temporal interaction can be represented as:

$$
(u,i,t)
$$

where:

- $u$ represents the source node, typically a user.
- $i$ represents the destination node, typically an item.
- $t$ represents the timestamp of the interaction.

This representation allows the model to preserve the temporal ordering of interactions without requiring fixed time intervals.

### Event-Driven Representation Updates

Event-based models attempt to update the representations of the nodes involved whenever new interaction events occur.

For example, when a user interacts with an item, the event may influence both the user's embedding and the item's embedding.

Some temporal graph models additionally incorporate explicit **interaction-order information** into edge representations.

This information may include:

- The position of an item within a user's interaction sequence.
- The position of a user within an item's interaction sequence.

These order-based attributes provide additional information about the relative timing and progression of interactions.

### Advantages

**Fine-grained temporal modeling:** Event-based approaches can represent individual interactions and their exact timestamps.

**Sensitivity to recent changes:** The model can incorporate new information as interaction events occur.

**Suitability for sequential recommendation:** Since event order is preserved, these approaches naturally support tasks in which the chronological sequence of interactions is important.

### Limitations

Event-based temporal graph models are generally more complex than snapshot-based approaches.

They may require sophisticated mechanisms for:

- Maintaining evolving node representations.
- Processing temporal dependencies.
- Sampling relevant historical interactions.
- Updating representations efficiently.

Consequently, their computational and implementation costs can be relatively high.

### Dynamic and Temporal Graph Neural Networks

**Dynamic Graph Neural Networks (DGNNs)** and **Temporal Graph Neural Networks (TGNs)** are designed to combine structural, temporal, and contextual information from evolving graphs.

These models extend conventional graph learning mechanisms to account for changing relationships and time-dependent interactions.

In recommendation systems, they provide a framework for learning representations that reflect both collaborative relationships and temporal user behavior.

The following section examines a representative approach developed specifically for sequential recommendation.

---

## Dynamic Graph Neural Networks for Sequential Recommendation (DGSR)

**Dynamic Graph Neural Networks for Sequential Recommendation (DGSR)** is a graph-based recommendation model designed to address limitations of conventional sequential recommendation methods.

Many sequential recommendation algorithms primarily analyze each user's interaction history independently.

Although these approaches can capture sequential patterns within individual users' histories, they may fail to explicitly exploit collaborative relationships across different users' interaction sequences.

DGSR addresses this limitation by modeling **dynamic collaborative signals** within a temporal user–item graph.

Its objective is to improve recommendation accuracy by combining sequential information with higher-order collaborative relationships.

## Motivation Behind DGSR

A fundamental challenge in sequential recommendation is inferring a user's current preferences from their historical interaction sequence.

Traditional sequential models often emphasize dependencies between items within the same user's interaction history.

However, this approach may overlook two important sources of information.

### 1. Higher-Order Collaborative Relationships

Different users' interaction sequences may be connected through shared items and indirect graph paths.

These connections provide collaborative information that cannot always be recovered from an individual user's sequence alone.

For example, users who interact with related groups of items may exhibit similar preferences even if their interaction histories do not contain identical sequences.

### 2. Temporal Evolution of Collaborative Relationships

Collaborative relationships are not necessarily stable over time.

The relevance of relationships between users and items may change as new interactions occur.

Therefore, it is important to model not only higher-order connectivity but also how its influence evolves over time.

DGSR was developed to address two central questions:

1. How can user–item interactions be represented as a dynamic graph while preserving interaction-order information?
2. How can dynamic collaborative signals be explicitly encoded to improve predictions for each user's interaction sequence?

The model answers these questions through a graph construction strategy and a specialized dynamic graph recommendation architecture.

---

## Architecture and Operation of DGSR

DGSR consists of four main components:

1. Dynamic Graph Construction.
2. Subgraph Sampling.
3. Dynamic Graph Recommendation Networks (DGRN).
4. Prediction Layer.

## 1. Dynamic Graph Construction

The first step is to transform users' interaction sequences into a dynamic graph.

Whenever a user $u$ interacts with an item $i$ at time $t$, an interaction edge is added between the corresponding nodes.

Unlike a conventional static interaction graph, this edge contains additional temporal and ordering information.

Specifically, the edge records:

- The timestamp of the interaction.
- The position of the item in the user's interaction sequence.
- The position of the user in the item's interaction sequence.

A temporal interaction edge can therefore be represented as:

$$
(u,i,t,o_{ui},o_{iu})
$$

where:

| Symbol | Description |
|---|---|
| $u$ | User involved in the interaction. |
| $i$ | Item involved in the interaction. |
| $t$ | Timestamp of the interaction. |
| $o_{ui}$ | Position of item $i$ in user $u$'s interaction sequence. |
| $o_{iu}$ | Position of user $u$ in item $i$'s interaction sequence. |

The resulting dynamic graph contains the user–item interactions observed up to the current time.

### Example

Suppose user $u_{1}$ interacts with three items in the following order:

$$
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
$$

The corresponding interaction timestamps are:

$$
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})
$$

The first two interactions may be represented as:

$$
(u_{1},i_{1},t_{1},1,1)
$$

$$
(u_{1},i_{2},t_{2},2,1)
$$

In the first interaction:

- Item $i_{1}$ occupies the first position in user $u_{1}$'s interaction sequence.
- User $u_{1}$ occupies the first position in item $i_{1}$'s interaction sequence.

In the second interaction:

- Item $i_{2}$ occupies the second position in user $u_{1}$'s interaction sequence.
- User $u_{1}$ occupies the first position in item $i_{2}$'s interaction sequence.

The fourth and fifth elements of each interaction tuple therefore encode the two corresponding order positions.

This representation preserves information about the chronological progression of interactions from both user and item perspectives.

## 2. Subgraph Sampling

As interactions accumulate, the dynamic graph becomes increasingly large.

Processing the entire graph for every recommendation request can introduce substantial computational costs.

Moreover, incorporating all available graph information may introduce irrelevant or noisy relationships into the representation-learning process.

To address these issues, DGSR employs a **subgraph sampling strategy**.

### Dynamic Subgraph Extraction

For each target user sequence, DGSR dynamically extracts a subgraph containing:

- The target user's interaction sequence.
- Related user and item nodes.
- Relevant multi-hop neighbors.
- Collaborative relationships associated with the target sequence.

The sampled subgraph provides a localized representation of the dynamic interaction graph.

Rather than processing every node and edge in the full graph, the model focuses on structural and temporal information relevant to the current prediction task.

### Purpose of Subgraph Sampling

Subgraph sampling serves two primary purposes.

**Computational efficiency:** Reducing the number of processed nodes and edges makes graph representation learning more manageable.

**Contextual relevance:** Selecting related interactions helps the model focus on collaborative information that is useful for understanding the target user's preferences.

Through this mechanism, DGSR obtains dynamic contextual information without requiring full-graph computation for every target sequence.

---

## 3. Dynamic Graph Recommendation Networks (DGRN)

The **Dynamic Graph Recommendation Network (DGRN)** is the central representation-learning component of DGSR.

Its purpose is to encode user preferences using the dynamic contextual information contained in sampled subgraphs.

DGRN employs two fundamental graph neural network operations:

- **Message Propagation:** Information is transmitted between connected nodes.
- **Node Updating:** Node representations are updated using the information obtained through propagation.

An important characteristic of DGRN is its explicit modeling of both long-term and short-term preference information.

### 3.1. Encoding Long-Term Information

Long-term information represents relatively persistent user preferences inferred from historical interactions and collaborative relationships.

DGRN uses a **Dynamic Graph Attention (DAT)** mechanism to capture this information.

DAT combines graph-based attention with relative interaction-order embeddings.

This allows the model to assign different importance weights to neighboring nodes according to their relevance and interaction order.

For example:

- Different items may contribute unequally to a user's representation.
- Different users may contribute unequally to an item's representation.
- The relative order of interactions may influence the importance assigned to each neighbor.

### Long-Term Information Propagation

For a user $u$, the long-term representation can be expressed as:

$$
h_{u}^{L}=\sum_{i\in N_{u}}\alpha_{ui}\left(W_{1}^{(l-1)}h_{i}^{(l-1)}+p_{ru'}^{V}\right)
$$

where:

| Symbol | Description |
|---|---|
| $h_{u}^{L}$ | Long-term representation of user $u$. |
| $N_{u}$ | Set of neighboring items associated with user $u$. |
| $\alpha_{ui}$ | Attention coefficient between user $u$ and item $i$. |
| $W_{1}^{(l-1)}$ | Learnable transformation matrix associated with the preceding layer. |
| $h_{i}^{(l-1)}$ | Embedding of item $i$ at layer $l-1$. |
| $p_{ru'}^{V}$ | Relative-order embedding incorporated into the attention-based message. |

The attention coefficient $\alpha_{ui}$ determines the contribution of each neighboring item to the user's long-term representation.

The relative-order embedding provides additional information about the temporal ordering of interactions.

Consequently, the resulting representation incorporates both graph connectivity and interaction-order information.

### 3.2. Encoding Short-Term Information

Long-term preferences alone may not adequately represent a user's immediate interests.

For example, a user may generally prefer action movies but temporarily search for comedy films.

To capture such changes, DGRN introduces a separate attention mechanism for **short-term preference modeling**.

This mechanism focuses on the relationship between the user's most recent interaction and previous interactions.

The objective is to identify historical items that are particularly relevant to the user's current interests.

### Short-Term Information Aggregation

For a user $u$, the short-term representation is computed as:

$$
h_{u}^{S}=\sum_{i\in N_{u}}\hat{\alpha}_{ui}h_{i}^{(l-1)}
$$

where:

- $h_{u}^{S}$ represents the user's short-term preference embedding.
- $\hat{\alpha}_{ui}$ is the attention coefficient associated with short-term information.
- $h_{i}^{(l-1)}$ is the embedding of neighboring item $i$ from the preceding layer.

The attention weights determine which historical interactions contribute most strongly to the short-term representation.

By emphasizing interactions related to the user's recent behavior, the model can capture temporary shifts in preferences.

### 3.3. Node Updating

After computing long-term and short-term representations, DGRN combines them with the node's embedding from the preceding layer.

For a user node, the update operation is:

$$
\mathbf{h}_{u}^{(l)}=\tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{3}^{(l)}\left[\mathbf{h}_{u}^{L}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{h}_{u}^{S}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{h}_{u}^{(l-1)}\right]\right)
$$

where:

- $\mathbf{h}_{u}^{(l)}$ is the updated user embedding at layer $l$.
- $\mathbf{h}_{u}^{L}$ is the long-term preference representation.
- $\mathbf{h}_{u}^{S}$ is the short-term preference representation.
- $\mathbf{h}_{u}^{(l-1)}$ is the user's embedding from the preceding layer.
- $\mathbf{W}_{3}^{(l)}$ is a learnable transformation matrix.
- $\mathbin{\|}$ denotes vector concatenation.
- $\tanh$ is the hyperbolic tangent activation function.

The concatenation operation combines the three sources of information into a single vector.

A learnable transformation then integrates these representations, while the nonlinear activation produces the updated node embedding.

A corresponding updating process is applied to item nodes.

Through repeated message propagation and node updating, DGRN learns representations that incorporate long-term preferences, short-term interests, and dynamic collaborative relationships.

---

## 4. Prediction Layer

The final objective of DGSR is to predict the next item in a user's interaction sequence.

DGSR formulates this task as a **link prediction problem** within the dynamic user–item graph.

In this formulation, predicting a future interaction corresponds to estimating the likelihood or relevance of a potential edge between a user node and a candidate item node.

### Multi-Layer User Representation

Before generating predictions, DGSR combines the user embeddings produced by different DGRN layers.

Specifically, embeddings from layer zero through layer $L$ are concatenated to construct a comprehensive final user representation.

This aggregation incorporates information captured at different graph propagation depths.

The resulting representation combines:

- Initial user characteristics.
- Local interaction information.
- Higher-order collaborative relationships.
- Long-term preference information.
- Short-term behavioral information.

### Link Prediction Function

The final user embedding is combined with each candidate item's embedding to compute a recommendation score.

The prediction function is:

$$
s_{ui}=h_{u}^{T}W_{P}e_{i}
$$

where:

| Symbol | Description |
|---|---|
| $s_{ui}$ | Predicted interaction score between user $u$ and item $i$. |
| $h_{u}$ | Final user embedding obtained from the DGRN layers. |
| $e_{i}$ | Embedding of candidate item $i$. |
| $W_{P}$ | Learnable transformation matrix used in the prediction layer. |
| $h_{u}^{T}$ | Transpose of the final user embedding. |

The learnable matrix $W_{P}$ allows the model to transform the representations when computing user–item compatibility.

Candidate items can then be ranked according to their predicted scores.

Items receiving higher scores are considered more relevant candidates for the user's next interaction.

## Advantages of DGSR

### 1. Explicit Modeling of Dynamic Collaborative Signals

A major advantage of DGSR is its ability to explicitly model collaborative relationships across different users' interaction sequences.

Unlike sequential recommendation methods that focus primarily on individual user histories, DGSR incorporates hidden higher-order connectivity patterns.

These relationships provide additional information about user preferences and item relevance.

### 2. Incorporating Temporal and Order Information

DGSR considers not only whether interactions occurred but also their temporal order.

Interaction timestamps and sequence positions are incorporated into the dynamic graph representation.

Relative-order embeddings further allow the attention mechanisms to account for the ordering of interactions.

This enables the model to capture temporal dependencies that may be overlooked by static graph-based recommendation approaches.

### 3. Improved Recommendation Performance

Experimental evaluations on public datasets have shown that DGSR can outperform several state-of-the-art sequential recommendation methods.

Its ability to exploit higher-order dynamic graph connectivity is particularly valuable in sparse datasets.

When individual users have limited interaction histories, collaborative information from related users and items can help enrich their representations.

Consequently, DGSR can mitigate some of the difficulties associated with sparse interaction data.

### 4. Architectural Flexibility

The DGSR framework is relatively flexible.

Its message propagation mechanisms can be modified or replaced to accommodate different graph representation-learning strategies.

This flexibility makes the framework adaptable to a variety of sequential recommendation architectures.

### 5. Efficiency in Large-Scale Recommendation Systems

DGSR uses subgraph sampling to restrict computation to relevant portions of the dynamic interaction graph.

This reduces the need to process the entire graph for every target sequence.

By combining selective subgraph extraction with graph-based message propagation, the model can utilize rich interaction information more efficiently.

Although computational costs remain an important consideration, these mechanisms support the application of dynamic graph learning to larger recommendation environments.

DGSR represents an important development in sequential recommendation by integrating temporal interaction modeling with higher-order collaborative information.

Its dynamic graph construction, order-aware attention mechanisms, and specialized graph representation-learning architecture demonstrate how temporal and collaborative signals can be combined to improve the prediction of future user interactions.
