## 4.1. Graph Representation Learning: Graph Embeddings

As discussed in the previous section, graph-structured data presents unique structural challenges that conventional machine learning models are not inherently designed to address.

To overcome these challenges and exploit the capabilities of machine learning algorithms, we need methods that transform complex graph structures into representations that these algorithms can process efficiently.

This motivates **Graph Representation Learning**, particularly **Graph Embedding** methods.

Graph representation learning aims to extract meaningful information from graph structures and encode it into numerical representations while preserving important relationships among entities.

In this section, we examine the fundamental principles of graph embeddings, early methods for learning them, and their applications in recommender systems.

### 4.1.1. Objectives of Graph Embeddings

The primary objective of graph embedding methods is to map nodes, edges, or entire graphs into numerical vectors, typically of relatively low dimensionality, within a **continuous vector space**.

These numerical representations are called **Embeddings**.

For a node $v$, an embedding can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{z}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{z}_{v}$ is the embedding vector of node $v$.
- $d$ is the dimensionality of the embedding space.
- $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denotes a $d$-dimensional real-valued vector space.

The purpose of an embedding is not merely to convert graph entities into numerical vectors. A useful embedding must preserve meaningful information about the original graph.

Three important objectives are:

1. **Proximity Preservation**
2. **Feature Preservation**
3. **Dimensionality Reduction**

#### Proximity Preservation

Nodes that are closely related in the original graph should ideally have similar representations in the embedding space.

The concept of proximity can be defined in several ways, including:

- Direct adjacency between nodes.
- The number of shared neighbors.
- Short paths connecting nodes.
- Similarity in structural roles or connectivity patterns.

For example, two nodes may be considered similar because they are directly connected or because they share many neighbors.

Depending on the embedding method, such relationships should be reflected in the learned vectors.

Similarity between embeddings can be measured using metrics such as Euclidean distance or cosine similarity.

For two embedding vectors $\mathbf{z}_{u}$ and $\mathbf{z}_{v}$, cosine similarity is defined as:

$$
Sim(\mathbf{z}_{u},\mathbf{z}_{v})=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{u}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{v}}{\lVert\mathbf{z}_{u}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{z}_{v}\rVert}
$$

A higher cosine similarity indicates greater directional similarity between the two vectors.

Alternatively, smaller Euclidean distances indicate greater proximity in the embedding space.

Importantly, different graph embedding methods may preserve different notions of proximity. Defining which relationships should be retained is therefore a fundamental modeling decision.

#### Feature Preservation

Graph nodes may contain intrinsic attributes in addition to their structural relationships.

Examples include:

- A user's age.
- A movie's genre.
- A movie's director.
- Features extracted by another machine learning model.

**Feature Preservation** aims to incorporate relevant node attributes into the learned representations.

For example, if two movies belong to the same genre, their embeddings may reflect that similarity even when their interaction histories are limited.

Likewise, a user's demographic or behavioral attributes may contribute to their learned representation.

The objective is to encode useful attribute information alongside relational information whenever such attributes are available and relevant.

#### Dimensionality Reduction

Graphs may contain large amounts of information distributed across numerous nodes and edges.

Directly representing every relationship can require high-dimensional or sparse data structures.

Graph embeddings aim to compress relevant information into substantially smaller dense vectors.

For example, an embedding may contain only 64 or 128 numerical values while capturing important structural and behavioral patterns.

This reduction can improve the efficiency of downstream machine learning algorithms.

However, dimensionality reduction necessarily involves a trade-off: the objective is to preserve the information most relevant to the intended task rather than reproduce every detail of the original graph.

### 4.1.2. Example: Embedding Users and Movies

Consider a graph representing interactions between users and movies.

The graph contains two types of nodes:

- User nodes.
- Movie nodes.

An edge connects a user to a movie when the user has watched or rated that movie.

The objective is to construct an embedding vector for each user and each movie.

These embeddings should summarize meaningful information about the corresponding entities.

#### Preserving Proximity

Suppose two science-fiction movies are frequently watched by the same users.

Although the movies are distinct entities, their interaction patterns indicate a meaningful relationship.

Their embeddings should therefore be relatively close in the learned vector space if the embedding method is designed to preserve this type of similarity.

Similarly, two users who consistently watch similar movies may receive similar user embeddings.

This process can be understood through an analogy with geographical maps.

Just as nearby cities appear close together on a map, closely related entities in an interaction graph should appear close together in the embedding space.

The difference is that graph embeddings represent relational similarity rather than physical distance.

#### Preserving Features

Suppose a movie has attributes such as:

- Science-fiction genre.
- Director X.

A user may also have attributes such as:

- Age: 30 years.

A feature-aware graph embedding method should incorporate relevant information from these attributes into the resulting vectors.

For example, a movie embedding may contain enough information for a downstream classifier to predict its likely genre, even without directly accessing the original genre label.

This illustrates how embeddings can encode meaningful semantic information about graph entities.

#### Reducing Dimensionality

A movie may have been watched by thousands of users, while an individual user may have interacted with hundreds of movies.

Representing all these interactions explicitly produces a large and potentially sparse dataset.

Graph embedding methods attempt to summarize this information in compact numerical vectors.

For example, a user or movie might be represented by a vector containing 64 or 128 values.

These vectors serve as compact representations of user preferences and movie characteristics.

Consequently, tasks such as comparing users, identifying similar movies, and generating recommendations can be performed more efficiently using the learned representations.

## 4.2. Why Do We Need Graph Embeddings?

Many established machine learning algorithms operate on numerical feature vectors.

Examples include:

- Support Vector Machines (SVMs).
- Logistic Regression.
- Conventional neural network layers.

Graph embeddings provide a mechanism for converting relational graph information into vector representations compatible with these algorithms.

Once embeddings have been learned, they can be used in several downstream tasks.

### 4.2.1. Link Prediction

**Link Prediction** aims to determine whether an edge exists or is likely to emerge between two nodes.

In recommender systems, this corresponds naturally to predicting potential user–item interactions.

For example:

Will user $u$ become interested in product $v$?

Graph embeddings can help identify latent relationships between users and items that may not be apparent from their directly observed interactions.

This capability is particularly valuable when addressing:

- **Data Sparsity:** Many user–item interactions are unobserved.
- **Cold-Start Problems:** New users or items have limited interaction histories.

By exploiting graph structure and available side information, embedding-based methods may infer relationships that conventional approaches struggle to identify.

Their effectiveness in cold-start settings depends on the information available for newly introduced users or items.

### 4.2.2. Node Clustering

**Node Clustering** groups nodes according to similarities in their learned representations.

For example, users with similar preferences may be assigned to the same cluster.

These clusters can support recommendation strategies that exploit shared interests among groups of users.

### 4.2.3. Node Classification

**Node Classification** assigns labels or categories to graph nodes.

For example, a model may use user embeddings to predict a user's preferred movie genre.

The embeddings provide numerical features that a classification algorithm can use to identify meaningful patterns.

### 4.2.4. Item Recommendation

Graph embeddings can be used directly to recommend new items to users.

For example, a recommendation model may compare user embeddings with item embeddings to estimate their compatibility.

Items with higher predicted compatibility can then be selected for recommendation.

### 4.2.5. Scalability and Reusability

An additional advantage of graph embeddings is that learned representations can be reused across multiple recommendation operations.

Rather than repeatedly analyzing the entire graph structure for every recommendation request, a system can use previously computed embeddings.

This can improve:

- Computational efficiency.
- Scalability.
- Model extensibility.
- Reusability of learned representations.

However, embeddings may need to be updated periodically as new users, items, and interactions are introduced.

## 4.3. Random Walk-Based Graph Embedding Methods

One of the most influential families of graph embedding methods is based on **Random Walks**.

These methods exploit the idea that nodes appearing near one another in random walks through a graph should have related embeddings.

A random walk is a sequence of nodes generated by repeatedly moving from the current node to one of its neighbors according to a transition probability.

Random walk-based embedding methods generally consist of two major steps.

### 4.3.1. Generating Node Sequences

The first step is to generate sequences of nodes by traversing the graph.

A typical procedure is:

1. Select a starting node.
2. Randomly choose one of its neighboring nodes.
3. Move to the selected neighbor.
4. Repeat the process to construct a sequence of nodes.

This procedure is repeated multiple times for nodes throughout the graph.

The resulting sequences can be interpreted as sentences, where each node corresponds to a word.

For example, a random walk might produce:

$$
v_{1}\rightarrow v_{4}\rightarrow v_{7}\rightarrow v_{3}\rightarrow v_{9}
$$

By generating many such sequences, the method collects information about the structural relationships among nodes.

### 4.3.2. Learning Embeddings Using Language Modeling Techniques

The generated node sequences are then processed using techniques inspired by natural language processing.

A prominent example is **Word2Vec**, which learns word embeddings based on patterns of co-occurrence within textual sequences.

In graph embedding applications:

- Words correspond to nodes.
- Sentences correspond to random walks.
- Word co-occurrence corresponds to node co-occurrence within random walks.

The learning algorithm attempts to construct embeddings that capture these co-occurrence relationships.

Nodes that frequently appear within similar contexts during random walks tend to develop related vector representations.

This allows graph structure to be transformed into numerical embeddings using techniques originally developed for language representation learning.

## 4.4. Node2Vec

**Node2Vec** is a widely used graph embedding algorithm based on random walks.

Its distinguishing feature is a flexible random walk strategy that can capture different types of structural relationships.

Node2Vec introduces two parameters:

1. **Return Parameter ($p$)**
2. **In-Out Parameter ($q$)**

These parameters control the random walk's tendency to revisit previously encountered regions or explore more distant parts of the graph.

The resulting walks can exhibit behavior resembling:

- **Breadth-First Search (BFS):** Emphasizing local neighborhoods.
- **Depth-First Search (DFS):** Exploring more distant graph regions.

This flexibility enables Node2Vec to capture both local proximity and broader structural patterns.

### 4.4.1. Return Parameter

The return parameter $p$ controls the likelihood of returning to the previously visited node.

A smaller value of $p$ increases the relative probability of immediately returning to the previous node.

This can cause the random walk to revisit local regions more frequently.

Conversely, a larger value of $p$ discourages immediate backtracking.

By controlling backtracking behavior, this parameter influences how the algorithm explores the graph.

### 4.4.2. In-Out Parameter

The in-out parameter $q$ controls the preference for exploring nodes that are closer to or farther from the previously visited node.

Its behavior can be summarized as follows.

**When $q>1$:**

The random walk favors nodes that remain relatively close to the previous node.

This encourages local neighborhood exploration and behavior resembling BFS.

Such walks are useful for capturing local proximity and community structure.

**When $q<1$:**

The random walk favors nodes that are farther from the previous node.

This encourages exploration of more distant graph regions and behavior resembling DFS.

Such walks can help capture broader structural patterns and similarities in the roles occupied by nodes.

### 4.4.3. Balancing Local and Global Structure

By adjusting $p$ and $q$, Node2Vec can generate embeddings that emphasize different aspects of graph structure.

For example:

- Local proximity may help identify users with similar interaction histories.
- Broader structural patterns may help identify users occupying similar roles within a network.

This adaptability makes Node2Vec useful for recommendation tasks in which both local behavioral similarities and global structural relationships may be informative.

## 4.5. N2VSCDNNR: A Local Recommender System Based on Node2Vec and Rich Information Networks

N2VSCDNNR is a clustering-based recommendation framework that combines **Node2Vec**, rich information networks, and an advanced clustering method.

The framework is designed to address challenges associated with data sparsity and recommendation efficiency.

These challenges become particularly important as the numbers of users, items, and interactions increase.

N2VSCDNNR illustrates how graph-based representation learning can be integrated with clustering and conventional recommendation algorithms to construct a more comprehensive recommendation framework.

### 4.5.1. Main Objectives

The principal objectives of N2VSCDNNR are:

- Reducing the effects of sparse interaction data.
- Improving recommendation accuracy.
- Increasing computational efficiency.
- Exploiting additional information about users, items, and item categories.

The framework consists of four main stages.

### 4.5.2. Stage 1: Constructing Rich Information Networks and One-Mode Projections

The first stage constructs several networks representing relationships among users, items, and categories.

Initially, two fundamental **Bipartite Networks** are created.

**User–Item Network**

This network represents interactions between users and items.

One set of nodes represents users, while the other represents items.

Edges indicate observed interactions.

**Item–Category Network**

This network represents relationships between items and their categories.

One set of nodes represents items, while the other represents categories.

An edge indicates that an item belongs to a particular category.

#### Constructing the User–Category Network

The user–item and item–category networks are combined to construct a third bipartite network:

**User–Category Network**

In this network, the weight connecting a user to a category represents the total number of times the user has examined items belonging to that category.

This provides additional information about the user's category-level interests.

#### One-Mode Projection

To enrich the network structure and reduce the effects of data sparsity, the bipartite networks are transformed into **One-Mode Projection Networks**.

Two important projection networks are constructed:

1. **User–User Projection Network**
2. **Item–Item Projection Network**

A one-mode projection transforms relationships between two different node types into relationships among nodes of the same type.

For example, two users may be connected in the user–user projection if they have interacted with common items or exhibit related category preferences.

Similarly, two items may be connected in the item–item projection based on shared users or category-related information.

These projections incorporate information such as:

- The number of common neighbors.
- Shared interaction patterns.
- Item category information.

By combining these signals, the framework creates stronger relationships among similar users and similar items.

### 4.5.3. Stage 2: Generating Embeddings with Node2Vec

After constructing the user–user and item–item projection networks, Node2Vec is applied to both networks.

The algorithm generates embeddings through its flexible neighborhood sampling strategy.

These embeddings can encode:

- Local structural information.
- Global structural information.
- Hidden relationships among users.
- Hidden relationships among items.

The enriched projection networks provide additional relational information that may not be available from the original sparse interaction data alone.

Consequently, Node2Vec can learn representations that better reflect similarities among users and items.

This helps reduce the negative effects of data sparsity on subsequent recommendation stages.

### 4.5.4. Stage 3: Clustering Users and Items with SCDNN

After generating embeddings, the next stage groups users and items into clusters.

N2VSCDNNR employs an advanced spectral clustering method called **SCDNN**.

This method incorporates:

- **Dynamic Nearest Neighbors**
- **Automatic Determination of Cluster Number (ADCN)**

The clustering method automatically determines cluster centers based on a normal distribution.

It is designed to handle **Multi-Scale Datasets**, in which different regions of the data may exhibit different densities.

Clustering is performed separately for:

1. User embeddings.
2. Item embeddings.

The resulting user clusters represent groups of users with related characteristics or preferences.

Similarly, item clusters represent groups of items with related characteristics.

These clusters provide the foundation for the subsequent personalized recommendation process.

### 4.5.5. Stage 4: Two-Stage Personalized Recommendation

After clustering users and items, N2VSCDNNR performs recommendation through a two-stage procedure.

#### First Stage: Recommending Item Clusters to User Clusters

The first stage recommends relevant item clusters to user clusters.

This process considers the number of user–item relationships between each item cluster and the target user cluster.

These relationships provide a measure of the association between the two clusters.

Using K-means, the item clusters are divided into two groups.

The group with the higher average weight is selected for recommendation to the target user cluster.

This stage identifies promising groups of items before individual recommendations are generated.

#### Second Stage: Recommending Individual Items

After selecting relevant item clusters, the framework applies more conventional recommendation algorithms.

These algorithms recommend specific items from the selected item clusters to individual users within the corresponding user clusters.

Thus, the overall recommendation process proceeds from coarse-grained cluster selection to more detailed item recommendation.

This hierarchical strategy reduces the number of candidate items that must be considered during personalized recommendation.

### 4.5.6. Overall Architecture of N2VSCDNNR

The complete framework can be summarized as follows:

1. **Network Construction:** Build user–item, item–category, and user–category networks, followed by user–user and item–item one-mode projections.
2. **Graph Embedding:** Apply Node2Vec to the projection networks to learn user and item representations.
3. **Clustering:** Use SCDNN to cluster users and items based on their embeddings.
4. **Personalized Recommendation:** Recommend item clusters to user clusters and subsequently recommend individual items.

By integrating graph embeddings, auxiliary information, and advanced clustering, N2VSCDNNR provides a framework for generating more accurate and computationally efficient recommendations.

The model also demonstrates how graph-based learning can be combined with established recommendation algorithms rather than replacing them entirely.

Graph-based machine learning remains an active research area with numerous approaches to representation learning and recommendation.

The methods discussed in this section illustrate several influential earlier techniques. Subsequent sections examine more recent approaches to graph learning and their applications in recommender systems.
