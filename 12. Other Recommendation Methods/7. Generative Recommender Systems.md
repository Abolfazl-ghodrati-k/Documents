In the chapter on Deep Learning and Recommender Systems, we examined several deep learning approaches to recommendation, including generative methods such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and autoregressive models.

Other generative approaches, including diffusion models, have also been developed for recommender systems. However, a detailed examination of these architectures is beyond the scope of this course.

Rather than focusing on the internal mechanisms of individual generative models, this section examines the fundamental concepts, general operating principles, opportunities, and challenges associated with **Generative Recommender Systems (Gen-RecSys)**.

## 5.1. Generative Recommender Systems (Gen-RecSys): A New Paradigm for Recommendation

Generative models have introduced a new and potentially transformative paradigm in recommender systems, extending the capabilities of conventional recommendation approaches.

**Generative Recommender Systems (Gen-RecSys)** are recommender systems that employ generative models to perform recommendation-related tasks.

Unlike systems that primarily estimate the relevance of existing items, generative recommender systems can model complex data distributions and use these learned distributions to produce recommendation-related outputs.

Depending on the underlying architecture and application, these outputs may include individual recommendations, structured recommendation lists, natural-language explanations, personalized content, or complete recommendation interfaces.

### 5.1.1. Differences Between Generative and Non-Generative Approaches

Traditional recommender systems generally focus on modeling user–item interactions and predicting user preferences based on historical data.

Many conventional recommendation models follow a **Discriminative Modeling** paradigm.

Discriminative models learn mappings from input information to specific prediction targets.

For example, a discriminative recommendation model may estimate:

- The rating a user would assign to an item.
- The probability that a user will click a recommended product.
- The likelihood that a user will purchase an item.
- A relevance score used to rank candidate items.

These models primarily aim to predict particular outcomes from observed features and interaction histories.

Generative recommender systems extend this approach by learning to model complex data distributions and generate samples or outputs from them.

This enables generative models to perform tasks beyond conventional preference prediction.

For example, a generative recommender system may produce:

- **Text:** Personalized product descriptions or recommendation explanations.
- **Images:** Visual content adapted to a user's preferences.
- **Videos:** Personalized or recommendation-related video content.
- **Interactions:** Plausible user–item interaction patterns.
- **Structured Outputs:** Recommendation lists, item collections, or complete recommendation pages.

The essential distinction is that generative approaches can learn how to construct outputs based on modeled data distributions rather than being limited to assigning scores to predefined candidates.

However, generative and discriminative approaches are not mutually exclusive. A practical recommender system may combine generative components with conventional retrieval, scoring, and ranking models.

## 5.2. Opportunities Created by Generative Recommender Systems

The integration of generative models into recommender systems introduces several important opportunities.

These include enhanced personalization, natural-language interaction, explainability, content generation, improved handling of sparse data, and integration with external knowledge.

### 5.2.1. Enhanced Personalization

One of the most promising capabilities of generative recommender systems is their potential to produce highly personalized recommendations and content.

Generative models can learn complex relationships among user preferences, historical interactions, contextual information, and item characteristics.

Their representation-learning and generalization capabilities may enable them to produce outputs that more closely reflect users' evolving interests.

For example, instead of merely selecting products from a fixed catalog based on historical interactions, a generative model may produce personalized descriptions or recommendation presentations adapted to the user's current preferences.

This is particularly valuable when user interests change over time.

By incorporating recent interactions and contextual signals, generative recommendation systems can potentially respond more effectively to dynamic user preferences.

### 5.2.2. Improved Conversational Interfaces

**Conversational Recommendation** allows users to interact with recommendation systems through natural-language dialogue.

Large Language Models (LLMs) have significantly expanded the possibilities of this approach.

An LLM-based conversational recommender can interpret user requests, maintain conversational context, and generate natural-language responses.

For example, a user might request:

> Recommend a science-fiction movie with a complex storyline, but avoid anything excessively long.

A conversational recommender can interpret the different constraints expressed in the request and generate an appropriate response.

Users may also refine their preferences through subsequent interactions.

For example, they may ask for recommendations that are less expensive, more recent, or more similar to a previously recommended item.

This creates a more flexible interaction mechanism than traditional recommendation interfaces that rely exclusively on predefined filters or implicit behavioral signals.

The primary advantage is the ability to express and refine preferences through natural language.

### 5.2.3. Richer Recommendation Explanations

Generative recommender systems can produce natural-language explanations describing why particular items are recommended.

Traditional recommendation systems may provide relatively simple explanations based on predefined templates or similarity relationships.

Generative models can potentially construct more detailed and context-sensitive explanations.

For example, a system might explain that a particular movie was recommended because its narrative structure, genre, and themes resemble those of movies the user previously enjoyed.

Such explanations can improve:

- **Transparency:** Helping users understand recommendation decisions.
- **Trust:** Increasing confidence in the recommendation process.
- **Persuasiveness:** Making recommendations more understandable and convincing.

However, a generated explanation is not necessarily a faithful description of the model's actual decision process.

Therefore, explanation quality should be evaluated not only in terms of linguistic fluency but also in terms of factual accuracy and consistency with the recommendation evidence.

### 5.2.4. Personalized Content Generation

Generative recommender systems can support applications in which the recommended content itself is generated or transformed.

This extends recommendation beyond selecting existing items.

Two important examples are virtual try-on and personalized visual content generation.

#### Virtual Try-On

**Virtual Try-On** enables users to visualize how a product might appear on themselves or within their environment.

For example, a user may want to see:

- How a particular outfit would look when worn.
- How a piece of furniture would appear in a room.
- How an accessory would complement an existing appearance.

Generative models can create visual representations that approximate these scenarios.

This capability can help users evaluate products before purchasing them.

#### Personalized Content Creation

Generative models can also create content adapted to particular user preferences or contexts.

For example, an e-commerce platform may generate an image showing a product in a scenario relevant to a specific user.

Instead of displaying the same product image to every customer, the system may generate contextualized visual content.

This creates opportunities for more personalized product presentation and recommendation experiences.

### 5.2.5. Addressing Data-Related Challenges

Data sparsity and cold-start problems remain fundamental challenges in recommender systems.

**Data Sparsity** occurs when only a small proportion of possible user–item interactions have been observed.

The **Cold-Start Problem** arises when a new user or item has insufficient historical interaction data to support reliable recommendations.

Generative models may help address these problems through two mechanisms.

#### Synthetic Data Generation

Generative models can produce synthetic interaction data that resembles patterns learned from existing observations.

Such data may be used to supplement limited training information.

For example, synthetic user–item interactions may provide additional training signals when observed interactions are sparse.

However, synthetic interactions must be used carefully because unrealistic or biased generated data can degrade recommendation performance.

#### Leveraging Pre-Trained Knowledge

Generative models, particularly large pre-trained models, may contain useful representations learned from extensive external datasets.

This knowledge can help recommender systems interpret users, items, or contexts even when domain-specific interaction data is limited.

For example, a pre-trained language model may extract meaningful information from a newly introduced product's textual description.

This can support recommendation before the product has accumulated a substantial interaction history.

Thus, pre-trained knowledge may help reduce dependence on extensive domain-specific interaction data.

### 5.2.6. Integration with External Knowledge

Generative recommender systems can incorporate external information through **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**.

RAG combines information retrieval with generative modeling.

Rather than relying exclusively on knowledge stored in the generative model's parameters, a RAG-based system retrieves relevant information from an external source and uses it to generate a response.

In recommendation applications, retrieved information may include:

- Product descriptions.
- Item attributes.
- User preference information.
- Knowledge-base entries.
- Other relevant contextual information.

For example, a conversational shopping assistant may retrieve current product specifications before generating personalized recommendations.

This approach can improve the relevance and factual grounding of generated responses.

RAG is particularly useful when recommendations depend on information that changes frequently or is not reliably represented in the generative model's training data.

## 5.3. Challenges of Generative Recommender Systems

Despite their capabilities, generative recommender systems introduce several challenges that are less prominent or take different forms in conventional recommendation approaches.

These challenges involve evaluation, computational requirements, multimodal data, controllability, and potential unintended consequences.

### 5.3.1. Evaluation

Evaluating generative recommender systems is one of the most significant challenges in this field.

Traditional recommendation metrics are designed primarily to evaluate tasks such as relevance prediction and item ranking.

However, generative systems may produce complex outputs that cannot be adequately assessed using these metrics alone.

#### Limitations of Traditional Evaluation Metrics

Conventional recommendation metrics include:

- **Recall@K**
- **NDCG@K (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain)**

These metrics are useful for evaluating whether relevant items appear among the top-ranked recommendations and how effectively they are positioned.

However, they do not fully capture the quality of generated text, images, explanations, or other content.

Consequently, evaluating generative recommendation tasks may require additional metrics.

Examples from Natural Language Processing include:

- **BLEU:** Measures overlap between generated text and reference text, emphasizing matching word sequences.
- **ROUGE:** Measures overlap between generated and reference text, commonly used in summarization evaluation.
- **Perplexity:** Measures how well a probabilistic language model predicts a sequence of tokens.

These metrics provide useful information about particular aspects of generated text.

Nevertheless, none of them independently provides a complete measure of recommendation quality, factual correctness, or user satisfaction.

Therefore, evaluation frameworks for generative recommender systems often require combinations of recommendation-specific and generation-specific measures.

#### Open-Ended Nature of Generative Tasks

Generative recommendation tasks frequently admit multiple valid outputs.

For example, a user requesting a recommendation explanation may receive several different explanations that are all linguistically appropriate and factually correct.

Similarly, a request for personalized content may have numerous acceptable visual or textual outputs.

This makes evaluation difficult because a single reference answer may not represent the complete set of valid responses.

Traditional evaluation methods that compare predictions against one expected output may therefore be insufficient.

#### Social Consequences and Potential Harm

Generative recommender systems must also be evaluated in terms of their broader effects.

Important considerations include:

- **Bias:** Systematic distortions in generated recommendations or content.
- **Fairness:** Unequal treatment of users, items, or demographic groups.
- **Privacy:** Risks associated with exposing or reconstructing sensitive information.
- **Manipulability:** The possibility that generated outputs or system behavior can be influenced in undesirable ways.
- **Environmental Impact:** Resource consumption associated with training and deploying large models.

Large Language Models introduce an additional concern because they may incorporate knowledge learned outside the recommendation domain.

This **Out-of-Domain Knowledge** can be beneficial, but it may also introduce new sources of bias.

For example, a model may reproduce stereotypes or associations learned from its pre-training data, even when those patterns are not supported by the recommendation platform's own interaction data.

Consequently, evaluating generative recommender systems requires considering both technical performance and potential social consequences.

### 5.3.2. Computational Burden

Generative models, particularly Large Language Models, often require substantial computational resources.

These requirements arise during both major stages of model operation.

**Training**

Training large generative models may require:

- Extensive datasets.
- Specialized hardware.
- Significant memory capacity.
- Large amounts of computation.

**Inference**

Generating recommendations or content may also require considerable computational resources.

Unlike models that produce a single relevance score through a relatively compact computation, some generative architectures produce outputs through repeated or sequential generation steps.

This can increase inference latency and serving costs.

High computational requirements also have environmental implications.

Large-scale training and inference consume energy, potentially increasing the environmental footprint of recommendation services.

Therefore, practical deployment must balance recommendation quality with computational efficiency, latency, infrastructure cost, and environmental impact.

### 5.3.3. Multimodal Data Collection and Alignment

Many generative recommendation applications involve multiple data modalities.

A **Modality** refers to a particular form of information, such as:

- Text.
- Images.
- Audio.
- Video.

Systems that combine several modalities are called **Multimodal Systems**.

For example, a virtual try-on application may need to process images of users, product images, and textual descriptions.

Such systems require datasets that capture meaningful relationships among different modalities.

#### Data Collection

Collecting multimodal datasets is generally more difficult than collecting unimodal datasets.

For example, a multimodal recommendation task may require aligned image–text–image triplets.

These could contain a source image, a textual instruction or description, and a corresponding target image.

Constructing such datasets requires more than collecting independent images and text documents.

The different components must correspond to one another meaningfully.

#### Cross-Modal Alignment

**Cross-Modal Alignment** refers to establishing meaningful correspondences between representations from different modalities.

For example, a model must understand which parts of a product description correspond to particular visual characteristics of the product.

Incorrect alignment may cause the system to generate content that is inconsistent with the available information.

This becomes particularly challenging when the system must combine several modalities simultaneously.

Consequently, multimodal data collection and representation alignment are major challenges in generative recommendation.

### 5.3.4. Controllability and Predictability

Generative models can produce flexible and diverse outputs, but this flexibility also creates difficulties in controlling their behavior.

**Controllability** refers to the ability to guide a generative model toward outputs that satisfy specified requirements or constraints.

In recommender systems, these constraints may concern:

- User preferences.
- Item availability.
- Recommendation relevance.
- Content requirements.
- Safety and reliability.

A generative recommender should produce outputs consistent with the available evidence and the intended recommendation task.

However, generative models may produce unexpected or undesirable results.

One particularly important problem is **Hallucination**.

Hallucination occurs when a generative model produces information that appears plausible but is unsupported, inaccurate, or fabricated.

For example, a conversational recommender might describe product features that do not exist or recommend an item using incorrect specifications.

Such errors can reduce user trust and undermine recommendation reliability.

Therefore, generative recommender systems require mechanisms for improving output control, reducing hallucinations, and limiting unintended consequences.

These considerations are especially important when generated outputs influence consequential user decisions.
