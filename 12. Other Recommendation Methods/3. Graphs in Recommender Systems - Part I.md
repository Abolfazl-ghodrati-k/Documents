## 3.1. The World of Graphs

In the real world, the identity and behavior of an entity are determined not only by its intrinsic characteristics but also by its relationships with other entities.

Consider a football team. Its performance depends not only on the individual abilities of its players, such as a striker's speed or a midfielder's passing accuracy, but also on the coordination among players, the coach's tactical decisions, team morale, and support from fans.

Similarly, a city is more than a collection of buildings and streets. Its structure and functionality are shaped by road networks, public transportation systems, and the flow of information, goods, and services between neighborhoods and neighboring cities.

In biological systems, the function of a protein is often influenced by its interactions with other proteins within a complex biological network.

The same principle applies to recommender systems.

A product, such as a movie or a song, cannot be fully characterized solely by its intrinsic attributes, such as genre, director, or duration. Its relationships with other products, the users who have interacted with it, and the preferences of users with similar interests also provide essential information for generating personalized recommendations.

These examples illustrate a fundamental principle:

**Understanding complex entities often requires analyzing their relationships and interactions rather than considering their individual attributes in isolation.**

To represent and analyze such relationships systematically, we need a mathematical structure capable of modeling both entities and the connections between them.

This structure is known as a **Graph**.

Graphs provide a general framework for representing networks and relational data. They allow complex systems to be expressed in a structured form that supports both visualization and mathematical analysis.

### 3.1.1. Fundamental Components of a Graph

A graph consists of two primary components:

1. **Nodes (Vertices)**
2. **Edges (Links)**

A graph is commonly represented mathematically as:

$$
G=(V,E)
$$

Where:

- $G$ denotes the graph.
- $V$ is the set of nodes or vertices.
- $E$ is the set of edges representing relationships between nodes.

#### Nodes

**Nodes** represent the entities whose relationships we want to model.

Depending on the application, a node may represent:

- A football player.
- A city.
- A building.
- A protein.
- A person in a social network.
- A user in a recommender system.
- A product, movie, or song.

For example, in a movie recommendation system, users and movies can both be represented as nodes.

#### Edges

**Edges** represent relationships or connections between nodes.

An edge indicates that two entities are related in some meaningful way.

Examples include:

- A teammate relationship between two football players.
- A road connecting two cities.
- A street connecting two buildings.
- A chemical interaction between two proteins.
- A user's rating of a movie.
- A similarity relationship between two movies.

In a recommender system, an edge may represent an observed interaction between a user and an item.

For example, if user $u$ has watched movie $i$, an edge can connect the corresponding user and movie nodes.

### 3.1.2. Why Are Graphs Important?

Graphs are among the most natural representations of relational information.

Many real-world systems can be understood as networks of interconnected entities.

Their importance arises from several fundamental capabilities.

#### Intuitive Representation of Complex Networks

Graphs provide a visual and conceptually intuitive representation of relationships.

Consider a social network in which individuals are represented as nodes and friendships are represented as edges.

The resulting graph makes it possible to visualize how individuals are connected, identify groups of closely related users, and examine the overall structure of the social network.

This representation is often more informative than analyzing each individual independently.

#### Representing Rich Relationship Information

Graphs can describe more than the existence or absence of a relationship.

They can also represent additional information about the relationship itself.

For example, edges may have weights, directions, and attributes.

These properties make graphs suitable for modeling complex real-world interactions.

### 3.1.3. Weighted Graphs

In a **Weighted Graph**, edges are associated with numerical values that describe the strength, intensity, cost, or another quantitative characteristic of a relationship.

For example:

- The strength of a friendship in a social network.
- The distance between two cities.
- The intensity of an interaction between a drug and a protein.
- The rating assigned by a user to a movie.

A weighted graph can be represented as:

$$
G=(V,E,w)
$$

Where:

- $V$ is the set of nodes.
- $E$ is the set of edges.
- $w$ is a function assigning numerical weights to edges.

For an edge connecting nodes $u$ and $v$, its weight may be written as:

$$
w(u,v)
$$

The interpretation of this value depends on the application.

For example, in a movie recommendation graph, an edge weight may represent the rating a user assigned to a movie.

In a transportation network, it may represent the distance or travel cost between two locations.

### 3.1.4. Directed Graphs

In some networks, relationships have a specific direction.

A **Directed Graph** represents such relationships using directed edges.

For example, on a social media platform, user $u$ may follow user $v$ without user $v$ following user $u$.

The relationship is therefore directional.

A directed edge from $u$ to $v$ is represented as:

$$
u\rightarrow v
$$

Other examples include:

- A person following another person.
- A disease affecting a particular organ.
- A webpage linking to another webpage.
- A user performing an action on an item.

In contrast, an **Undirected Graph** represents relationships without an inherent direction.

For example, a mutual friendship relationship may be modeled using an undirected edge.

The distinction between directed and undirected graphs is important because it determines how relationships are interpreted and how information can propagate through the network.

### 3.1.5. Node and Edge Attributes

In addition to graph connectivity, nodes and edges may contain descriptive attributes.

**Node Attributes**

Node attributes describe the properties of individual entities.

For example, a person node in a social network may contain:

- Age.
- Gender.
- City of residence.

Similarly, an item node in a recommender system may contain:

- Product category.
- Price.
- Brand.
- Textual description.
- Other metadata.

**Edge Attributes**

Edge attributes describe properties of relationships.

For example, an edge representing a medical diagnosis may contain:

- Diagnosis date.
- Disease severity.

In a recommender system, an interaction edge may contain:

- Interaction type.
- Rating value.
- Interaction timestamp.
- Other contextual information.

By combining node attributes, edge attributes, and graph connectivity, a model can represent both the characteristics of entities and the structure of their relationships.

### 3.1.6. Heterogeneous Graphs

Graphs may contain multiple types of nodes and edges.

Such graphs are commonly called **Heterogeneous Graphs**.

For example, a social network may contain different node types:

- Users.
- Groups.
- Posts.

It may also contain different relationship types:

- User follows user.
- User joins group.
- User creates post.
- User likes post.

Similarly, a biomedical graph may contain:

- Drugs.
- Diseases.
- Proteins.

Relationships may represent drug–protein interactions, disease–protein associations, or other biological connections.

In recommender systems, a heterogeneous graph may include users, items, categories, and other entities, together with the relationships among them.

This flexibility allows heterogeneous graphs to capture richer information than representations that consider only one entity type or one relationship type.

### 3.1.7. Graph-Based Network Analysis

Graphs support a wide range of analytical methods for discovering patterns within complex networks.

These methods can be used to:

- Identify hidden relationships.
- Detect important or influential nodes.
- Discover critical connections in a network.
- Predict potential future relationships.
- Group similar entities into clusters or communities.

For example, graph analysis may identify influential individuals in a social network or critical locations within a transportation system.

In recommender systems, similar techniques can help discover relationships among users and items that are not immediately apparent from their individual attributes.

A particularly important application is **Link Prediction**, which aims to estimate whether a relationship exists or may emerge between two nodes.

In recommendation, predicting a potential user–item interaction can be interpreted as a form of link prediction.

### 3.1.8. Flexibility Across Application Domains

One of the most important advantages of graphs is their flexibility.

The same general representation can be used to model:

- Large-scale social networks.
- Biological interaction networks.
- Transportation systems.
- Webpage connectivity.
- Knowledge networks.
- User–item interaction networks.

This flexibility provides a unified framework for studying relational structures across different domains.

Although the meaning of nodes and edges varies between applications, many graph-based analytical principles remain applicable.

## 3.2. Graphs in Recommender Systems: Beyond Traditional Models

Graphs provide a natural representation for the entities and relationships involved in recommender systems.

A recommendation graph may contain nodes representing:

- Users.
- Products.
- Movies.
- Songs.
- Item attributes.
- Other relevant entities.

Edges may represent relationships such as:

- A user rating a product.
- A user viewing an item.
- A user purchasing a product.
- Two items being similar.
- Two users sharing similar preferences.

This graph-based perspective provides a unified framework for representing different types of recommendation data.

However, graph-structured data also introduces challenges that are not adequately addressed by many conventional machine learning architectures.

The need for specialized graph learning methods arises from three fundamental characteristics of graphs:

1. Variable graph structure and size.
2. Permutation invariance.
3. Complex and indirect relational dependencies.

### 3.2.1. Variable Graph Structure and Size

Graphs can vary substantially in their number of nodes and edges.

For example:

- One graph may contain only a few dozen nodes.
- Another may contain millions or billions of nodes.

Furthermore, different nodes within the same graph may have very different numbers of neighbors.

A node may be connected to only a few other nodes, while another may have hundreds or thousands of connections.

This property creates a challenge for conventional machine learning models.

Many traditional neural architectures are designed to process inputs with predefined dimensions or structures.

For example, a neural network designed for image processing may require images to be resized to a particular resolution.

This ensures that the input conforms to the expected structure.

Graphs, however, do not naturally have a universal fixed size.

Similarly, the number of neighbors associated with each node is not constant.

As a result, directly applying conventional fixed-structure neural networks to graph data can be difficult.

Graph learning models must be able to process structures with varying numbers of nodes, edges, and neighborhood connections.

### 3.2.2. Permutation Invariance

A second important characteristic of graphs is that their nodes do not have an inherent ordering.

The order in which nodes are stored in memory should not change the meaning of the graph.

Similarly, the order in which a node's neighbors are listed should not affect the representation learned for that node.

This requirement is associated with **Permutation Invariance**.

Consider a node connected to three neighboring nodes.

The graph structure remains unchanged regardless of the order in which those neighbors are listed.

Therefore, a model that aggregates information from the neighbors should produce the same result for every ordering of those neighbors.

For example, suppose the neighboring nodes are:

$$
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
$$

The following orderings describe the same neighborhood:

$$
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
$$

$$
(v_{3},v_{1},v_{2})
$$

A permutation-invariant neighborhood aggregation operation should produce identical results for both orderings.

This is an important distinction from architectures in which input positions have specific meanings.

For example, in a conventional sequence model, changing the order of elements may change the interpretation of the sequence.

In a graph, however, arbitrary storage order should not determine the learned representation.

Graph learning methods must therefore use operations that respect the unordered nature of graph neighborhoods.

For node-level prediction, this principle is also related to **Permutation Equivariance**: consistently relabeling graph nodes should correspondingly relabel the model's node-level outputs without changing their underlying values.

### 3.2.3. Complex Relational Dependencies

Graphs can represent dependencies that extend beyond direct relationships between neighboring nodes.

For example, a user in a recommender system may be influenced by:

- Products they have previously viewed.
- Products they have purchased.
- Other users with similar preferences.
- Friends within a social network.
- Items associated with those users.

These relationships can form complex networks in which information propagates through multiple intermediate entities.

This creates **Multi-Hop Dependencies**.

A one-hop relationship connects two directly adjacent nodes.

A two-hop relationship connects nodes through one intermediate node.

More generally, a multi-hop relationship involves a path containing several edges.

For example, consider the following relationship:

$$
u_{1}\rightarrow i_{1}\rightarrow u_{2}\rightarrow i_{2}
$$

Where:

- $u_{1}$ and $u_{2}$ represent users.
- $i_{1}$ and $i_{2}$ represent items.
- The edges represent user–item interactions.

Suppose both users interacted with item $i_{1}$.

If user $u_{2}$ also interacted with item $i_{2}$, this multi-hop relationship may provide evidence that item $i_{2}$ could be relevant to user $u_{1}$.

Although user $u_{1}$ has not directly interacted with item $i_{2}$, the graph contains an indirect relationship between them.

Such relationships are fundamental to collaborative recommendation.

### 3.2.4. Hierarchical and Cascading Effects

Information and influence may propagate through several layers of a graph.

For example, a user's preferences may be influenced by multiple friends, whose own preferences may be influenced by other users.

Similarly, an item's relevance may depend on relationships with several users and other items.

These effects can propagate through the network in a hierarchical or cascading manner.

Traditional machine learning models designed primarily for independent feature vectors are not inherently structured to capture these multi-hop relational dependencies.

Although such relationships can sometimes be encoded through manually engineered features, doing so becomes increasingly difficult as the graph grows in size and complexity.

Graph-specific learning methods provide mechanisms for incorporating relational structure directly into representation learning.

## 3.3. Learning Representations from Graphs

To exploit the information contained in graph structures, researchers have developed machine learning methods specifically designed for graph data.

Two important families of approaches are:

1. **Graph Embedding Methods**
2. **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)**

### 3.3.1. Graph Embedding Methods

**Graph Embedding** methods aim to map graph entities or entire graphs into dense, lower-dimensional vector representations.

The objective is to preserve useful structural information while transforming graph data into numerical representations suitable for machine learning.

For example, a node embedding maps a node $v$ into a vector:

$$
\mathbf{z}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{z}_{v}$ is the learned embedding of node $v$.
- $d$ is the embedding dimension.
- $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denotes a $d$-dimensional real-valued vector space.

The learned representation should capture meaningful information about the node's position and relationships within the graph.

For example, nodes with similar structural roles or closely related neighborhoods may receive similar embeddings, depending on the embedding method and its learning objective.

In recommender systems, graph embeddings can represent users and items in a shared latent space.

These representations can then support tasks such as:

- User–item relevance estimation.
- Similar-item retrieval.
- Link prediction.
- Personalized recommendation.

Graph embedding methods provide a way to transform complex relational information into compact numerical representations.

### 3.3.2. Graph Neural Networks

**Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)** are neural architectures designed specifically for learning from graph-structured data.

Unlike conventional neural networks that primarily operate on fixed-dimensional feature vectors, images, or sequences, GNNs explicitly incorporate graph connectivity into their computations.

A central idea in many GNN architectures is that a node can learn a representation by combining information from its neighbors.

This process is commonly known as **Neighborhood Aggregation** or **Message Passing**.

Conceptually, a GNN repeatedly performs two operations:

**1. Neighborhood Information Aggregation**

Each node collects information from its neighboring nodes.

**2. Node Representation Update**

The collected information is combined with the node's existing representation to produce an updated representation.

By repeating this process across multiple layers, a node can incorporate information from increasingly distant parts of the graph.

For example:

- One layer can incorporate information from direct neighbors.
- Two layers can incorporate information from nodes reachable through two-hop paths.
- Additional layers can capture information from more distant neighborhoods.

This mechanism enables GNNs to model complex relationships that are difficult to represent using conventional feature-based approaches.

### 3.3.3. Why Graph Learning Methods Are Suitable for Recommendation

Graph embedding methods and GNNs are designed to address the structural characteristics of relational data.

They offer mechanisms for handling:

**Variable Graph Structures**

Graph learning methods can operate on graphs containing different numbers of nodes, edges, and neighborhood connections.

**Permutation-Invariant Neighborhood Processing**

Appropriate aggregation functions can ensure that arbitrary changes in neighbor ordering do not alter learned node representations.

**Complex Relational Dependencies**

Graph-based methods can exploit direct and indirect relationships among users, items, and other entities.

**Multi-Hop Information Propagation**

Information can be aggregated across multiple graph connections, allowing models to capture collaborative signals beyond immediate interactions.

These capabilities make graph learning methods particularly attractive for recommender systems.

By learning from both entity attributes and relational structure, graph-based recommendation models can potentially generate more accurate, relevant, and interpretable recommendations.
