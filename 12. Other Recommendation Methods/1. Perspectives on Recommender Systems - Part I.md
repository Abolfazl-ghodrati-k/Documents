As emphasized throughout this course, recommender systems have become essential components of the modern digital ecosystem. They help users discover products, services, and content that align with their individual preferences.

Recommender systems can be classified from several perspectives, each providing different insights into their design, objectives, and practical applications.

In previous chapters, we examined the fundamental categories of recommender systems based on their underlying algorithms, including collaborative filtering, content-based filtering, and hybrid approaches.

However, algorithmic classification is only one way to understand recommendation systems.

A broader classification considers four principal dimensions:

1. **Recommendation Stage:** The role of a recommendation model within a multi-stage recommendation pipeline.
2. **Recommendation Scenario:** The characteristics of the interaction environment and the types of information available.
3. **Recommendation Objective:** The specific outcomes that the recommendation system is designed to optimize.
4. **Application Domain:** The practical context in which the recommendation system operates.

This section examines the first two dimensions: recommendation stages and recommendation scenarios. The remaining dimensions, recommendation objectives and application domains, are discussed in the following section.

## 1.1. Classification by Recommendation Stage

Large-scale recommender systems commonly employ a **multi-stage recommendation pipeline**, in which different models perform distinct tasks.

Rather than evaluating every available item using a single computationally expensive model, the system progressively reduces the number of candidate items.

A typical recommendation pipeline consists of three stages:

1. **Matching (Candidate Retrieval)**
2. **Ranking**
3. **Re-Ranking**

Each stage has different computational requirements, modeling objectives, and optimization constraints.

### 1.1.1. Matching

**Matching**, also known as **Candidate Retrieval** or **Candidate Generation**, is the first stage of a large-scale recommendation pipeline.

Its primary objective is to retrieve a relatively small set of potentially relevant items from an extremely large item collection.

In practical systems, the complete catalog may contain millions or even billions of items.

Evaluating every item using a sophisticated ranking model would be computationally expensive and incompatible with strict online serving latency requirements.

Therefore, the matching stage substantially reduces the search space.

For example, a recommendation system may reduce a catalog containing millions of products to a candidate set containing only several hundred potentially relevant products.

The matching stage focuses on two principal objectives:

- **Efficient Candidate Retrieval:** Identifying potentially relevant items with minimal computational overhead.
- **Coarse-Grained Preference Modeling:** Capturing broad patterns in user preferences without performing highly detailed relevance estimation.

Because of the large search space and strict latency constraints, matching models must be computationally efficient.

Complex neural architectures that require expensive inference for every possible user–item pair are generally unsuitable for exhaustive candidate evaluation.

Instead, practical matching systems often rely on compact representations, efficient retrieval algorithms, or architectures that support fast candidate search.

For example, user and item embeddings may be computed and used to retrieve candidates through approximate nearest-neighbor search.

The central challenge is to balance computational efficiency with the ability to retrieve relevant items.

A matching model that is extremely fast but consistently excludes relevant items will limit the effectiveness of all subsequent recommendation stages.

### 1.1.2. Ranking

After matching, candidate items retrieved from multiple sources or retrieval channels are combined into a unified candidate list.

The **Ranking Stage** assigns relevance scores to these candidates and orders them according to their estimated suitability for the user.

Typically, the highest-scoring items are selected for further processing.

For example, several hundred candidates may be reduced to a few dozen highly ranked items.

Because the number of candidate items is substantially smaller than the original catalog, the ranking stage can employ more computationally sophisticated models.

These models may incorporate a wide range of features, including:

- User profile information.
- Historical interaction patterns.
- Item attributes.
- Contextual information.
- Relationships between users and items.
- Complex interactions among different features.

Advanced neural architectures, including Transformer-based models, can be used to capture these relationships.

The primary objective of ranking is to achieve **fine-grained relevance estimation**.

Unlike matching, which identifies broadly relevant candidates, ranking attempts to distinguish among items that may all appear potentially relevant.

For example, two products may belong to the same category and match a user's general interests, but their specific characteristics may make one substantially more suitable than the other.

The ranking model must learn such distinctions.

A central challenge is **Feature Interaction Modeling**.

When many heterogeneous features are involved, the model must learn how combinations of features influence user preferences.

For example, the relevance of a product category may depend on the user's recent browsing history, purchase frequency, and current context.

Capturing these nonlinear and context-dependent relationships is essential for accurate ranking.

### 1.1.3. Re-Ranking

The ranking stage primarily focuses on estimating item relevance.

However, a list containing individually relevant items does not necessarily constitute an optimal recommendation list.

For example, the highest-ranked items may be extremely similar to one another.

Although each item may be relevant individually, displaying many nearly identical recommendations can reduce the overall usefulness of the list.

The **Re-Ranking Stage** addresses this problem by modifying the ordering or composition of the ranked candidate list.

Its objective is to satisfy additional recommendation requirements beyond individual relevance.

These requirements may include:

- **Novelty:** Introducing items that users have not previously encountered.
- **Diversity:** Avoiding excessive similarity among recommended items.
- **Fairness:** Providing appropriate exposure to different items, providers, or groups.
- **Business Constraints:** Satisfying operational or commercial requirements.

Re-ranking may involve removing certain items, replacing them, or changing their positions.

An important consideration is the relationship between items within the recommendation list.

For example, suppose the ranking model assigns high scores to several products that are nearly identical substitutes.

Displaying these products consecutively may create unnecessary redundancy.

A re-ranking model can identify this relationship and adjust the list to provide a more diverse selection.

Thus, re-ranking evaluates not only the relevance of individual items but also the quality of the recommendation list as a whole.

The three stages can be summarized as follows:

| Stage | Primary Objective | Typical Scale | Main Challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matching | Retrieve potentially relevant candidates | Millions or billions of items | Efficient retrieval under strict latency constraints |
| Ranking | Estimate detailed relevance and select top candidates | Hundreds or thousands of candidates | Modeling complex feature interactions |
| Re-Ranking | Optimize the final recommendation list | Dozens of highly ranked items | Balancing relevance, diversity, novelty, fairness, and business requirements |

## 1.2. Classification by Recommendation Scenario

Recommender systems can also be classified according to the interaction scenarios they address.

Different scenarios involve different types of user behavior, available information, and recommendation objectives.

For example, a recommendation system operating within a social network must account for relationships among users, while a sequential recommender must consider the temporal ordering of interactions.

The following categories represent important recommendation scenarios.

### 1.2.1. Social Recommendation

Social platforms have fundamentally changed how users discover, evaluate, and interact with content.

In these environments, individual behavior is influenced not only by personal preferences but also by social relationships.

**Social Recommendation** incorporates information about relationships among users into the recommendation process.

Two important concepts are particularly relevant.

**Social Influence**

Social influence refers to the effect that other people's actions, preferences, or opinions have on an individual's behavior.

For example, a user on WeChat Video may become interested in a particular video because several friends have liked it.

The user's interaction may therefore be influenced by social connections rather than solely by personal preferences.

**Social Homophily**

Social homophily describes the tendency of individuals with similar characteristics or preferences to establish social relationships.

For example, users who enjoy similar types of music may be more likely to connect with one another.

This creates an important relationship between social network structure and user preferences.

However, social influence and social homophily are conceptually different.

Social influence describes how relationships affect behavior, whereas social homophily describes how similarity can contribute to the formation of relationships.

Social recommendation methods attempt to exploit these relationships to improve recommendation quality.

By incorporating social connections, the system can obtain additional information about user preferences, particularly when direct interaction histories are limited.

### 1.2.2. Sequential Recommendation

Users generate sequences of interactions over time.

These interactions may include:

- Viewing products.
- Clicking recommendations.
- Watching videos.
- Purchasing items.
- Listening to music.

The ordering of these interactions often contains valuable information about user preferences.

**Sequential Recommendation** aims to learn patterns from a user's ordered interaction history and predict the next item with which the user is likely to interact.

Suppose a user's historical interaction sequence is:

$$
S_{u}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
$$

Where:

- $S_{u}$ represents the interaction sequence of user $u$.
- $i_{j}$ denotes the item associated with the $j$-th interaction.
- $t$ represents the current sequence length.

The objective is to predict the next item:

$$
i_{t+1}
$$

Traditional recommendation methods, such as conventional collaborative filtering, often model user preferences using observed user–item interactions without explicitly considering their temporal ordering.

In contrast, sequential recommendation attempts to capture patterns that depend on the order and evolution of user behavior.

For example, a user who recently purchased a camera may subsequently become interested in camera accessories.

The sequence of interactions provides information that may not be available from an unordered collection of historical purchases.

#### Challenges in Sequential Recommendation

Sequential recommendation involves two principal challenges.

**1. Modeling Short-Term, Long-Term, and Dynamic Preferences**

The model must extract meaningful preference information from each interaction sequence.

This becomes increasingly difficult as the sequence length grows.

A user's interests may contain several components:

- **Short-Term Preferences:** Interests associated with recent interactions.
- **Long-Term Preferences:** Relatively stable interests developed over extended periods.
- **Dynamic Preferences:** Interests that evolve as the user interacts with new items.

A successful sequential recommender must account for these components when predicting the next interaction.

Long sequences can make it difficult to distinguish recent behavioral signals from persistent preferences.

**2. Learning Collaborative Signals Across Sequences**

Sequential recommendation must also consider relationships among different interaction sequences.

The same item may appear in multiple users' sequences.

Similarly, a single user may generate multiple sequences over time.

These overlapping interactions contain collaborative information.

For example, if many users interact with similar groups of items in comparable orders, the model may discover useful relationships among those items.

Extracting such cross-sequence collaborative signals can improve representation learning and recommendation accuracy.

### 1.2.3. Session-Based Recommendation

In some recommendation scenarios, long-term user interaction histories are unavailable.

This may occur because:

- Users interact anonymously.
- Persistent user identifiers are unavailable.
- Storage resources are limited.
- Long-term behavior tracking is unnecessary or restricted.

Such situations are common in certain online retail and mobile content consumption environments.

For example, a recommendation service may observe only the interactions performed during a single browsing session.

The system cannot necessarily access the user's previous sessions or a persistent user profile.

Traditional collaborative filtering approaches may perform poorly in these circumstances because they depend on historical user–item interactions.

**Session-Based Recommendation (SBR)** addresses this problem by predicting the next item using only the behavioral information available within the current session.

A session may consist of a short sequence of interactions:

$$
S=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
$$

The objective is to predict the next item in that session.

#### Difference Between Sequential and Session-Based Recommendation

Sequential recommendation may use a user's interaction history across multiple sessions.

Session-based recommendation, in its anonymous setting, treats each session independently.

Even when two sessions belong to the same individual, the model may have no reliable information linking them.

Consequently, session-based recommendation focuses on **Session-Level Preferences** rather than persistent user profiles.

For example, a user browsing several smartphone accessories during a session may receive recommendations based on those recent interactions, even if the system does not know the user's identity.

This makes session-based recommendation particularly useful when user identification and long-term behavioral tracking are unavailable.

### 1.2.4. Bundle Recommendation

Traditional recommender systems generally focus on recommending individual items.

However, many real-world applications involve recommending collections of related items.

A **Bundle** is a collection of items presented or consumed together.

Bundles are also important marketing tools because they allow products to be promoted as coordinated groups.

**Bundle Recommendation** aims to recommend combinations of items rather than isolated products.

Examples include:

- Music playlists on Spotify.
- Pinterest boards.
- Computer component bundles.
- Book collections on Amazon.
- Coordinated fashion outfits.
- Pharmaceutical combinations.

In these scenarios, recommendation quality depends not only on the relevance of each individual item but also on how well the items function together.

For example, recommending a collection of computer components requires considering compatibility among the components.

Similarly, recommending a fashion outfit requires evaluating whether the selected clothing items form a coherent combination.

Thus, bundle recommendation introduces an additional modeling requirement: learning relationships among items within the same bundle.

The system must consider both user preferences and the collective suitability of the recommended item set.

### 1.2.5. Cross-Domain Recommendation

Users frequently interact with products and services across multiple domains.

For example, the same individual may consume movies, music, books, and online shopping content.

Information from one domain may provide useful evidence about preferences in another.

**Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR)** exploits information from multiple domains to improve recommendation performance.

This approach is particularly promising for addressing:

- Cold-start problems.
- Data sparsity.
- Limited interaction histories.

Cross-domain recommendation methods can be broadly divided into two categories.

#### Single-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation (STCDR)

In **Single-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation**, knowledge is transferred from a source domain to a specific target domain.

The information transfer is unidirectional.

For example, a recommendation system may use information from users' movie preferences to improve recommendations in a book domain.

In this setting:

- The movie domain serves as the source domain.
- The book domain serves as the target domain.

The objective is to improve recommendation performance in the target domain using information learned from the source domain.

#### Dual-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation (DTCDR)

**Dual-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation** allows information to be transferred in both directions.

Rather than treating one domain exclusively as the source and the other exclusively as the target, both domains can benefit from shared knowledge.

For example, movie preferences may help improve book recommendations, while book preferences may also contribute to better movie recommendations.

This bidirectional information transfer allows both domains to benefit from their complementary interaction data.

#### Multi-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation (MTCDR)

The same principle can be extended to more than two domains.

In **Multi-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation**, information from several domains is used jointly to improve recommendations.

For example, a system may combine information from:

- Movies.
- Music.
- Books.
- Online shopping.

The goal is to exploit shared patterns and complementary information across multiple domains.

Cross-domain recommendation can improve representation learning when individual domains contain insufficient interaction data.

### 1.2.6. Multi-Behavior Recommendation

Users interact with recommender systems through multiple types of behavior.

For example, on a video platform, a user may:

- Click a video.
- Save it.
- Share it.
- Comment on it.

Similarly, on an e-commerce platform, a user may:

- Click a product.
- View its details.
- Add it to a shopping cart.
- Save it to a wishlist.
- Share it.
- Purchase it.

These behaviors provide different types of information about user preferences.

**Multi-Behavior Recommendation** attempts to exploit multiple interaction types to improve the prediction of a particular target behavior.

For example, an e-commerce platform may primarily aim to predict purchases.

However, purchases are typically much less frequent than clicks, product views, or other interactions.

Using only purchase records may therefore result in a highly sparse training dataset.

Multi-behavior recommendation addresses this limitation by incorporating additional behavioral signals.

#### Mathematical Representation

Consider a user $u$ and an item $v$.

Suppose there are $K$ different behavior types:

$$
\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{K}\}
$$

For each behavior type $i$, define:

$$
y_{i}=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if behavior } i \text{ is observed}\\0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

Where:

- $y_{i}$ indicates whether the $i$-th behavior was observed for the user–item pair.
- $K$ is the total number of behavior types.
- $u$ represents the user.
- $v$ represents the item.

The recommendation system aims to improve the prediction accuracy of a specific target behavior:

$$
y_{t}
$$

For example, the target behavior may be purchasing a product, while clicks, shares, and wishlist additions provide auxiliary information.

#### Challenges in Multi-Behavior Recommendation

Multi-behavior recommendation involves two principal challenges.

**1. Modeling the Influence of Different Behaviors**

Different interaction types have different relationships with the target behavior.

For example, adding a product to a shopping cart may provide stronger evidence of purchase intention than merely viewing the product.

However, these relationships may vary across users.

For one user, adding an item to a wishlist may indicate strong purchase intention.

For another user, wishlist additions may represent only casual interest.

Therefore, the model must learn how different behavior types influence the target behavior for different users.

This requires distinguishing between strong and weak behavioral signals while accounting for individual differences.

**2. Learning Comprehensive Representations Across Behavior Types**

Different user behaviors reflect different aspects of preference.

For example:

- Clicking may indicate curiosity or initial interest.
- Saving may indicate potential future interest.
- Sharing may indicate perceived relevance to others.
- Purchasing may indicate stronger commitment.

These behaviors are not semantically equivalent.

Consequently, treating every interaction type as an identical positive signal may discard important information.

A multi-behavior recommendation model must incorporate the distinct meanings of different behaviors into its representation-learning process.

By learning from multiple behavioral signals, the system can construct richer user and item representations and improve predictions for the target interaction type.
