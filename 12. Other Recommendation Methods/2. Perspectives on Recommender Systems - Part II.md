In the previous section, we examined two perspectives for classifying recommender systems: **recommendation stage** and **recommendation scenario**.

We now consider two additional classification dimensions: **recommendation objectives** and **application domains**.

While stage-based classification describes the role of a model within the recommendation pipeline, and scenario-based classification focuses on the characteristics of user interactions, objective-based classification examines what a recommendation system seeks to optimize. Application-based classification, in turn, considers the particular domains and types of content for which recommendations are generated.

## 2.1. Classification by Recommendation Objective

Recommendation accuracy is often considered the primary objective of recommender systems. However, producing accurate predictions does not necessarily guarantee a satisfactory recommendation experience.

For example, a system may accurately identify a user's preferred category while repeatedly recommending nearly identical items. Similarly, highly relevant recommendations may still be difficult to understand or may systematically disadvantage certain users or content providers.

Therefore, modern recommender systems increasingly consider objectives beyond predictive accuracy.

Three particularly important objectives are:

1. **Diversity**
2. **Explainability**
3. **Fairness**

### 2.1.1. Diversity

**Diversity** refers to the extent to which a recommendation system provides varied items rather than repeatedly recommending similar content.

Diversity is important because excessively homogeneous recommendation lists may discourage users from exploring unfamiliar products, services, or content.

Two principal forms of diversity are commonly considered.

#### Individual-Level Diversity

**Individual-Level Diversity** measures the dissimilarity among items recommended to a particular user.

Its objective is to ensure that a user's recommendation list covers a sufficiently broad range of relevant topics or categories.

For example, an e-commerce recommender may recommend products from several categories rather than displaying only products of one type.

Similarly, a music recommendation system may include songs from multiple genres instead of repeatedly recommending songs from a single genre.

Individual-level diversity can be understood through two related characteristics:

- **Topic Coverage:** The number of distinct topics or categories represented in a recommendation list.
- **Distributional Balance:** The extent to which recommended items are distributed across those topics.

The meaning of a topic depends on the recommendation domain.

For example:

- In e-commerce, topics may correspond to product categories.
- In music recommendation, topics may correspond to musical genres.
- In movie recommendation, topics may correspond to film genres.

A recommendation list with high individual-level diversity exposes the user to a wider range of potentially relevant content.

#### System-Level Diversity

**System-Level Diversity** evaluates recommendation diversity across the entire user population.

Unlike individual-level diversity, which focuses on the composition of one user's recommendation list, system-level diversity examines the recommendations delivered to different users.

A system has low system-level diversity when it repeatedly recommends the same popular items to most users.

This behavior can lead to excessive concentration of recommendation exposure among a relatively small number of popular items.

Meanwhile, less popular items may receive very little visibility.

These less popular items are commonly called **Long-Tail Items**.

The long tail refers to the large collection of items that individually receive relatively few interactions compared with highly popular items.

A system with greater system-level diversity provides broader exposure across the item catalog rather than concentrating recommendations almost exclusively on popular items.

#### Challenges in Achieving Diversity

Achieving diversity at both the individual and system levels involves two fundamental challenges.

**Challenge 1: Differences in Interaction Signal Strength**

Different items and categories often have substantially different amounts of interaction data.

Consider a user named Zahra who spends most of her time shopping for electronic products.

Her interaction history may include:

- Viewing dozens of smartphones.
- Reading numerous product reviews.
- Purchasing several electronic devices.

In contrast, she may have interacted with only a few clothing products.

Consequently, the recommendation system receives much stronger signals about Zahra's interest in electronics than about her interest in clothing.

This imbalance creates a modeling challenge.

The system can learn Zahra's preferences for electronics with relatively high confidence, but it has much less evidence about her preferences for clothing.

Recommending diverse content from less frequently explored categories therefore becomes more difficult.

A similar problem occurs with long-tail items.

Popular items typically accumulate large numbers of interactions, allowing recommendation models to learn relatively informative representations.

Long-tail items receive fewer interactions and therefore provide weaker behavioral signals.

As a result, recommendation systems may struggle to identify relevant long-tail items, even when those items would be valuable to particular users.

**Challenge 2: The Accuracy–Diversity Trade-Off**

A second, and often more significant, challenge is the conflict between recommendation accuracy and diversity.

Recommendation models are commonly optimized to identify items that are most likely to match a user's established preferences.

However, maximizing relevance can lead to highly repetitive recommendation lists.

Consider a user named Omid who consistently watches action movies.

A system optimized exclusively for predictive accuracy may recommend action movies almost exclusively.

Although these recommendations may closely match Omid's historical preferences, they provide little opportunity to discover content from other genres.

Introducing movies from different genres may improve diversity, but some of these recommendations may have lower predicted relevance.

This creates the **Accuracy–Diversity Trade-Off**.

The objective is to balance two potentially competing requirements:

- Recommend items that are highly relevant to the user.
- Maintain sufficient diversity to support discovery and exploration.

An effective recommendation system must consider both objectives rather than optimizing either one in isolation.

### 2.1.2. Explainability

Many contemporary recommender systems employ deep learning architectures that can model complex relationships among users, items, and contextual information.

However, these architectures are often difficult to interpret.

**Explainable Recommendation** aims not only to generate accurate recommendations but also to provide meaningful explanations of how and why particular items are recommended to specific users.

For example, a movie recommendation system might explain that a film was recommended because the user previously enjoyed movies from the same genre or featuring similar characteristics.

The objective is to make recommendation decisions more understandable.

Explainability provides several important benefits.

**Transparency**

Explanations help users understand the factors influencing recommendation decisions.

Greater transparency can reduce uncertainty about how the system uses available information.

**Persuasiveness**

An understandable explanation may make a recommendation more convincing.

For example, a user may be more willing to consider an unfamiliar product when the system explains its relationship to previously expressed preferences.

**User Trust**

Providing meaningful explanations can increase confidence in the recommendation process.

Users may be more willing to rely on a system when they understand the reasoning behind its suggestions.

**Debugging and Model Improvement**

Explainability also benefits developers and researchers.

By examining the factors influencing recommendations, specialists may identify:

- Incorrectly learned relationships.
- Unexpected model behavior.
- Potential sources of bias.
- Problems in feature processing.
- Opportunities for model refinement.

Thus, explainability contributes both to the user experience and to the development and maintenance of recommendation models.

### 2.1.3. Fairness

Recommender systems learn from historical data and are influenced by the algorithms used to process that data.

Consequently, they may reproduce or amplify existing biases.

**Fairness in Recommender Systems** aims to prevent recommendation decisions from systematically discriminating against particular users, user groups, items, or content providers.

Fairness-aware recommendation methods attempt to identify and mitigate biases that could produce unequal or unjustified outcomes.

Two principal forms of fairness can be distinguished according to the stakeholders involved.

#### User Fairness

**User Fairness** focuses on ensuring that recommendation quality and opportunities are not systematically biased against particular users or demographic groups.

For example, a recommendation system should avoid providing consistently lower-quality recommendations to certain user populations because of biases in the training data or model.

The objective is to prevent unfair differences in recommendation outcomes across users or groups.

#### Item Fairness

**Item Fairness** concerns the fair representation and exposure of items within the recommendation system.

A common problem is **Popularity Bias**, in which already popular items receive disproportionately high recommendation exposure.

Because popular items accumulate more interactions, models may repeatedly recommend them, further increasing their popularity.

Meanwhile, less popular items may receive insufficient exposure even when they are relevant to particular users.

Item fairness seeks to reduce such systematic disadvantages and provide more equitable opportunities for different items to appear in recommendation results.

Fairness is therefore a multi-stakeholder concern.

A recommendation system must consider not only the relevance of its recommendations but also how its decisions affect different users and items.

## 2.2. Classification by Application Domain

Recommender systems can also be classified according to the application domain in which they operate or the type of content they recommend.

Different application domains introduce distinct data characteristics, user behaviors, and optimization requirements.

### 2.2.1. Product Recommendation

**Product Recommendation**, also known as **E-Commerce Recommendation**, is one of the most widely recognized applications of recommender systems.

Its primary purpose is to recommend products that users are likely to find relevant or purchase.

In e-commerce environments, recommendation performance is closely associated with commercial value.

Consequently, systems must consider user behaviors that are strongly related to business outcomes.

Important interaction types include:

- Product views.
- Clicks.
- Adding products to shopping carts.
- Purchases.

Purchase behavior is particularly important because it is directly related to platform revenue.

However, other interactions also provide valuable information about user preferences and purchase intentions.

Product recommendation systems commonly use:

- Browsing histories.
- Previous purchases.
- Explicit user preferences.
- Behavioral similarities among users.

For example, an online shopping platform may recommend products based on items a user has recently viewed, previously purchased, or items preferred by users with similar interests.

The objective is to identify products that are relevant to individual users while supporting the commercial goals of the platform.

### 2.2.2. Point-of-Interest Recommendation

**Point-of-Interest (POI) Recommendation** focuses on recommending physical locations that users may wish to visit.

Examples include:

- Restaurants.
- Tourist attractions.
- Retail stores.
- Entertainment venues.
- Other geographically identifiable locations.

Unlike conventional online product recommendation, POI recommendation must account for the physical characteristics of locations and the constraints associated with visiting them.

Two factors are particularly important:

1. **Spatial Factors**
2. **Temporal Factors**

#### Spatial Factors

Spatial factors describe the geographical characteristics of points of interest.

The most important spatial attribute is geographical location.

Users' activities are strongly influenced by the areas in which they live, work, or travel.

For example, a user may prefer a nearby restaurant over a distant restaurant even if the distant restaurant has a higher predicted relevance score.

This occurs because visiting a physical location requires travel, time, and effort.

Unlike online products, which users can browse or purchase remotely, physical points of interest are constrained by geographical accessibility.

Therefore, POI recommendation systems must account for the relationship between users' activity regions and candidate locations.

#### Temporal Factors

Temporal factors are also essential because visits and check-ins occur over time.

A user's sequence of visited locations can reveal meaningful behavioral patterns.

For example, the locations a user visits during the morning may differ from those visited during the evening.

Similarly, previous visits may provide information about likely future destinations.

Because location visits form temporal sequences, **Sequential Recommendation** methods are particularly relevant to POI recommendation.

These methods can learn patterns from historical visit sequences and use them to predict the next location a user may visit.

Effective POI recommendation therefore requires the integration of spatial and temporal information.

### 2.2.3. News Recommendation

**News Recommendation** helps users discover news articles that match their interests, reading habits, and current information needs.

A news recommendation system may consider:

- Previously read articles.
- Preferred news topics.
- Reading behavior.
- Current events.
- The relevance and freshness of available articles.

News recommendation differs from many other recommendation applications because news items contain substantial textual information.

Consequently, the system must construct meaningful representations of news content.

#### Textual Content Modeling

News articles contain information such as:

- Headlines.
- Article bodies.
- Topics.
- Named entities.
- Semantic relationships.

**Natural Language Processing (NLP)** techniques can be combined with recommendation models to extract informative features from this textual content.

These representations help the system estimate relationships between articles and user interests.

#### News Freshness

Another important characteristic is the time-sensitive nature of news.

Users generally prefer recent and relevant information.

An article that was highly relevant several days ago may no longer be useful when newer developments become available.

Consequently, news recommendation systems must account for content freshness.

#### Rapidly Changing Candidate Sets

The available collection of news articles changes continuously.

New articles are published frequently, while older articles may rapidly lose relevance.

Therefore, the recommendation system must select appropriate items from a highly dynamic candidate pool.

This creates a significant challenge because the model must continuously adapt to new content and changing user interests.

### 2.2.4. Movie Recommendation

**Movie Recommendation** is one of the earliest and most extensively studied applications of recommender systems.

Research in this domain has contributed substantially to the development of recommendation algorithms.

A notable example is the **Netflix Prize**, a movie recommendation competition that stimulated extensive research into collaborative filtering and predictive recommendation methods.

Movie recommendation systems typically use information such as:

- User ratings.
- Viewing histories.
- Previous interactions.
- Movie characteristics.
- Similarities among users or movies.

The objective is to recommend movies that users are likely to enjoy.

For example, a system may identify movies preferred by users with similar viewing histories or estimate a user's preference for an unseen movie based on previously rated films.

Movie recommendation has played an important role in the development and evaluation of recommendation techniques.

### 2.2.5. Video Recommendation

**Video Recommendation** is closely related to movie recommendation but covers a broader range of video content.

It includes:

- Movies.
- Long-form videos.
- Short-form videos.
- User-generated content.
- Other forms of online video.

Major application environments include platforms such as:

- Instagram.
- YouTube.
- TikTok.

Although movie and video recommendation share several modeling principles, video recommendation often involves a wider variety of content formats and interaction patterns.

For example, users may consume short videos rapidly, generating dense sequences of interactions within relatively short periods.

Video recommendation systems must therefore account for the characteristics of different content formats and user consumption behaviors.

### 2.2.6. Music Recommendation

**Music Recommendation** focuses on recommending musical content according to user preferences and listening behavior.

Popular music streaming platforms such as Spotify and Apple Music use recommendation systems to suggest:

- Songs.
- Artists.
- Playlists.

Music recommendation systems may use listening histories, previously preferred songs, and relationships among musical items to identify relevant content.

For example, a system may recommend songs similar to those a user frequently listens to or suggest artists associated with the user's established musical preferences.

Playlist recommendation introduces an additional consideration because the system may need to recommend a collection of songs rather than an individual track.

This creates a connection between music recommendation and bundle recommendation, where the compatibility and collective suitability of multiple items become important.
