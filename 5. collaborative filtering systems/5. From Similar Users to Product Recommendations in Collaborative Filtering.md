In the preceding sections on collaborative filtering, we introduced User-Based Collaborative Filtering (UBCF), established that its central task is to identify similar users, examined explicit and implicit feedback and their implications for the problem formulation, and reviewed several measures of user similarity. We can now take the final practical step: use similarity values and the ratings provided by similar users to produce actual recommendations for a target user.

This section focuses specifically on the complete UBCF procedure for **explicit feedback** in the form of ratings. After computing similarities, we identify the nearest neighbors of a target user, use the ratings that those neighbors assigned to items the target user has not rated to estimate predicted ratings, and finally construct a ranked recommendation list. This procedure is the core of a rating-based UBCF implementation.

## Step 1: Finding Neighbors

Computing similarity between a target user and every other user in the system can be computationally expensive, especially in large systems with millions of users. Unless advanced optimization techniques are used, it is typically not performed entirely in real time. In practice, we seek a smaller, relevant set of users: the **nearest neighbors**.

In this context, a neighbor is a user who meets the following conditions:

1. The user is sufficiently similar to the target user according to the selected similarity measure, such as Pearson correlation.
2. The user has enough co-rated or commonly interacted-with items in common with the target user for the similarity estimate to be meaningful. For example, the pair may be required to have rated at least a specified number of common items. This filter helps prevent chance similarities.
3. The user has rated the target item whose rating we wish to predict. Without such a rating, the user's preference cannot contribute directly to the prediction.

### 1.1 Selecting the Top-$N$ Neighbors

After similarities have been computed or retrieved, the usual procedure for each target user and each unrated item for which a prediction is required is as follows:

1. Identify the users who have rated the target item.
2. From these users, retain those who qualify as potential neighbors; for example, their similarity exceeds a threshold or their number of common interactions with the target user is sufficient.
3. Select the $N$ users with the greatest similarity to the target user from this candidate set. These users are the nearest neighbors for that particular item.

### Challenges in Neighbor Selection

- **Choosing $N$:** The number of neighbors, $N$, is an important hyperparameter. If $N$ is too small, the available information may be insufficient for a reliable prediction. If $N$ is too large, less similar users may introduce noise. The best value is usually selected empirically using training and validation data.
- **Filtering thresholds:** A system must choose thresholds for minimum similarity and minimum numbers of common interactions before treating a user as a potential neighbor.
- **Efficient retrieval:** Even if similarities are computed offline, rapidly finding the top-$N$ neighbors for an active user can require efficient data structures, such as indexes, at serving time.

## Step 2: Predicting Ratings

Once the top-$N$ neighbors for a target user and target item have been identified, their ratings for that item can be used to predict the target user's rating. Several prediction algorithms are available, but the most common basic approach is a weighted average.

### 2.1 Simple Averaging

The simplest method is to calculate the mean rating given to the target item by the $N$ neighbors:

$$\hat{r}_{ui}=\frac{1}{\lvert N(u)\rvert}\sum_{v\in N(u)}r_{vi}$$

Here, $N(u)$ is the set of neighbors of user $u$, and $r_{vi}$ is the rating assigned by user $v$ to item $i$.

This method has two important limitations. First, it ignores the strength of each neighbor's similarity: a neighbor with similarity $0.8$ has the same influence as one with similarity $0.2$. Second, it does not account for neighbors' rating biases. For example, some neighbors may generally assign ratings that are higher than the overall average.

### 2.2 Weighted Averaging: The Standard Approach

Weighted averaging is the standard UBCF approach for explicit feedback. Each neighbor's contribution is weighted by their similarity to the target user. To account for differences in rating behavior, the calculation uses each neighbor's deviation from their own mean rating. Thus, a more similar neighbor has greater influence on the prediction.

The mean-centered weighted-average formula is:

$$\hat{r}_{ui}=\bar{r}_{u}+\frac{\sum_{v\in N(u)}sim(u,v)(r_{vi}-\bar{r}_{v})}{\sum_{v\in N(u)}\lvert sim(u,v)\rvert}$$

where:

- $\hat{r}_{ui}$ is the predicted rating of user $u$ for item $i$.
- $\bar{r}_{u}$ is the mean rating assigned by user $u$ across all items they have rated. This term returns the prediction to the target user's own rating scale.
- $N(u)$ is the set of $N$ neighbors of user $u$ who have also rated item $i$.
- $sim(u,v)$ is the similarity between target user $u$ and neighbor $v$, computed in an earlier step using Pearson correlation, cosine similarity, or another measure. It serves as the weight.
- $r_{vi}$ is the actual rating that neighbor $v$ assigned to item $i$.
- $\bar{r}_{v}$ is the mean rating assigned by neighbor $v$ across all items they have rated. The term $(r_{vi}-\bar{r}_{v})$ indicates whether neighbor $v$ rated the item above or below their own average.
- $\sum_{v\in N(u)}\lvert sim(u,v)\rvert$ is the sum of the absolute similarity values of the neighbors. It normalizes the weighted contribution. The absolute value keeps the denominator positive when negatively correlated neighbors are included.

This formula is preferable for three reasons:

1. **It accounts for similarity.** More similar neighbors, with larger $sim(u,v)$ values, receive greater weight.
2. **It reduces neighbor-rating bias.** By subtracting each neighbor's mean rating, the calculation considers how much the neighbor liked the item relative to their usual behavior. Neighbors who are generally generous or strict raters are less likely to distort the result.
3. **It adapts to the target user's scale.** Adding the target user's mean rating at the end places the prediction on that user's rating scale.

More advanced methods may use regression or more complex statistical models for prediction. Nevertheless, weighted averaging is the foundation for many such approaches and remains the most common technique in UBCF with explicit ratings.

## Step 3: Generating Recommendations from Predicted Ratings

After predicted ratings have been computed for a set of items that the target user has not rated—for example, items rated by at least one close neighbor but not yet encountered by the target user—the final step is straightforward:

1. Sort the items in descending order of predicted rating.
2. Present the top $K$ items from this ranked list as the final recommendations. Here, $K$ is the desired number of recommendations, such as the top 10 movies.

## A Step-by-Step Example

Consider the following rating matrix. Ratings range from 1 to 5, and a question mark indicates that no rating is available.

| User $\\backslash$ Item | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| User 1 | 5 | ? | 4 | ? | 1 |
| User 2 | ? | ? | ? | 5 | 4 |
| User 3 | 4 | ? | 5 | 4 | ? |
| User 4 | ? | 3 | ? | ? | 5 |

The mean rating of each user is:

$$\bar{r}_{1}=\frac{5+4+1}{3}=3.33,\quad\bar{r}_{2}=\frac{5+4}{2}=4.5,\quad\bar{r}_{3}=\frac{4+5+4}{3}=4.33,\quad\bar{r}_{4}=\frac{3+5}{2}=4$$

### Steps 1 and 2: Finding Neighbors for User 1 and Item B

Which users have rated item B? Only User 4 has done so. Therefore, User 4 is the only potential neighbor for item B. The neighbor set is $N(1)=\{4\}$; in this simple example, $N=1$.

Assume that the Pearson similarity between User 1 and User 4 has already been calculated as $0.6$, so $sim(1,4)=0.6$.

### Step 3: Predicting the Rating with Weighted Averaging

Using the weighted-average formula:

$$\hat{r}_{1B}=\bar{r}_{1}+\frac{\sum_{v\in N(1)}sim(1,v)(r_{vB}-\bar{r}_{v})}{\sum_{v\in N(1)}\lvert sim(1,v)\rvert}$$

Because User 4 is the only neighbor:

$$\hat{r}_{1B}=\bar{r}_{1}+\frac{sim(1,4)(r_{4B}-\bar{r}_{4})}{\lvert sim(1,4)\rvert}$$

$$\hat{r}_{1B}=3.33+\frac{0.6(3-4)}{\lvert0.6\rvert}$$

$$\hat{r}_{1B}=3.33+\frac{0.6(-1)}{0.6}$$

$$\hat{r}_{1B}=3.33-1$$

$$\hat{r}_{1B}=2.33$$

Thus, the predicted rating of User 1 for item B is $2.33$.

### Step 4: Producing Recommendations

If this process is repeated for User 1's other unrated items, B and D, their predicted ratings can be ranked to produce recommendations. For example, suppose the predicted rating for item D is $4.8$. The recommendation list for User 1 would be, in order: item D, with predicted rating $4.8$, followed by item B, with predicted rating $2.33$.

## Example 2

Consider a more practical UBCF example involving five users and five movies in a streaming service. Ratings range from 1 to 5, and a question mark indicates that no rating is available.

| User $\\backslash$ Movie | Movie 1 | Movie 2 | Movie 3 | Movie 4 | Movie 5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Armin (target user) | 5 | ? | 4 | 3 | 1 |
| Behnaz | 4 | 5 | ? | 2 | ? |
| Saeed | ? | 4 | 5 | ? | 3 |
| Maryam | 3 | ? | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| Reza | 5 | 5 | 4 | ? | ? |

Our goal is to calculate Armin's predicted rating for Movie 2, which Armin has not yet rated.

### Step 1: Calculating Each User's Mean Rating

To use the Pearson-based weighted-average formula, we first calculate each user's mean from their observed ratings:

$$\bar{r}_{\text{Armin}}=\frac{5+4+3+1}{4}=3.25$$

$$\bar{r}_{\text{Behnaz}}=\frac{4+5+2}{3}\approx3.67$$

$$\bar{r}_{\text{Saeed}}=\frac{4+5+3}{3}=4.00$$

$$\bar{r}_{\text{Maryam}}=\frac{3+3+4+4}{4}=3.50$$

$$\bar{r}_{\text{Reza}}=\frac{5+5+4}{3}\approx4.67$$

### Step 2: Finding Armin's Neighbors for Movie 2

We first identify the users who rated Movie 2: Behnaz, who assigned a rating of 5; Saeed, who assigned a rating of 4; and Reza, who assigned a rating of 5. Maryam did not rate Movie 2 and is therefore not a potential neighbor for this item.

Assume that similarities, calculated previously using the Pearson correlation coefficient, are as follows:

$$sim(\text{Armin},\text{Behnaz})=0.33$$

$$sim(\text{Armin},\text{Saeed})=0.89$$

$$sim(\text{Armin},\text{Reza})=0.05$$

These similarity values are assumed for illustration. In a real application, they must be computed from the items rated in common by Armin and each of the other users, as described in the preceding section.

Suppose we select the top $N=2$ neighbors. Among the three potential neighbors, the two most similar to Armin are Saeed, with similarity $0.89$, and Behnaz, with similarity $0.33$. Therefore, the neighbor set for predicting Armin's rating of Movie 2 is $N(\text{Armin})=\{\text{Saeed},\text{Behnaz}\}$.

### Step 3: Predicting the Rating with Weighted Averaging

We use the weighted-average formula:

$$\hat{r}_{ui}=\bar{r}_{u}+\frac{\sum_{v\in N(u)}sim(u,v)(r_{vi}-\bar{r}_{v})}{\sum_{v\in N(u)}\lvert sim(u,v)\rvert}$$

Substituting the values for Armin and Movie 2 gives:

$$\hat{r}_{\text{Armin},\text{Movie 2}}=3.25+\frac{0.89(4-4.00)+0.33(5-3.67)}{\lvert0.89\rvert+\lvert0.33\rvert}$$

$$\hat{r}_{\text{Armin},\text{Movie 2}}=3.25+\frac{0+0.4389}{0.89+0.33}$$

$$\hat{r}_{\text{Armin},\text{Movie 2}}=3.25+\frac{0.4389}{1.22}$$

$$\hat{r}_{\text{Armin},\text{Movie 2}}=3.25+0.35975\approx3.61$$

Therefore, Armin's predicted rating for Movie 2 is approximately $3.61$.

### Step 4: Producing the Recommendation

The neighbor-selection and rating-prediction process must be repeated for every eligible item that Armin has not yet rated. In the matrix shown here, Movie 2 is the only such movie. In a larger catalog, the unrated eligible items would be sorted from highest to lowest predicted rating, and the top $K$ items would be recommended to Armin. For example, if additional unrated movies had predicted ratings of $4.5$ and $3.61$, respectively, and $K=2$, they would be recommended in that order.

## Implementation Considerations and Challenges in the Prediction Step

- **Data sparsity:** If none of the selected $N$ neighbors has rated a particular item, no neighborhood-based rating prediction can be made for that item. This is a primary reason why UBCF is challenging in environments with extremely sparse data.
- **Choice of $N$ and filtering rules:** These choices directly affect both rating-prediction accuracy and recommendation coverage. A very small $N$ may produce unstable predictions.
- **Handling zero or negative similarities:** The weighted-average formula can include negatively similar neighbors through negative weights. This can help identify items that the target user is likely to dislike. In practice, implementations should define and evaluate a policy for negative similarities, such as retaining them, excluding them, or using a separate model, because the choice can materially affect accuracy and recommendation behavior.

## Summary

In UBCF with explicit feedback, converting user similarity into actual recommendations consists of three main stages: identifying eligible nearest neighbors, predicting ratings for unseen items from neighbors' ratings—often with a weighted average—and ranking items by predicted rating to produce a recommendation list.

By considering similarity strength and adjusting for each user's average rating behavior, the weighted-average algorithm provides a strong basis for rating prediction. Its effectiveness, however, depends heavily on data quality, an appropriate similarity measure, and a suitable number of neighbors. Its main limitations arise from the difficulty of finding sufficient, meaningful neighbors in sparse user-item matrices.
