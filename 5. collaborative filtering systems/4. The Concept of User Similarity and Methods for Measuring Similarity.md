In previous lessons, we introduced **User-Based Collaborative Filtering (UBCF)** and saw that its core logic is based on identifying users whose preferences or behavior are similar to those of a target user. In the previous lesson, we also saw that the type of data available to us—explicit or implicit—changes the problem that _UBCF_ is designed to solve: **rating prediction** in explicit data versus **interaction-probability prediction** in implicit data.

Regardless of whether the ultimate goal is to predict a rating or an interaction probability, however, the first and most critical step in _UBCF_ remains the same: **computing the degree of “similarity” between the target user and the other users in the system.** Whether two users are considered “similar” depends entirely on the criterion used to measure that similarity.

In this lesson, we examine the concept of similarity in the context of _UBCF_. We will see that different similarity measures view the data in different ways and capture different aspects of what it means for two users to be **“similar.”** Understanding these differences is essential for selecting an appropriate similarity measure based on the type of data available and, ultimately, for producing more accurate recommendations.

In user-based recommender systems, **user similarity** refers to having **similar preference or interaction patterns**. In other words, if two users tend to assign similar ratings to the same items in explicit data, or interact with similar sets of items in implicit data, we consider them similar.

Imagine that you and a friend both give ratings of 5 to 1980s action movies, ratings of 1 to romantic comedies, and ratings of 4 to wildlife documentaries. Your rating patterns are highly similar, even if your overall average ratings differ slightly—for example, your friend may generally be more generous when assigning ratings. In _UBCF_, this similarity in patterns is crucial.

##### **Why Is Similarity Critical?**

1. **Finding reliable neighbors:** The goal is to identify a small group of users who are sufficiently similar to the target user that their behavior can provide a meaningful prediction.
2. **Transferring preference information:** By identifying similar users, we can recommend to the target user items that those users have liked but that the target user has not yet encountered, under the assumption that the target user is also likely to appreciate them.

Technically, to compute the similarity between two users $u$ and $v$, we examine their corresponding rows in the user–item matrix. These rows are vectors in the item space. Similarity measures attempt to quantify the proximity or alignment of these vectors.

## **Primary Measures for Computing User Similarity**

We now introduce and examine commonly used similarity measures. For each measure, we will consider how it works, its corresponding formula, how it differs from the other measures, and, most importantly, how well it fits different types of data—explicit and implicit—and how its use affects the resulting recommendations.

### **1. Cosine Similarity**

Cosine similarity is one of the most widely used similarity measures in recommender systems. It measures similarity based on the **angle between two user vectors** in the item space. User vectors correspond to rows of the user–item matrix, whose values may represent explicit ratings or implicit interaction intensities.

The cosine of an angle of $0^\circ$—that is, two vectors pointing in the same direction—is equal to $1$, representing maximum similarity. The cosine of an angle of $90^\circ$ is equal to $0$, representing no directional similarity. The cosine of an angle of $180^\circ$ is equal to $-1$, representing maximum opposition. **However, in implicit data, where interaction values are nonnegative, the minimum cosine similarity is generally $0$.**

**Formula:** For user vectors $u$ and $v$, corresponding to rows of the user–item matrix:

​​$$
\operatorname{Cosine}(u,v)=\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}r_{ui}r_{vi}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}r_{ui}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}r_{vi}^{2}}}
$$​​


where $I_{uv}$ is the set of items with which **both users** have interacted. Missing entries in the user–item matrix are not considered in this computation, and $r_{ui}$ denotes the interaction value of user $u$ with item $i$, such as a rating, a visit count, or a binary value of $0/1$.

The resulting cosine similarity generally lies in the interval $[-1,1]$. A value of $1$ indicates maximum directional similarity, whereas $-1$ indicates complete opposition. For nonnegative interaction data, such as binary or count-based implicit feedback, the practical range is $[0,1]$.

1. **Suitable for explicit data (ratings):** Cosine similarity is well suited to explicit data, such as ratings on a scale from 1 to 5, because it can identify similar rating patterns. For example, if two users assign similar ratings to similar movies, they will have a high cosine similarity.
2. **Applicable to implicit data (binary or count-based):** When the data are binary ($0$ and $1$), cosine similarity behaves similarly to a normalized overlap measure and is closely related to Jaccard similarity. When the data are count-based, such as the number of visits, cosine similarity can measure similarity according to the relative magnitude or intensity of interactions, identifying users who interact with items with similar relative intensities.

> Users identified as similar according to cosine similarity are those whose preference vectors are directionally aligned. In explicit data, this means users whose relative rating patterns are similar. In binary implicit data, it means users with substantial overlap in the items they have interacted with. In count-based implicit data, it means users whose relative interaction intensities across items are similar.

**Advantages:**

- Simple and easy to understand.
- Performs well with sparse data when the number of shared items is relatively small.
- Relatively fast to compute.

**Disadvantages:**

- It does not explicitly account for differences in users’ rating or interaction scales. For example, if one user generally assigns higher ratings than another, cosine similarity may not fully reflect the underlying similarity in their preferences.
- In implicit data, it may assign inappropriate influence to highly active users.

### **Example**

|**Item 1**|**Item 2**|**Item 3**|
|---|---|---|
|User A|4|0|
|User B|5|1|
|User C|0|0|

The cosine similarity between two vectors $A$ and $B$ is defined as:

$$
\operatorname{Similarity}(A,B)=\frac{A\cdot B}{\lVert A\rVert\lVert B\rVert}
$$


#### **Similarity Between User A and User B**

User A = $(4,0,3)$

User B = $(5,1,4)$

```
A · B = 4×5 + 0×1 + 3×4 = 20 + 0 + 12 = 32

||A|| = sqrt(4² + 0² + 3²) = sqrt(16 + 0 + 9) = sqrt(25) = 5

||B|| = sqrt(5² + 1² + 4²) = sqrt(25 + 1 + 16) = sqrt(42) ≈ 6.48

Similarity(A, B) ≈ 32 / (5 × 6.48) ≈ 32 / 32.4 ≈ 0.99
```

#### **Similarity Between User A and User C**

User C = $(0,0,5)$

```
A · C = 4×0 + 0×0 + 3×5 = 0 + 0 + 15 = 15

||C|| = sqrt(0² + 0² + 5²) = sqrt(25) = 5

Similarity(A, C) = 15 / (5 × 5) = 15 / 25 = 0.6
```

#### **Similarity Between User B and User C**

```
B · C = 5×0 + 1×0 + 4×5 = 0 + 0 + 20 = 20

Similarity(B, C) = 20 / (6.48 × 5) ≈ 20 / 32.4 ≈ 0.62
```

The resulting similarity matrix is:

|**User**|**User A**|**User B**|**User C**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**User A**|1.00|0.99|0.60|
|**User B**|0.99|1.00|0.62|
|**User C**|0.60|0.62|1.00|

The final table shows that, according to cosine similarity, **User A and User B are highly similar to each other**.

### **2. Pearson Correlation Coefficient**

The Pearson correlation coefficient measures the linear relationship between the ratings of two users while taking their mean ratings into account. More specifically, it measures the **linear correlation** between the users’ ratings or interaction values after **removing the effect of each user’s mean rating**.

It answers the following question:

**“Is there a linear relationship between the interactions of two users—for example, their ratings? In other words, when one user’s ratings increase relative to their own average, do the other user’s ratings also increase relative to their own average?”**

For users $u$ and $v$, and their set of commonly rated items $I_{uv}$:

$$
\operatorname{Pearson}(u,v)=\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}(r_{ui}-\bar{r}_{u})(r_{vi}-\bar{r}_{v})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}(r_{ui}-\bar{r}_{u})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}(r_{vi}-\bar{r}_{v})^{2}}}
$$

where:

- $r_{ui}$: the rating assigned by user $u$ to item $i$
- $r_{vi}$: the rating assigned by user $v$ to item $i$
- $\bar{r}_{u}$: the mean rating of user $u$ over the commonly rated items
- $\bar{r}_{v}$: the mean rating of user $v$ over the commonly rated items

> The range extends from $-1$, indicating an inverse relationship, to $1$, indicating perfect positive correlation. In recommender-system implementations, negative correlations are sometimes clipped or transformed depending on the prediction strategy, but the underlying Pearson coefficient itself ranges from $-1$ to $1$.

**Difference from Cosine Similarity:**

- Unlike cosine similarity, which depends directly on the raw rating values, Pearson correlation subtracts the users’ mean ratings and therefore removes the effect of rating scale. This means that if two users exhibit similar rating patterns but use different parts of the rating scale—for example, one consistently assigns ratings of 4 and 5 while the other assigns ratings of 2 and 3—they can still be identified as similar. The key advantage of Pearson over cosine similarity is its ability to disregard the fact that one user consistently assigns higher ratings while another consistently assigns lower ratings. It focuses only on the **relative pattern** of increases and decreases in ratings with respect to each user’s own mean.
- Pearson correlation is particularly useful when users exhibit different rating biases—for example, when some users are systematically more strict than others.
- **Highly suitable for explicit data (numerical ratings).** This is because Pearson correlation relies heavily on the concepts of the mean and deviation from the mean, both of which have a meaningful interpretation for explicit ratings.
- **Not generally suitable for binary implicit data:** For binary implicit data containing only zeros and ones, the mean represents the proportion of interacted items, while deviations from the mean do not carry the same preference-related meaning that they have in explicit ratings. Therefore, Pearson correlation is generally not recommended for binary implicit data. It can be applied to count-based data, such as visit counts, but its interpretation requires care.

> Users identified as similar according to Pearson correlation are users whose **relative preferences** are similar. Suppose User A rates movie X as 5 and movie Y as 4, giving an average of 4.5, while User B rates movie X as 3 and movie Y as 2, giving an average of 2.5. Pearson correlation indicates a perfect positive correlation of $+1$, because both users rate movie X higher than movie Y relative to their own means. Pearson correlation can therefore identify neighbors whose rating scales differ substantially but whose underlying preference patterns are deeply similar.

**Advantages:**

- Compensates for differences in users’ rating scales or rating behavior.
- Can provide high accuracy for explicit data with diverse rating patterns.

**Disadvantages:**

- When only a small number of items are shared, the resulting correlation can be unstable or unreliable.
- Its computation is more involved than that of cosine similarity.

**When should it be used?** Use Pearson correlation when the data are explicit ratings and you want to neutralize user-specific rating-scale bias, provided that users have a sufficient number of commonly rated items.
