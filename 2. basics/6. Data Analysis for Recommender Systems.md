In the previous chapter, we introduced measures of **central tendency** (mean, median, and mode) and **dispersion** (variance and standard deviation). These statistics provide concise summaries of numerical datasets and help us understand their overall characteristics. While these measures are invaluable, they represent only the first step in exploratory data analysis.

When developing recommender systems, we often need to answer more sophisticated questions. For example:

- Are users generally generous or conservative when assigning ratings?
    
- Are ratings concentrated around a particular value, or are they widely dispersed?
    
- Do users with similar preferences exhibit similar rating behaviors?
    
- How can we efficiently analyze datasets that contain millions of interactions?
    

Answering these questions requires additional statistical tools. In this chapter, we introduce three important concepts that are widely used in recommender systems:

- **Data distributions**, which reveal how values are spread throughout a dataset.
    
- **Covariance and correlation**, which measure relationships between numerical variables.
    
- **Sampling**, which enables efficient analysis of large-scale datasets by working with representative subsets.
    

Together, these techniques provide deeper insights into user behavior and data characteristics, ultimately leading to better preprocessing, feature engineering, and recommendation models.

# Data Distributions

During the design and analysis of recommender systems, it is common to encounter questions such as:

- Do most users assign high ratings or low ratings?
    
- Are ratings clustered around the average, or are they evenly distributed across the rating scale?
    
- Are only a handful of items rated by many users, or does user attention spread evenly across the catalog?
    

The answers to these questions can significantly influence the design of a recommendation algorithm. To obtain these insights, we first need to examine the **distribution of the data**.

A **data distribution** describes how frequently different values occur within a dataset and how those values are spread across the numerical range. Rather than focusing on individual observations, distributions provide a global view of the dataset.

Understanding data distributions is particularly important in recommender systems because they reveal the overall structure of user interactions. For example, a distribution may indicate that most users prefer giving high ratings, suggesting a positive rating bias. Alternatively, it may show that only a small number of popular items receive the majority of user interactions, revealing a highly skewed dataset.

Analyzing data distributions also helps us to:

- identify highly active and inactive users,
    
- detect outliers and unusual observations,
    
- measure class imbalance or popularity bias,
    
- evaluate whether preprocessing or normalization is necessary, and
    
- make informed decisions when selecting or designing recommendation algorithms.
    

Consequently, examining data distributions is one of the first steps in exploratory data analysis for recommender systems.

---

## Visualizing Data with Histograms

One of the most effective ways to understand a data distribution is through visualization. Among the available visualization techniques, the **histogram** is one of the most widely used tools for numerical data.

A histogram groups numerical values into intervals, known as **bins**, and displays the number of observations that fall within each interval. This provides an intuitive picture of how data are distributed across the value range.

Pandas can generate histograms using visualization libraries such as **Matplotlib** and **Plotly**. In this chapter, we use **Plotly**, which provides highly interactive visualizations that are particularly well suited for Jupyter Notebooks and web applications.

To demonstrate histogram visualization, we first create a small dataset containing user ratings.

Histograms are equally useful for analyzing user behavior. Instead of visualizing rating values, we can examine how many ratings each user has submitted.

user_rating_counts = ratings_df['user_id'].value_counts()

# Covariance and Correlation

In many data analysis tasks, understanding the distribution of individual variables is only the beginning. In recommender systems, we are often equally interested in **how different variables are related to one another**. For example, we may ask:

- Do users who like one movie also tend to like another?
    
- Do users with similar rating patterns share similar preferences?
    
- Does an increase in one variable correspond to an increase or decrease in another?
    

Questions like these require statistical measures that quantify the relationship between numerical variables. Two of the most widely used measures are **covariance** and **correlation**. Although they are closely related, they differ significantly in interpretation and practical use.

---

## Covariance

**Covariance** measures the extent to which two numerical variables change together. It indicates whether the variables tend to increase and decrease simultaneously or move in opposite directions.

The sign of the covariance provides a basic interpretation:

- **Positive covariance** indicates that both variables generally move in the same direction. As one variable increases, the other tends to increase as well.
    
- **Negative covariance** indicates that the variables move in opposite directions. As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.
    
- A covariance value **close to zero** suggests that there is little or no linear relationship between the variables.
    

Although covariance provides useful information about the direction of a relationship, it has an important limitation: **its magnitude depends on the scale of the variables**.

For example, a covariance value of **1,000** may indicate a very strong relationship in one dataset but only a weak relationship in another. Since covariance has no fixed range, its numerical value is difficult to interpret or compare across different datasets.

For this reason, covariance is rarely used directly when measuring similarity in recommender systems. Instead, it serves as the foundation for a more interpretable measure: **correlation**.

---

## Correlation

**Correlation** is a standardized version of covariance. Unlike covariance, its value is always constrained to the interval

$$
-1 \leq r \leq 1  
$$

where (r) denotes the correlation coefficient.

Because correlation is independent of the units and scale of the data, it is much easier to interpret and compare across different variables.

The most commonly used correlation measure is the **Pearson correlation coefficient**, which measures the strength and direction of a **linear relationship** between two variables.

The Pearson correlation coefficient can be interpreted as follows:

- **+1** indicates a perfect positive linear relationship. Whenever one variable increases, the other increases proportionally.
    
- **−1** indicates a perfect negative linear relationship. Whenever one variable increases, the other decreases proportionally.
    
- **0** indicates no linear relationship between the variables.
    

Most real-world datasets produce values somewhere between these extremes. As the coefficient moves closer to either +1 or −1, the linear relationship becomes stronger.

---

## Correlation in Recommender Systems

Correlation plays a central role in many recommendation algorithms, particularly **collaborative filtering** methods.

In **user-based collaborative filtering**, recommendations are generated by identifying users with similar preferences. One common way to measure this similarity is by computing the Pearson correlation between users' rating vectors. Users with high positive correlations tend to rate the same items similarly and therefore are likely to have similar tastes.

The same principle applies to **item-based collaborative filtering**. Instead of comparing users, the algorithm compares items based on how users have rated them. Items with high positive correlations are often liked by the same users and can therefore be recommended together.

Because of its intuitive interpretation and scale independence, Pearson correlation has become one of the most widely used similarity measures in recommender systems.

---

## Interpreting the Correlation Matrix

A **correlation matrix** reports the correlation coefficient for every pair of variables in the dataset. Since correlation is symmetric, the matrix is also symmetric; consequently, the correlation between `user_1` and `user_3` is identical to the correlation between `user_3` and `user_1`.

For example, the correlation between **user_1** and **user_3** is **−0.5**. This indicates a moderate negative linear relationship between their rating patterns. In general, when one user assigns relatively higher ratings to an item, the other tends to assign relatively lower ratings.

The diagonal entries of the matrix are always equal to **1** because each variable is perfectly correlated with itself. This serves as a useful sanity check when interpreting correlation matrices.

In practice, recommender systems use matrices like this to identify similar users or similar items. Users with high positive correlations are likely to share common preferences, making them valuable sources for collaborative recommendations.

## Covariance vs. Correlation

Although covariance and correlation are closely related, they serve different purposes. The table below summarizes their key differences.

| Feature                 | Covariance                                                                             | Correlation                                                                                  |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Value range**         | ($-\infty$) to ($+\infty$)                                                             | (-1) to (+1)                                                                                 |
| **Units**               | Depends on the units of the variables                                                  | Unitless (normalized)                                                                        |
| **Interpretation**      | Indicates the direction of joint variation but is difficult to compare across datasets | Indicates both the strength and direction of a linear relationship and is easy to interpret  |
| **Dependence on scale** | Yes                                                                                    | No                                                                                           |
| **Common applications** | Intermediate statistical analysis                                                      | Similarity measurement, machine learning, recommender systems, and exploratory data analysis |

Because of its normalized scale and intuitive interpretation, **correlation is almost always preferred over covariance when measuring similarity in recommender systems**.

# Sampling

As recommender systems grow, so do their datasets. Modern recommendation platforms often contain millions of users, millions of items, and billions of recorded interactions. While these large datasets enable highly accurate recommendation models, they also introduce practical challenges. Analyzing or experimenting with the entire dataset can become computationally expensive, time-consuming, and, in many cases, unnecessary.

Fortunately, many exploratory analyses and early-stage experiments do not require access to the complete dataset. Instead, they can be performed on a carefully selected subset that accurately reflects the characteristics of the original data. This process is known as **sampling**.

---

## What Is Sampling?

**Sampling** is the process of selecting a subset of observations from a larger dataset. The selected subset, called a **sample**, should represent the original dataset as faithfully as possible.

If the sample accurately captures the statistical properties of the full dataset, conclusions drawn from the sample are often applicable to the entire population while requiring significantly less computational effort.

Depending on the application, sampling can be performed using various strategies. The most common approaches include:

- **Random sampling**, where observations are selected completely at random.
    
- **Stratified sampling**, where the dataset is divided into groups (strata), and samples are drawn proportionally from each group.
    
- **Systematic sampling**, where observations are selected at regular intervals.
    
- **Cluster sampling**, where entire groups of observations are selected instead of individual records.
    

Among these techniques, **random sampling** is the simplest and one of the most frequently used methods during exploratory data analysis.

---

## Why Is Sampling Important in Recommender Systems?

Sampling is particularly valuable in recommender systems because interaction datasets are typically enormous. Working directly with the complete dataset during model development can require substantial computational resources and significantly increase experimentation time.

Instead, developers often begin by working with a representative sample. This approach offers several practical advantages:

- Algorithms can be implemented and debugged more quickly.
    
- Experimental results can be obtained in a fraction of the time.
    
- Less memory and computational power are required.
    
- Multiple modeling approaches can be evaluated before training on the complete dataset.
    

Once the algorithm has been validated on the sampled data, it can be applied to the full dataset with greater confidence.

Sampling therefore plays an important role throughout the development lifecycle of a recommender system, from exploratory data analysis to algorithm prototyping and performance evaluation.

---

## Random Sampling with Pandas

Pandas provides a convenient method called `sample()` for selecting random observations from a DataFrame.

Suppose we want to draw a small random sample from the `ratings_df` dataset introduced earlier. The `sample()` method supports two common sampling strategies:

- selecting a fixed number of rows, and
    
- selecting a fixed fraction of the dataset.
    

---

### Sampling a Fixed Number of Rows

To randomly select exactly three observations, we specify the `n` parameter.

```python
random_sample_n = ratings_df.sample(n=3, random_state=42)

print("Random sample of 3 rows:")
print(random_sample_n)
```

**Output**

```text
Random sample of 3 rows:

    user_id  item_id  rating
15        5      102       4
19        5      108       5
27        2      104       5
```

The parameter `n=3` instructs Pandas to return exactly three randomly selected rows.

The `random_state` parameter specifies the seed of the random number generator. Setting a fixed seed ensures that the same sample is produced every time the code is executed, making experiments reproducible.

---

### Sampling a Fraction of the Dataset

Instead of specifying the number of observations, we can request a percentage of the dataset using the `frac` parameter.

The following example randomly selects **50%** of the dataset.

```python
random_sample_frac = ratings_df.sample(frac=0.5, random_state=42)

print("Random sample of 50% of rows:")
print(random_sample_frac)
```

**Output**

```text
Random sample of 50% of rows:

    user_id  item_id  rating
15        5      102       4
19        5      108       5
27        2      104       5
26        1      106       4
8         3      103       4
24        1      103       2
21        6      101       3
12        4      101       1
32        5      106       5
9         3      104       3
0         1      101       5
4         2      103       1
16        5      103       5
17        5      105       4
5         2      106       4
13        4      104       4
```

Here, `frac=0.5` instructs Pandas to randomly select **half of the observations** in the DataFrame.

As before, the fixed `random_state` guarantees that the same subset will be generated whenever the code is executed.

---

## Common Parameters of `DataFrame.sample()`

The `sample()` method provides several useful parameters for controlling the sampling process.

|Parameter|Description|
|---|---|
|`n`|Specifies the exact number of rows to sample. Cannot be used together with `frac`.|
|`frac`|Specifies the fraction of the dataset to sample (e.g., `0.2` for 20%).|
|`replace`|Determines whether sampling is performed with replacement. The default value is `False`, meaning each row can be selected only once.|
|`weights`|Assigns sampling probabilities to individual rows, allowing some observations to be selected more frequently than others.|
|`random_state`|Sets the random seed to ensure reproducible sampling results.|
|`axis`|Specifies whether rows (`axis=0`) or columns (`axis=1`) should be sampled.|

---

## Choosing an Appropriate Sampling Strategy

Selecting an appropriate sampling strategy depends on the characteristics of the dataset and the goals of the analysis.

For exploratory analysis, **simple random sampling** is usually sufficient because it is easy to implement and generally provides a representative subset when the dataset is reasonably balanced.

However, many recommender system datasets exhibit significant imbalance. For example:

- a small number of users may contribute the majority of ratings,
    
- a few popular items may account for most user interactions,
    
- certain rating values may occur far more frequently than others.
    

In these situations, simple random sampling may fail to preserve important characteristics of the original dataset. More advanced techniques, such as **stratified sampling**, can produce samples that better reflect the underlying data distribution.

The choice of sampling method should therefore consider both the structure of the dataset and the objectives of the analysis.
