### 1.3.2 Defining a Function to Count Users Within Age Ranges

To calculate the number of users belonging to a specified age range, we define a function named `get_group_count()`.

```python
def get_group_count(min_age, max_age, dataset):
    age_group = dataset.apply(lambda x: True if max_age > x['Alder'] > min_age else False, axis=1)
    count = len(age_group[age_group == True].index)
    return count
```

The function accepts three arguments:

- `min_age`: The lower boundary of the age range.
- `max_age`: The upper boundary of the age range.
- `dataset`: The DataFrame containing the relevant user records.

The expression:

```python
max_age > x['Alder'] > min_age
```

checks whether the user's recorded age value lies strictly between the specified boundaries.

Mathematically, the condition is:

$$
a_{\min}<a<a_{\max}
$$

Where:

- $a$ is the recorded age value.
- $a_{\min}$ is the lower boundary.
- $a_{\max}$ is the upper boundary.

The `.apply()` method evaluates this condition for each row of the DataFrame.

The resulting Boolean values identify records that satisfy the condition.

Finally, the function counts the matching records and returns the result.

Because the comparisons use strict inequalities, observations equal to either boundary are excluded.

The subsequent calls select overlapping numerical boundaries deliberately so that the intended discrete age values are included.

## Predicting Ratings Versus Positive Interactions

At the beginning of the project, we assume that the recommendation system aims to predict the numerical ratings users would assign to movies.

For example, a model might predict that a user would rate a particular movie:

- 3.5 out of 5.
- 4.2 out of 5.
- 4.8 out of 5.

This is a **rating prediction task**, commonly evaluated using regression-oriented metrics such as RMSE or MAE.

However, not all recommender systems aim to predict exact ratings.

Some systems instead attempt to identify items with which users are likely to have positive interactions.

This creates an alternative formulation in which ratings are converted into binary relevance labels.

For example, a rating of 4 or 5 may be interpreted as a positive interaction.

Under this definition:

$$
y_{u,i}=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if }R_{u,i}\ge4\\0 & \text{if }R_{u,i}<4\end{cases}
$$

Where:

- $R_{u,i}$ is the original rating.
- $y_{u,i}$ is the binary relevance label.
- A value of 1 indicates a positive observed interaction.
- A value of 0 indicates an observed interaction that does not satisfy the positive-rating threshold.

This distinction is important because it changes the objective of the recommendation system.

A rating prediction model attempts to estimate a numerical preference value.

A relevance prediction or ranking model instead attempts to identify and prioritize items likely to satisfy the user's interests.

### Selecting a Positive-Interaction Threshold

The threshold used to define positive interactions can significantly affect the available training data.

For example, consider two possible definitions.

**Strict Threshold**

Only ratings of 5 are considered positive:

$$
y_{u,i}=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if }R_{u,i}=5\\0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

This definition identifies only the strongest positive ratings.

However, it may exclude many ratings of 4 that also indicate user satisfaction.

**Less Restrictive Threshold**

Ratings of 4 and 5 are considered positive:

$$
y_{u,i}=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if }R_{u,i}\ge4\\0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

This retains a larger number of positive observations.

A less restrictive threshold may provide more training examples while still focusing on relatively favorable user evaluations.

However, increasing the number of positive labels does not necessarily guarantee better model performance.

The appropriate threshold depends on the dataset, application objectives, and evaluation methodology.

Therefore, the choice should be investigated experimentally.

For example, a useful experiment would compare recommendation models trained using:

- Ratings of 5 as positive interactions.
- Ratings of 4 or higher as positive interactions.

The models could then be evaluated using the same validation procedure to determine how the selected threshold affects recommendation quality.

An important consideration is that **missing interactions must not automatically be treated as observed negative ratings**.

A user may not have interacted with an item simply because they have never encountered it.

This distinction becomes particularly important when converting explicit ratings into implicit relevance signals.

### Calculating Total Ratings per Genre

Next, we define a function to calculate the total number of rating observations associated with each genre.

```python
# Function for extracting the total nr of ratings for all genres
def get_total_rating_count():
    all_ratings = []
    totals = []
    for count in genres_rating_count:
        all_ratings.append(count[2])

    lower = 0
    for i in range(lower, len(all_ratings), 5):
        lower = i
        totals.append(sum(all_ratings[lower:lower+5]))

    return totals
```

The function first collects all rating counts into a single list.

Because the counts are stored in groups of five, corresponding to ratings 1 through 5 for each genre, the function sums every consecutive group of five values.

The result is a list containing the total number of ratings associated with each genre.

Mathematically, for a genre $g$, the total rating count is:

$$
N_{g}=\sum_{r=1}^{5}n_{g,r}
$$

Where:

- $N_g$ is the total number of ratings associated with genre $g$.
- $n_{g,r}$ is the number of ratings equal to $r$ for genre $g$.

Because a movie may belong to multiple genres, a single rating observation can contribute to the total counts of several genres.

Therefore, the sum of rating counts across all genres may exceed the total number of ratings in the dataset.

### Calculating the Average Rating for Each Genre

The average rating for a genre is calculated using a weighted arithmetic mean.

The formula is:

$$
\bar{R}_{g}=\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{5}r\cdot n_{g,r}}{\sum_{r=1}^{5}n_{g,r}}
$$

Where:

- $\bar{R}_g$ is the average rating associated with genre $g$.
- $r$ is a rating value between 1 and 5.
- $n_{g,r}$ is the number of ratings equal to $r$ within genre $g$.

Expanding the numerator gives:

$$
\bar{R}_{g}=\frac{1n_{g,1}+2n_{g,2}+3n_{g,3}+4n_{g,4}+5n_{g,5}}{N_g}
$$

This formula assigns each rating value a weight equal to its frequency.

For example, a genre with many ratings of 4 and 5 will generally have a higher average rating than a genre dominated by ratings of 1 and 2.

Although these quantities are related, they describe different aspects of the dataset.

A content-based recommender can use genre metadata to identify movies matching a user's interests, while collaborative filtering can exploit observed rating patterns to estimate preferences.

Combining these two sources of information provides a foundation for the hybrid recommendation model developed in subsequent phases.

### Relationship Between Genre Frequency and Average Ratings

Genres with many ratings may exhibit relatively stable estimates of their average rating because they are supported by larger numbers of observations.

For example, Action, Comedy, and Drama have substantial rating counts, and their average ratings lie reasonably close to the overall dataset mean.

However, a larger sample size does not mathematically guarantee that a genre's average rating will converge to the overall average across all genres.

Instead, with sufficient representative observations, the estimated mean for a particular genre tends toward that genre's underlying expected rating.

This distinction is important because different genres may have genuinely different rating distributions.

Less frequently rated genres, such as Western, Documentary, and Film-Noir, may also exhibit relatively high or low average ratings.

These differences can influence how frequently items from those genres appear in recommendations, especially when recommendation algorithms incorporate popularity or average-rating information.

Nevertheless, ranking items exclusively by genre-level average ratings would overlook individual user preferences and potentially favor genres with concentrated audiences.

A personalized recommender should therefore consider both the statistical properties of genre-level ratings and the preferences of the target user.
