# Beyond Simple Hybridization: The Need for Sequential Recommender Systems (Further Reading)

In previous chapters and sections, we examined several recommendation approaches, including **Content-Based Filtering**, which generates recommendations based on a user's explicitly or implicitly expressed preferences, and **Collaborative Filtering**, which exploits behavioral similarities among users or items.

We also explored how hybridization strategies, such as **Switching Hybrid Systems**, which select one recommendation algorithm according to the current circumstances, and **Weighted Hybrid Systems**, which combine predictions from multiple algorithms using different weights, can improve recommendation quality.

However, the digital environment is continuously evolving, and increasingly sophisticated recommendation approaches are required to provide accurate, personalized, and contextually relevant recommendations that respond to users' immediate behavior.

This section provides a conceptual comparison between the hybrid recommendation approaches discussed previously and more advanced recommendation architectures that will be examined in subsequent chapters.

One important limitation of many traditional recommendation models, including conventional hybrid models, is that they do not explicitly consider the **order in which user interactions occur**.

For example, a conventional collaborative filtering model may recognize that a user has interacted with several products without distinguishing whether the user purchased a smartphone before or after purchasing its accessories.

Similarly, a content-based model may identify similarities between television episodes without explicitly representing the order in which those episodes should be watched.

This limitation motivates a more advanced approach known as **Sequential Recommender Systems (SRS)**.

Sequential recommendation models explicitly represent the order of user interactions and exploit dependencies between successive actions to predict what a user is likely to interact with next.

To understand why sequential information matters, consider the following scenarios.

## Motivating Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Intelligent Shopping Recommendations

Ali decides to purchase a new smartphone.

He spends several hours exploring online marketplaces, such as Digikala and Torob, comparing different smartphone models and examining their specifications.

After selecting and purchasing a smartphone, the online store immediately recommends related products, such as:

- A protective phone case.
- A screen protector.
- Wireless headphones.

These recommendations are particularly relevant because of the action Ali has just completed.

The order of events is essential:

1. Ali searches for and compares smartphones.
2. He selects and purchases a smartphone.
3. The system recommends compatible accessories.

A recommendation for a protective case becomes more meaningful after the smartphone purchase than before it.

The system therefore benefits from recognizing not only the user's general interest in electronics but also the **sequence of actions leading to the current recommendation request**.

### Scenario 2: Continuous Television Series Viewing

Mohammad hears positive reviews of the television series *The Lion Skin (Poost-e Shir)* from his friends and decides to watch it on Filimo.

After finishing the first episode, the platform immediately recommends the second episode.

In this situation, the ordering of interactions is fundamental.

The platform must recognize that Mohammad has completed Episode 1 and that Episode 2 is the logical next item in the viewing sequence.

Simply knowing that Mohammad enjoys television series would not provide sufficient information to determine the most appropriate next recommendation.

Instead, the system must incorporate the user's most recent interaction and the sequential relationship between episodes.

### Why Does Interaction Order Matter?

In both scenarios, the recommendations depend on more than general preferences or item characteristics.

They depend on the **temporal and sequential relationships between user actions**.

In the first scenario, purchasing a smartphone creates a potential need for compatible accessories.

In the second scenario, completing one episode establishes a natural progression toward the following episode.

These examples demonstrate that the sequence of previous interactions provides valuable information about a user's immediate needs.

Traditional recommendation models often summarize user behavior as a collection of interactions without explicitly preserving their order.

Sequential recommendation models, in contrast, attempt to understand:

- Which actions occurred previously.
- The order in which those actions occurred.
- How earlier interactions influence subsequent decisions.
- Which action or item is most likely to follow the current interaction sequence.

Thus, sequential recommendation focuses not only on **what users prefer**, but also on **what users are likely to prefer next, given what they have already done**.

This principle forms the foundation of Sequential Recommender Systems.

## Sequential Recommender Systems (SRS)

**Sequential Recommender Systems (SRS)** are recommendation models that predict users' future interactions by learning sequential dependencies among their previous user–item interactions.

These interactions may include:

- Viewing an item.
- Clicking a product.
- Purchasing an item.
- Watching a video.
- Listening to a song.
- Rating or liking content.

Rather than treating these interactions as independent observations, sequential recommendation models represent them as ordered sequences.

The primary objective is to predict the next item, or a set of likely future items, based on the user's historical interaction sequence.

For example, consider a user who performs the following actions:

**Search for smartphones → View smartphone specifications → Purchase a smartphone → Purchase a protective case**

A sequential recommendation model attempts to learn the relationships between these actions and use them to anticipate the user's next likely interaction.

### Representing User Interaction Sequences

A user's interaction history can be represented as an ordered sequence:

$$
S_{u}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
$$

Where:

- $S_{u}$ represents the interaction sequence of user $u$.
- $i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t}$ represent the items with which the user has interacted.
- $t$ represents the number of observed interactions in the sequence.
- The position of each item indicates its order within the interaction history.

The goal of a sequential recommender system is to estimate the probability that the user will interact with a particular item next:

$$
P(i_{t+1}=i\mid i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
$$

This expression represents the probability that item $i$ will be the user's next interaction, given the sequence of previous interactions.

A recommendation can then be generated by selecting an item with a high predicted probability:

$$
\hat{i}_{t+1}=\arg\max_{i\in I}P(i_{t+1}=i\mid S_{u})
$$

Where:

- $I$ represents the set of candidate items.
- $\hat{i}_{t+1}$ is the predicted next item.
- $P(i_{t+1}=i\mid S_{u})$ represents the estimated probability of the next interaction involving item $i$.

This formulation illustrates the fundamental difference between sequential recommendation and traditional preference prediction.

A conventional model may estimate whether a user generally likes an item, whereas a sequential model estimates whether that item is appropriate **at the current position in the user's interaction sequence**.

### Sequential Dependencies in User Behavior

User–item interactions are often dynamic and sequentially dependent rather than static and independent.

For example, purchasing a smartphone may increase the likelihood of purchasing compatible accessories shortly afterward.

Similarly, watching the first episode of a series may increase the likelihood of watching its second episode.

Sequential recommendation models attempt to capture these dependencies by learning patterns across ordered interactions.

They may also model broader behavioral characteristics, including:

- Sequential relationships between items.
- Dependencies between earlier and later user actions.
- Changes in user preferences over time.
- Changes in item popularity.
- Relationships between user behavior and the surrounding context.

Consider another example involving travel planning.

Suppose Ahmad performs the following actions:

1. Books a flight.
2. Reserves a hotel.
3. Rents a car.

What is he likely to do next?

Depending on his destination and travel preferences, the next relevant recommendation might involve local attractions, travel insurance, or activities at the destination.

The system can use the sequence of completed reservations to infer the user's current stage in the travel-planning process.

Similarly, consider Amir, an enthusiastic user of Apple products, who purchases an iPhone, an Apple Watch, and AirPods.

A sequential recommender can examine the ordering of these purchases and identify patterns indicating which complementary products users with similar purchase sequences tend to acquire next.

These examples illustrate how subsequent actions can depend on earlier interactions within an ordered behavioral sequence.

## Key Characteristics of Sequential Recommender Systems

Sequential recommendation differs from conventional content-based and collaborative filtering in several important ways.

These differences primarily concern how models represent user behavior, interpret context, capture changing preferences, and generate personalized predictions.

### 1. Dynamic Representation of User Behavior

Traditional recommendation approaches often construct a relatively static representation of user preferences.

For example, a collaborative filtering model may represent a user through previously assigned ratings or implicit interaction values.

Similarly, a content-based model may construct a profile describing the user's preferred genres, categories, or attributes.

Such representations provide useful information about general preferences, but many conventional implementations do not explicitly preserve the order of interactions.

They effectively describe a **snapshot of user preferences**.

Sequential recommendation models, however, treat user behavior as an evolving process.

Rather than examining only which items the user has interacted with, they consider the order in which those interactions occurred.

This is analogous to replacing a static photograph with a continuous recording of user activity.

For example, a sequential model may observe that a user:

1. Watches a movie trailer.
2. Searches for the movie.
3. Opens the movie's information page.
4. Watches the movie.
5. Searches for related content.

Each action contributes information about the user's developing interests and intentions.

The model attempts to determine which interaction is likely to occur after the most recently observed actions.

#### Why Is This Useful?

Human decisions are frequently influenced by recent experiences, current needs, and ongoing activities.

For example, a user's immediate interests while planning a vacation may differ substantially from their long-term entertainment preferences.

By modeling interaction order, sequential recommenders can capture these changes and generate recommendations that are more appropriate to the user's current activity.

### 2. Contextual Awareness

Sequential recommender systems can use previous interactions to infer the user's current behavioral context.

For example, actions such as liking a post, watching a movie, or purchasing a product can provide clues about the user's immediate interests.

Suppose a user has just finished watching the first episode of a television series.

The recent interaction indicates that the user may currently be interested in continuing that series.

A sequential recommendation model can use this information to prioritize the next episode.

A conventional recommendation model that ignores interaction order may know only that the user generally enjoys television series.

It may therefore recommend an unrelated series that matches the user's overall preferences rather than the next episode of the current series.

Sequential models can produce more contextually appropriate recommendations because they incorporate the progression of recent interactions.

However, it is important to distinguish **sequential context** from broader contextual information.

Sequential context primarily describes the user's recent interaction history, while broader contextual variables may include time, location, device type, and other environmental conditions.

Advanced sequential recommendation architectures may integrate both types of information.

### 3. Modeling the Evolution of User Preferences

User preferences are not necessarily constant over time.

A user may prefer action movies during one period and gradually develop a stronger interest in comedy.

Similarly, a customer who previously purchased electronic devices may later become interested in home furnishings or fitness equipment.

This phenomenon is known as **preference evolution**.

Sequential recommendation models are designed to capture such changes by analyzing how user interactions develop over time.

Rather than assuming that earlier preferences remain equally relevant indefinitely, these models can assign different importance to interactions according to their position in the sequence.

Recent interactions may be particularly informative when predicting immediate needs.

However, older interactions may still reveal stable long-term preferences.

An effective sequential recommendation model should therefore distinguish between:

- **Long-term preferences:** Relatively stable interests that persist across extended periods.
- **Short-term preferences:** Temporary interests associated with recent actions, immediate needs, or specific sessions.

Sequential models can also capture changes in item popularity when appropriate temporal information is available.

For example, interest in a particular product category may increase during a seasonal event or following a new product release.

By incorporating temporal and sequential information, the model can adapt recommendations as behavioral patterns evolve.

### 4. Improved Personalization and Recommendation Relevance

By incorporating interaction order, recent behavioral context, and evolving preferences, sequential recommenders can generate more contextually relevant predictions.

Their potential advantages include:

**Higher Recommendation Accuracy**

Sequential models can identify behavioral dependencies that may be missed by methods that treat interactions as unordered observations.

When these dependencies are informative, incorporating them can improve prediction accuracy.

**More Personalized Recommendations**

Sequential models can respond to a user's current activities rather than relying exclusively on long-term preference profiles.

This allows recommendations to reflect immediate interests and changing needs.

**Greater Adaptability**

As new interactions occur, the user's sequence can be updated, allowing the model to adjust its predictions.

For example, a user who begins exploring an unfamiliar product category may receive recommendations related to that category even if it does not dominate their historical interaction profile.

These benefits are especially valuable in applications where interaction order strongly influences future behavior.

Nevertheless, sequential recommendation does not automatically outperform traditional approaches in every application.

Its effectiveness depends on the availability of meaningful sequential patterns, the quality of interaction data, and the suitability of the selected model architecture.

## Challenges of Sequential Recommender Systems

Despite their advantages, sequential recommendation models introduce several technical and methodological challenges.

These challenges arise from the need to represent ordered interactions, learn dependencies across different time scales, and process potentially large volumes of behavioral data.

### 1. Modeling Long Interaction Sequences

Users may accumulate extensive interaction histories over months or years.

For example, a video streaming platform may record thousands of viewing events for a single user.

Similarly, an e-commerce platform may contain many years of searches, product views, and purchases.

These interactions form potentially long sequences.

As sequence length increases, learning meaningful relationships between earlier and later actions becomes more difficult.

The model must determine which historical interactions remain relevant and which provide little useful information for predicting the next item.

Long sequences may also contain multiple unrelated interests, making it difficult to identify the behavioral patterns that matter most.

Processing these sequences can require substantial computational resources.

The exact computational cost depends on the model architecture.

For example, recurrent neural networks process sequential information through recurrent state updates, whereas standard self-attention mechanisms can require computation and memory that grow quadratically with sequence length.

Consequently, efficiently processing long user histories remains an important challenge in sequential recommendation.

### 2. Learning Long-Range Behavioral Dependencies

Not all meaningful relationships occur between consecutive interactions.

Sometimes an earlier action influences another action much later in the sequence.

For example, a user may purchase a flowerpot and, several months later, order a rose plant.

Between these two purchases, the user may have interacted with many unrelated products.

A sequential recommender must determine whether the earlier flowerpot purchase contains useful information for predicting the later purchase.

This is an example of a **long-range dependency**.

Long-range dependencies are difficult to identify because many intermediate interactions may separate related events.

Furthermore, behavioral relationships are not always obvious or deterministic.

Purchasing a flowerpot does not guarantee that a user will eventually purchase flowers.

The model must instead learn whether such patterns occur frequently enough to provide useful predictive information.

#### Advanced Models for Long-Range Dependencies

More sophisticated sequential learning architectures have been developed to address these challenges.

One important family consists of **Transformer-based models**.

Transformers use attention mechanisms to identify relationships between different positions in a sequence.

In particular, self-attention allows the model to evaluate the relevance of earlier interactions when constructing representations of the current sequence.

This can help the model identify important historical actions even when they are separated by many intermediate events.

Transformers also form the architectural foundation of many modern large language models, including the GPT family.

Their ability to model dependencies across sequences has contributed to their adoption in sequential recommendation.

However, attention-based architectures introduce their own computational challenges, especially for long sequences.

Effective modeling also requires careful data preprocessing and appropriate representations of items, interaction positions, and temporal information.

### 3. Integrating Side Information and Complex Behavioral Structures

User behavior contains more information than the identities of interacted items.

A recommendation system may also have access to additional attributes, commonly called **side information**.

Examples include:

- User age or demographic characteristics.
- Geographic location.
- Item categories and descriptive features.
- Movie genres.
- Product specifications.
- Interaction timestamps.
- Session boundaries.
- Different interaction types, such as clicks, purchases, and ratings.

These additional attributes may help explain why users interact with particular items.

For example, a user may browse products during one session without intending to purchase anything, while another session may reflect immediate purchasing intent.

The same item interaction can therefore have different meanings depending on the surrounding behavior.

Moreover, interactions may be connected across multiple sessions.

A user might investigate a product during one session and purchase it several days later.

A sequential recommendation model must decide how to incorporate these relationships while preserving relevant ordering information.

Integrating heterogeneous side information and complex cross-session dependencies increases both model complexity and data preparation requirements.

### 4. Data Sparsity in Interaction Sequences

Sequential recommendation systems can exploit behavioral information that conventional unordered approaches may overlook.

However, they are not immune to **data sparsity**.

Some users have very short interaction histories, while others interact with many unrelated items.

Even long sequences may contain sparse or weakly connected behavioral patterns.

For example, a user may interact with one product category only occasionally, making it difficult to learn meaningful sequential relationships within that category.

Sparse sequential data can also make it difficult to estimate long-term preferences reliably.

The problem becomes particularly challenging when the number of possible items is large relative to the number of observed interactions.

As a result, sequential models may benefit from additional content features, collaborative information, or other sources of side information.

This creates opportunities to combine sequential recommendation with the hybridization strategies introduced earlier.

### 5. Identifying Immediate User Intent

In some recommendation scenarios, the system must infer a user's current interests from only a few recent interactions.

For example, a recommendation request may follow just three clicks.

These interactions may represent a temporary intention that differs from the user's historical preferences.

Suppose a user who usually purchases books begins searching for smartphone accessories.

The system must determine whether these recent actions indicate a genuine short-term purchasing intent or merely casual exploration.

This is the challenge of modeling **short-term preferences**.

Short-term interests may persist for only minutes or hours and may change rapidly as users move between tasks.

Accurately identifying these interests from limited observations is difficult because a small number of actions may not provide sufficient evidence.

Sequential recommendation models must therefore balance recent behavioral signals against more stable historical preferences.

This balance is especially important in session-based recommendation, where predictions are often generated from a short sequence of interactions within a single session.

### 6. High Computational Requirements

Advanced sequential recommendation models may require significant computational resources during training and inference.

The computational burden can arise from several factors:

- Large numbers of users and items.
- Long interaction sequences.
- High-dimensional item representations.
- Complex neural network architectures.
- Attention mechanisms that process relationships between sequence positions.
- The need to update recommendations as new interactions arrive.

Training models that capture complex sequential patterns may require substantial processing time and memory.

In production environments, inference latency is another important consideration.

A recommender system may need to generate predictions quickly while serving many concurrent users.

Consequently, practical sequential recommendation requires careful architectural design, efficient data processing, and suitable optimization techniques.

The computational cost also depends on the complexity of the selected method.

Not every sequential recommender requires a large neural network. Simpler approaches, such as Markov-chain models or item-transition methods, may provide effective and computationally efficient alternatives when the underlying sequential patterns are relatively simple.

More sophisticated architectures become particularly valuable when the application requires modeling complex dependencies, changing preferences, or long behavioral histories.
