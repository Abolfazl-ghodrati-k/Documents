In the previous lesson, we introduced the concept of **Collaborative Filtering (CF)**, one of the fundamental approaches used in recommender systems. Collaborative filtering leverages user interaction data—such as ratings, purchases, or clicks—to recommend items that a user is likely to find interesting. It can be broadly categorized into two major approaches:

- **User-Based Collaborative Filtering (UBCF)**
- **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering (IBCF)**

Imagine that there are two bookstores on the same street.

The first store, **Ehsan's Books**, is run by a bookseller named Ehsan, who has an extraordinary memory for remembering customers and their reading preferences.

When you enter the store, he says:

> "You have very similar tastes to Mr. Azizi, who visited last week. He bought _One Hundred Years of Solitude_, _Crime and Punishment_, and _The Alchemist_, but he did not enjoy _The Little Prince_. Since you also liked _One Hundred Years of Solitude_ and _Crime and Punishment_, you'll probably enjoy _The Alchemist_ as well."

This is an example of **User-Based Collaborative Filtering**. The system first identifies users who have preferences similar to yours and then recommends items that those similar users enjoyed.

At the other end of the street is another bookstore called **World of Books**, managed by Ms. Saeidi.

When you visit her store, she says:

> "I see that you purchased _One Hundred Years of Solitude_. Customers who bought this book also tended to enjoy _Love in the Time of Cholera_. The two books have similar characteristics, and purchasing patterns show that they are frequently bought together."

This illustrates **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering**. Instead of searching for users with similar preferences, the system identifies items similar to those you have already liked and recommends those similar items.

# User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering

## User-Based Collaborative Filtering (UBCF)

In **User-Based Collaborative Filtering**, the recommender system first identifies users who are similar to the target user. These similar users are commonly referred to as **nearest neighbors**. The system then recommends items that these neighbors have enjoyed but that the target user has not yet interacted with.

Imagine walking into a large bookstore looking for a new book to read. Who would provide the most useful recommendation? Most likely, someone whose reading preferences closely resemble your own.

The basic User-Based Collaborative Filtering algorithm consists of the following steps:

1. **Compute user similarity.**  
    Calculate the similarity between every pair of users.
2. **Construct the neighborhood.**  
    Identify the set of users that are most similar to the target user.
3. **Predict ratings.**  
    Estimate the target user's rating for items they have not yet evaluated.
4. **Generate recommendations.**  
    Recommend the items with the highest predicted ratings.

### Advantages of User-Based Collaborative Filtering

- **Interpretability**  
    Recommendations are easy to explain:
    "Users with preferences similar to yours liked this item, so we recommend it to you."
- **Serendipity**  
    User-based methods can recommend items that may not be content-wise similar to what the  user has previously consumed, yet are still highly relevant because users with similar overall tastes discovered and appreciated them. This often leads to pleasantly unexpected recommendations. A well-known example is **Spotify**, whose recommendations frequently introduce users to artists or genres they would not have found through simple content similarity.
- **Easy adaptation for new users**  
    As soon as a new user performs a few initial interactions, the system can identify similar users and begin generating recommendations.
- **Higher recommendation accuracy**  
    In many practical applications, User-Based Collaborative Filtering generally achieves better recommendation accuracy than pure content-based filtering.


## Item-Based Collaborative Filtering (IBCF)

In **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering**, the recommender system searches for **similar items** rather than similar users. The similarity between items is computed based on user interaction patterns. After identifying items that are similar to those a user has previously liked, the system recommends these similar items to the user.

This approach was introduced by **Amazon in 1998** and became widely adopted because of its excellent scalability, particularly in systems with very large user populations.

The objective of Item-Based Collaborative Filtering is to identify items that are _similar_ to the items the current user has already enjoyed. Item similarity is determined from the historical interactions of many users. In other words, if a large number of users have rated, purchased, or consumed two items together, those items are considered similar. The recommender system then suggests items that are similar to the user's previously preferred items.

The Item-Based Collaborative Filtering algorithm consists of the following steps:

1. **Compute item similarity.**  
    Calculate the similarity between every pair of items based on user interactions.
2. **Build the item similarity model.**  
    Construct an item-to-item similarity matrix.
3. **Predict ratings.**  
    Estimate the target user's rating for items they have not yet evaluated by using the user's ratings on similar items.
4. **Generate recommendations.**  
    Recommend the items with the highest predicted ratings.

### Advantages of Item-Based Collaborative Filtering

- **Better scalability for systems with many users**
    
    Although constructing the item-item similarity matrix can be computationally expensive, it is typically performed only once—or updated periodically as an offline process. Recommendation generation is then extremely efficient because the system only needs to examine the items the user already likes and retrieve their most similar items.
    
    This property makes Item-Based Collaborative Filtering particularly suitable for platforms such as Amazon, which contain millions of users and millions of products. In many applications, the item catalog grows much more slowly than the user base, making offline similarity computation practical.
    
- **Partial resistance to the user cold-start problem**
    
    While completely new users still present a challenge, a user with only a handful of interactions can already receive meaningful recommendations because the system can identify items similar to those few previously consumed.
    
- **Greater model stability**
    
    The similarity between two items—for example, whether _The Matrix_ and _Inception_ are considered similar—tends to remain relatively stable over time. In contrast, user preferences may evolve considerably. As a result, the item similarity matrix requires less frequent reconstruction than a user similarity matrix.
    
- **Explainability**
    
    Recommendations are straightforward to explain:
    
    "Because you liked item **X**, we recommend item **Y**, since users who liked **X** also tended to like  **Y**."
    
    This explanation is equivalent to stating that items **X** and **Y** have a high similarity score.

# Similarities Between User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering

Both User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering share several fundamental characteristics:

- Both rely on historical user-item interaction data, such as ratings, purchases, or clicks, to generate recommendations.
- Both are based on the concept of finding **neighbors**:
    - similar users in User-Based Collaborative Filtering,
    - similar items in Item-Based Collaborative Filtering.
- Both suffer from **data sparsity** and **cold-start** problems.
- Both belong to the family of **memory-based collaborative filtering algorithms** and do not require auxiliary information about users or items, except when incorporated into hybrid recommender systems.

# Differences Between User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering

|Feature|User-Based Collaborative Filtering (UBCF)|Item-Based Collaborative Filtering (IBCF)|
|---|---|---|
|**Core idea**|Find similar users|Find similar items|
|**Similarity basis**|Similarity between user vectors (rows of the user-item matrix)|Similarity between item vectors (columns of the user-item matrix)|
|**Similarity computation cost**|Usually performed online when recommendations are requested; computationally expensive for large user populations|Usually computed offline and updated periodically; expensive only during preprocessing|
|**Prediction cost**|Requires collecting ratings from neighboring users; relatively expensive|Requires examining the user's ratings on similar items; significantly faster|
|**Primary scalability advantage**|Better suited to systems with many items but relatively few users|Better suited to systems with many users and a relatively stable item catalog|
|**User cold-start problem**|More severe, since a new user has no similar neighbors|Less severe, as only a few interactions are sufficient to identify similar items|
|**Item cold-start problem**|Less severe, because new items can gradually appear within user neighborhoods|More severe, because new items initially have no similarity relationships with existing items|
|**Model stability**|Lower, since user preferences evolve over time|Higher, because item relationships are generally more stable|
|**Serendipity**|Greater potential for surprising and unexpected recommendations|More likely to recommend items that are closely related to previously consumed items|
|**Explainability**|"Users similar to you liked this item."|"Because you liked this item, we recommend that item."|
|**Memory requirements**|Stores user similarity information|Stores the item-item similarity matrix, which may become very large|

## Which Approach Is Better?

The choice between User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering depends heavily on the characteristics of the platform and the available data.

The following scenarios illustrate when each approach is most appropriate.

## Scenario 1: A Large E-Commerce Platform (e.g., Amazon or DigiKala)

**Characteristics**

- A very large number of users.
- A large product catalog.
- New products are continuously added.
- User interactions primarily consist of purchases or product views.

In this scenario, the number of users is significantly larger than the number of items, while products are relatively more stable than users. For example, a book or a movie typically remains in the catalog for a long time. Consequently, **scalability with respect to the number of users becomes the primary concern**.

User-Based Collaborative Filtering struggles in such environments because it must compute user similarities in real time for a massive user base. In contrast, Item-Based Collaborative Filtering performs most of its computationally intensive work offline by constructing the item similarity matrix. Once this matrix has been built, generating recommendations is extremely fast.

Although newly added products initially suffer from the item cold-start problem, this limitation can be partially addressed using complementary techniques such as **Content-Based Filtering**.

Therefore, **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering is generally the preferred and most widely used approach for large-scale e-commerce platforms**. The familiar recommendation:

> _"Customers who bought this product also bought..."_

is a classic example of Item-Based Collaborative Filtering.

---

## Scenario 2: Movie or Music Streaming Services (e.g., Netflix or Spotify)

**Characteristics**

- A very large user base.
- A relatively stable catalog of movies, TV shows, or music.
- New content is added periodically.
- User interactions mainly consist of ratings, watching, or listening.

Similar to e-commerce platforms, streaming services have enormous user populations, making scalability a critical requirement.

Since movies and albums remain available for long periods, item similarities tend to be stable over time. This stability makes Item-Based Collaborative Filtering particularly attractive, as the item-item similarity matrix can be computed offline and reused efficiently.

Although newly released movies or albums introduce an item cold-start problem, this issue is generally less severe than in e-commerce, where entirely new products are introduced continuously.

Consequently, **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering remains one of the most popular approaches for streaming platforms** due to its scalability and the stability of item relationships.

However, to improve **serendipity** and achieve deeper personalization, production systems frequently combine Item-Based Collaborative Filtering with hybrid recommender techniques or more advanced model-based methods such as **Matrix Factorization**.

---

## Scenario 3: A Large News Website

**Characteristics**

- A large number of users.
- A huge and rapidly changing collection of news articles.
- New articles are published continuously.
- User interactions mainly consist of clicks and page views.

In this scenario, the primary challenge is the extremely short lifespan of items.

Maintaining an item-item similarity matrix for millions of news articles that are constantly being created and becoming obsolete is computationally expensive and often impractical.

Although User-Based Collaborative Filtering also faces scalability challenges with a large user base, it has one important advantage: recommendations can be generated from the recent reading behavior of similar users, allowing newly published articles to appear in recommendations almost immediately.

Even so, **both pure User-Based and pure Item-Based Collaborative Filtering perform poorly in this environment**.

In practice, news recommendation systems commonly employ:

- **Content-Based Filtering**, which uses article topics and textual content,
- short-term behavioral modeling,
- or **Model-Based Collaborative Filtering** methods such as **Matrix Factorization**, which are better suited to rapidly evolving content.

Therefore, **neither pure UBCF nor pure IBCF is considered an ideal solution for large news recommendation systems.**

---

## Scenario 4: A Small Online Community or Specialized Platform

**Characteristics**

- A relatively small user base.
- A relatively small and stable collection of items.
- Examples include specialized forums or niche online stores.

In this setting, scalability is no longer the dominant issue because both the user base and the item catalog are relatively small.

Instead, the main challenge becomes **data sparsity**.

With only a limited number of users and interactions, computing reliable similarity scores—whether between users or between items—becomes difficult.

User-Based Collaborative Filtering may suffer because there are too few users with sufficient overlap in their interaction histories. Item-Based Collaborative Filtering may exhibit somewhat greater stability because relationships between items generally change less frequently than relationships between users.

As a result, **both approaches are viable candidates**, although **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering often has a slight advantage** due to the greater stability of item similarities and its relative robustness to sparsity along the user dimension.

Nevertheless, sparse interaction data remains the dominant challenge, and hybrid or alternative recommendation techniques are often required.

# Beyond User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering

The User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering algorithms discussed so far belong to the family of **memory-based recommender systems**.

As the name suggests, memory-based methods retain the complete user-item interaction matrix—including ratings, purchases, clicks, or other interactions—and perform similarity calculations directly on these stored data during recommendation generation.

The limitations of User-Based and Item-Based Collaborative Filtering, particularly their scalability issues and sensitivity to sparse data, motivated the development of more advanced techniques known as **model-based recommender systems**.

Unlike memory-based approaches, **model-based methods** do not rely on repeatedly processing the entire interaction matrix during recommendation time.

Instead, they learn a mathematical or statistical model from historical interaction data. This model attempts to uncover **latent factors** that explain user preferences and item characteristics.

Rather than computing similarities between users or items, the learned model predicts how likely a user is to interact with or assign a high rating to an item.

The underlying assumption is that both users and items can be represented by a relatively small number of hidden characteristics.

For example, consider Netflix.

Suppose a user enjoys action films such as _John Wick_ as well as dramatic films such as _The Shawshank Redemption_. A model-based recommender system may infer that this user prefers movies with characteristics such as **high intensity** and **deep storytelling**. It may therefore recommend a film like _The Dark Knight_, even if no particularly similar user explicitly recommended it.

These latent characteristics are learned automatically from interaction data rather than being manually defined.

This is precisely where **machine learning models** and **neural networks** become essential. These techniques will be explored in detail in later chapters.

# Overview of Recommender System Types

Ultimately, **User-Based Collaborative Filtering (UBCF)** and **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering (IBCF)** are the two principal approaches within **memory-based collaborative filtering**. Each has its own strengths and limitations.

User-Based Collaborative Filtering generates recommendations by identifying users with similar preferences, often producing more diverse and serendipitous recommendations. However, its computational cost increases substantially as the number of users grows.

Item-Based Collaborative Filtering, on the other hand, focuses on similarities between items. It offers greater scalability and model stability, making it the preferred approach for large-scale platforms such as Amazon.

Both approaches, however, face significant challenges, including:

- **Data sparsity**, where relatively few user-item interactions are available.
- **The cold-start problem**, involving new users or newly introduced items.

These limitations motivated the development of more advanced **model-based recommender systems**, particularly techniques such as **Matrix Factorization**, which learn latent representations of users and items instead of relying directly on similarity computations.
