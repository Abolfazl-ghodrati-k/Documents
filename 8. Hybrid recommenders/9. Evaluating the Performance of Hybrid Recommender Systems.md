## From Classical Evaluation Metrics to Practical Assessment in Real-World Applications

Consider a recommender system that combines several recommendation techniques to suggest movies, books, or products to users. After developing such a system, an essential question arises: **How effectively does it perform?**

Evaluating a recommender system involves more than determining whether its predictions are numerically accurate. We must also investigate whether the recommendations satisfy users' preferences, provide sufficient diversity, introduce useful new items, and remain effective under challenging conditions such as the cold-start problem.

These considerations are especially important for **Hybrid Recommender Systems**, which integrate multiple recommendation approaches, such as Collaborative Filtering and Content-Based Filtering.

A comprehensive evaluation must therefore examine both the predictive performance of the constituent algorithms and the quality of their combined recommendations.

This section introduces the principal evaluation metrics, experimental methodologies, and practical techniques used to assess hybrid recommender systems.

## Why Is Evaluating Hybrid Recommender Systems Challenging?

Hybrid recommender systems are generally more complex than standalone recommendation models because they integrate multiple algorithms through strategies such as weighted combination, switching, or multi-stage processing.

This additional complexity introduces several evaluation challenges.

### 1. Evaluating Individual Components

Each constituent recommendation algorithm should be evaluated independently.

For example, a hybrid system combining collaborative and content-based filtering should first establish the predictive performance of each component.

This helps identify their individual strengths and weaknesses.

As demonstrated in the preceding implementation sections, evaluating each model before combining its predictions establishes a useful performance baseline.

### 2. Evaluating the Combined System

The final hybrid architecture must also be evaluated as an integrated model.

Even if individual components perform well, their combination does not necessarily produce better recommendations.

For example, two models may make highly correlated prediction errors, limiting the benefits of combining their outputs.

Therefore, the effectiveness of hybridization must be established through direct evaluation of the final recommendations.

### 3. Optimizing Hybridization Parameters

Hybrid architectures often introduce additional parameters that influence recommendation quality.

For example, a weighted hybrid model requires selecting the relative contributions of its component algorithms.

These weights must be optimized using suitable validation data.

Similarly, a switching hybrid requires appropriate decision criteria, while a cascade architecture requires decisions about candidate selection and ranking.

### 4. Evaluating Special Recommendation Scenarios

A hybrid system should also be evaluated under conditions where individual recommendation approaches are known to struggle.

One important example is the **cold-start problem**, which occurs when newly registered users or newly introduced items have insufficient interaction histories.

Other important scenarios include sparse interaction data, rapidly changing user preferences, and uneven item popularity.

A comprehensive evaluation should determine whether hybridization provides measurable advantages under these conditions.

## Key Evaluation Metrics

Recommendation system evaluation can be divided into two broad categories:

1. **Accuracy Metrics**, which measure how well model predictions correspond to observed user preferences.
2. **Beyond-Accuracy Metrics**, which evaluate additional aspects of recommendation quality and user experience.

These categories address different objectives and should generally be considered together.

# Accuracy Metrics

Accuracy metrics assess the correspondence between model predictions and observed user preferences.

The appropriate metric depends on the recommendation task.

Two important task formulations are:

- **Rating Prediction**, where the model predicts numerical ratings.
- **Top-N Recommendation**, where the model ranks candidate items and recommends the most relevant ones.

## 1. Rating Prediction Metrics

### Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

**Mean Absolute Error (MAE)** measures the average absolute difference between predicted ratings and actual ratings.

Its mathematical definition is:

$$
MAE=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|r_i-\hat{r}_i|
$$

Where:

- $n$ is the number of evaluated predictions.
- $r_i$ is the actual rating for observation $i$.
- $\hat{r}_i$ is the corresponding predicted rating.

MAE treats prediction errors according to their absolute magnitude.

For example, an error of 0.5 contributes exactly 0.5 to the error sum, regardless of whether the prediction is above or below the actual rating.

A smaller MAE indicates more accurate predictions.

#### Numerical Example

Suppose a hybrid recommender predicts the following ratings for three movies:

| Movie | Predicted Rating | Actual Rating | Absolute Error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Movie 1 | 4.5 | 4 | 0.5 |
| Movie 2 | 3.2 | 3 | 0.2 |
| Movie 3 | 2.8 | 3 | 0.2 |

The individual absolute errors are:

$$
|4.5-4|=0.5
$$

$$
|3.2-3|=0.2
$$

$$
|2.8-3|=0.2
$$

Therefore:

$$
MAE=\frac{0.5+0.2+0.2}{3}=0.3
$$

The resulting MAE is:

$$
MAE=0.3
$$

This means that the predictions differ from the actual ratings by an average absolute amount of 0.3 rating units.

### Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)

**Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)** measures prediction error by calculating the square root of the average squared difference between predicted and actual ratings.

It is defined as:

$$
RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_i-\hat{r}_i)^{2}}
$$

Where:

- $n$ is the number of evaluated predictions.
- $r_i$ is the actual rating.
- $\hat{r}_i$ is the predicted rating.

Unlike MAE, RMSE squares prediction errors before averaging them.

Consequently, larger errors contribute disproportionately to the result.

This makes RMSE especially useful when large prediction errors are considered particularly undesirable.

#### Numerical Example

Using the same three movies, the squared errors are:

$$
(4.5-4)^{2}=0.25
$$

$$
(3.2-3)^{2}=0.04
$$

$$
(2.8-3)^{2}=0.04
$$

The RMSE is therefore:

$$
RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{0.25+0.04+0.04}{3}}=\sqrt{0.11}\approx0.332
$$

Thus:

$$
RMSE\approx0.332
$$

A lower RMSE indicates that the predicted ratings are closer to the observed ratings.

Because RMSE penalizes large errors more heavily than MAE, it is appropriate when minimizing substantial prediction deviations is an important objective.

## 2. Top-N Recommendation Metrics

In many practical recommender systems, the objective is not to predict the exact rating that a user will assign to every item.

Instead, the objective is to produce a ranked list containing the items most likely to be relevant to that user.

For example, a movie recommendation platform may display the ten movies predicted to be most relevant to a particular user.

This is known as **Top-N Recommendation**.

Top-N evaluation requires defining which items are considered relevant.

For explicit-rating datasets, relevance may be determined using a rating threshold.

For example, movies with observed ratings of 4 or 5 may be considered relevant, while lower-rated movies are treated as nonrelevant within the evaluated observations.

The following metrics assess different aspects of ranked recommendation quality.

### Precision@K

**Precision@K** measures the proportion of the first $K$ recommended items that are relevant.

Its definition is:

$$
Precision@K=\frac{|\mathrm{Rel}_u\cap\mathrm{Rec}_u^K|}{K}
$$

Where:

- $\mathrm{Rel}_u$ is the set of items considered relevant to user $u$.
- $\mathrm{Rec}_u^K$ is the set containing the first $K$ recommended items.
- $|\mathrm{Rel}_u\cap\mathrm{Rec}_u^K|$ is the number of relevant items among the first $K$ recommendations.

#### Example

Suppose the system recommends ten movies, seven of which are relevant.

Then:

$$
Precision@10=\frac{7}{10}=0.7
$$

The recommendation list has a precision of 70%.

This means that seven out of every ten recommended movies are relevant under the selected relevance definition.

### Recall@K

**Recall@K** measures the proportion of all relevant items that appear among the first $K$ recommendations.

It is defined as:

$$
Recall@K=\frac{|\mathrm{Rel}_u\cap\mathrm{Rec}_u^K|}{|\mathrm{Rel}_u|}
$$

Where:

- $\mathrm{Rel}_u$ is the set of relevant items for user $u$.
- $\mathrm{Rec}_u^K$ contains the first $K$ recommendations.

Recall measures how much of the user's relevant item set has been successfully retrieved.

#### Example

Suppose a user has 20 relevant movies in the evaluation dataset.

If seven of these movies appear among the first ten recommendations:

$$
Recall@10=\frac{7}{20}=0.35
$$

The resulting recall is 35%.

This indicates that the recommendation list successfully retrieves 35% of the user's known relevant movies.

### F1-Score

**F1-Score** combines Precision and Recall using their harmonic mean.

It provides a single measure that reflects the balance between these two metrics.

The formula is:

$$
F1=\frac{2\cdot Precision\cdot Recall}{Precision+Recall}
$$

For Top-K recommendation, we can write:

$$
F1@K=\frac{2\cdot Precision@K\cdot Recall@K}{Precision@K+Recall@K}
$$

The harmonic mean is useful because it produces a low value when either Precision or Recall is low.

Therefore, obtaining a high F1-Score requires reasonably strong performance on both measures.

#### Numerical Example

Suppose:

$$
Precision@10=0.7
$$

and:

$$
Recall@10=0.35
$$

Then:

$$
F1@10=\frac{2\cdot0.7\cdot0.35}{0.7+0.35}
$$

Therefore:

$$
F1@10=\frac{0.49}{1.05}\approx0.467
$$

The resulting F1-Score is approximately:

$$
F1@10\approx0.467
$$

If both Precision and Recall are zero, the F1-Score is conventionally defined as zero.

### Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG@K)

**Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG@K)** evaluates the quality of the ordering of recommended items.

Unlike Precision@K and Recall@K, which primarily evaluate membership in the recommended set, NDCG considers the positions of relevant items within the ranked list.

The underlying principle is that relevant items should appear near the top of the recommendations.

A highly relevant item appearing at rank 1 contributes more to the score than the same item appearing at rank 10.

#### Discounted Cumulative Gain

A common formulation of Discounted Cumulative Gain is:

$$
DCG@K=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{2^{rel_j}-1}{\log_2(j+1)}
$$

Where:

- $rel_j$ is the relevance value of the item at ranking position $j$.
- $K$ is the number of evaluated recommendation positions.
- The logarithmic denominator discounts contributions from lower-ranked items.

The exponential relevance term allows highly relevant items to receive greater importance when graded relevance values are available.

#### Ideal Discounted Cumulative Gain

To normalize the score, we calculate the DCG of the ideal ranking, in which items are ordered by decreasing relevance.

This quantity is called **Ideal Discounted Cumulative Gain (IDCG)**.

NDCG is then calculated as:

$$
NDCG@K=\frac{DCG@K}{IDCG@K}
$$

When the ideal DCG is positive, the resulting score generally lies between 0 and 1.

A score closer to 1 indicates that the recommendation order more closely resembles the ideal relevance ordering.

When no relevant items are available, an evaluation convention must be specified for handling the zero denominator.

#### Example

Suppose two systems recommend the same set of movies.

The first system places the most relevant movies near the beginning of its recommendation list.

The second system includes those movies but places several of them much lower.

The first system will generally achieve a higher NDCG.

For example:

- System A: `NDCG@10 = 0.90`
- System B: `NDCG@10 = 0.60`

This indicates that System A provides a better relevance-based ranking under the chosen evaluation labels.

Importantly, NDCG measures the ranking positions of relevant items, not simply the popularity of recommended items.

# Beyond-Accuracy Metrics

Predictive accuracy is important, but it does not fully describe the quality of a recommender system.

A model may achieve high prediction accuracy while repeatedly recommending very similar or already familiar items.

Such recommendations may be technically relevant but provide limited value in terms of discovery and user experience.

**Beyond-Accuracy Metrics** evaluate additional characteristics of recommendation lists.

These include coverage, diversity, novelty, and serendipity.

## 1. Coverage

**Coverage** measures the extent to which a recommendation system can recommend items from the available catalog or serve the relevant user population.

One common form is **Catalog Coverage**, which measures the proportion of catalog items that appear in recommendation lists.

It can be expressed as:

$$
Coverage_{\text{catalog}}=\frac{|\bigcup_{u\in U}\mathrm{Rec}_u|}{|I|}
$$

Where:

- $U$ is the set of evaluated users.
- $I$ is the complete item catalog.
- $\mathrm{Rec}_u$ is the set of items recommended to user $u$.

Coverage can also be defined relative to a set of relevant items.

For example, suppose a user has 1,000 relevant movies among a catalog of 10,000 movies.

If the evaluated recommendation process retrieves 500 of those relevant movies, the proportion of the relevant set covered is:

$$
Coverage_{\text{relevant}}=\frac{500}{1000}=0.5
$$

Therefore:

$$
Coverage_{\text{relevant}}=50\%
$$

This indicates that the system has retrieved half of the user's known relevant items.

However, this measure is more closely related to recall over an evaluated recommendation set than to conventional catalog coverage.

For rigorous evaluation, the exact definition of coverage must therefore be specified.

## 2. Diversity

**Diversity** measures how different the recommended items are from one another.

A recommendation list containing highly similar items generally has low diversity.

A list containing items from several different genres, categories, or semantic groups typically has greater diversity.

For example, suppose a movie recommendation system suggests only comedy movies.

Even if all the recommendations match the user's preferences, the list may provide limited variety.

A list containing Comedy, Drama, and Action movies may offer a more diverse recommendation experience.

Diversity is often calculated using item dissimilarity.

One common formulation is **Intra-List Diversity (ILD)**:

$$
ILD(L)=\frac{2}{|L|(|L|-1)}\sum_{i<j,\ i,j\in L}d(i,j)
$$

Where:

- $L$ is the recommendation list.
- $|L|$ is the number of recommended items.
- $d(i,j)$ is a dissimilarity measure between items $i$ and $j$.

This metric measures the average pairwise dissimilarity between recommended items.

For example, the dissimilarity measure may be calculated from movie genres or item embeddings.

Greater diversity may encourage exploration and reduce repetitive recommendations.

However, excessive diversity can reduce relevance if the system recommends items unrelated to the user's interests.

## 3. Novelty

**Novelty** measures the extent to which recommendations introduce users to unfamiliar or less commonly encountered items.

For example, recommending an independent film that a user has never heard of may provide greater novelty than recommending a widely known Hollywood blockbuster.

Novelty is often associated with item popularity.

Less popular items may be considered more novel because users are less likely to have encountered them previously.

However, popularity is only a proxy for novelty.

An item may be globally popular but unfamiliar to a particular user, while a relatively obscure item may already be well known to that user.

Novelty evaluation should therefore consider the available information about user familiarity whenever possible.

## 4. Serendipity

**Serendipity** describes recommendations that are both unexpected and useful or relevant to the user.

A recommendation is not necessarily serendipitous simply because it is unusual.

It must also provide positive value.

For example, consider a user who consistently watches science-fiction movies.

The system recommends a sports documentary that the user would not ordinarily search for.

If the user watches and enjoys the documentary, the recommendation may be considered serendipitous.

Serendipity therefore combines two important characteristics:

- **Unexpectedness:** The item differs from what the user would ordinarily expect.
- **Relevance:** The item is nevertheless useful, interesting, or satisfying.

Measuring serendipity is challenging because unexpectedness and satisfaction are partly subjective.

Some evaluation approaches compare recommendations against a primitive or expected baseline, while others incorporate user feedback.

## The Importance of Beyond-Accuracy Evaluation

A study published in 2019 reported that, among the studies it examined:

- Accuracy was evaluated in 62 studies.
- Diversity was evaluated in 7 studies.
- User satisfaction was evaluated in 10 studies.

These reported counts illustrate that traditional accuracy metrics have historically received greater attention than some user-centered evaluation dimensions.

However, focusing exclusively on accuracy may overlook important aspects of recommendation quality.

For hybrid recommender systems, evaluating both accuracy and beyond-accuracy properties is particularly useful because different component algorithms may contribute different strengths.

For example, a collaborative filtering model may provide strong relevance predictions, while a content-based component may increase catalog coverage or help recommend less popular items.

A hybrid architecture should therefore be evaluated according to multiple objectives rather than a single numerical score.

## Evaluation Methodologies

Recommendation systems can be evaluated using several experimental methodologies.

Two major categories are:

1. **Offline Evaluation**
2. **Online Evaluation**

These approaches differ in their data requirements, experimental conditions, costs, and ability to measure real-world user behavior.

# 1. Offline Evaluation

Offline evaluation measures recommendation performance using previously collected interaction data.

Datasets such as MovieLens are commonly used for this purpose.

The dataset is divided into training and evaluation subsets.

For example:

- 80% of the interactions may be used for training.
- 20% may be reserved for testing.

The exact proportions depend on the experimental design.

### Offline Evaluation Procedure

A typical offline experiment consists of three stages.

**Step 1: Train the Recommendation Model**

The model learns from the training interactions.

For a hybrid system, this may involve training separate content-based and collaborative filtering components.

**Step 2: Generate Predictions or Recommendations**

The trained system generates predicted ratings or ranked recommendation lists for the evaluation observations.

**Step 3: Calculate Evaluation Metrics**

The predictions are compared with the available ground-truth observations.

Depending on the task, evaluation metrics may include:

- MAE.
- RMSE.
- Precision@K.
- Recall@K.
- F1@K.
- NDCG@K.

### Example

Suppose a recommendation system generates ten movie recommendations.

Seven of the recommended movies are relevant.

Then:

$$
Precision@10=\frac{7}{10}=0.7
$$

This provides an offline estimate of recommendation precision.

### Advantages of Offline Evaluation

Offline evaluation has several practical advantages.

It is generally:

- Relatively inexpensive.
- Easy to reproduce.
- Faster than large-scale online experimentation.
- Suitable for comparing many candidate models.
- Useful during model development and hyperparameter optimization.

Because evaluation is performed using historical data, researchers can experiment without directly affecting users.

### Limitations of Offline Evaluation

The most important limitation is that historical interaction data does not fully capture how users would respond to new recommendations.

For example, an item may be absent from a user's historical interactions because it was never displayed to that user.

This does not necessarily mean the item was irrelevant.

Historical datasets may also contain exposure bias, popularity bias, and other forms of selection bias.

Furthermore, users' preferences may change over time.

As a result, strong offline performance does not guarantee equally strong performance after deployment.

Offline evaluation should therefore be interpreted as an estimate of recommendation quality under the assumptions of the selected dataset and evaluation protocol.

# 2. Online Evaluation

Online evaluation measures recommendation performance through interactions with real users.

Unlike offline evaluation, it observes behavior generated while users interact with the deployed recommendation system.

Online evaluation may use metrics such as:

- Click-through rate.
- Conversion rate.
- Viewing duration.
- Session duration.
- Purchase frequency.
- User satisfaction.

Because these measurements reflect actual user interactions, online evaluation can provide direct evidence about the practical effectiveness of recommendations.

### Example

Suppose a movie streaming platform displays recommended movies to its users.

If 70% of the relevant recommendation opportunities lead to clicks, the measured interaction rate is:

$$
CTR=0.70
$$

The exact interpretation depends on the definition of the denominator.

For example, click-through rate is commonly defined as:

$$
CTR=\frac{\text{Number of Clicks}}{\text{Number of Recommendation Impressions}}
$$

An impression represents an instance in which a recommendation is displayed.

This definition is distinct from the percentage of users who clicked at least one recommendation.

Therefore, online evaluation metrics must be defined precisely before their results are interpreted.

### Advantages of Online Evaluation

Online evaluation provides information about actual user responses.

It can reveal effects that may not be captured by offline accuracy metrics.

For example, a highly accurate recommender might produce repetitive suggestions that users eventually stop clicking.

An online experiment may reveal this problem even when offline Precision@K remains high.

### Limitations of Online Evaluation

Online experiments require additional resources, including:

- A functioning production recommendation system.
- Real users.
- Experiment assignment mechanisms.
- Interaction logging infrastructure.
- Reliable monitoring and evaluation tools.

They may also introduce operational risks if an experimental model produces poor recommendations.

Consequently, online evaluation is generally more expensive and operationally complex than offline evaluation.

## Advanced Evaluation Methodologies

Traditional offline and online evaluation methods can be extended to better account for changing user behavior and the complexity of real-world recommendation environments.

Three particularly important approaches are:

1. Time-Based Cross-Validation.
2. A/B Testing.
3. User Interaction Log Analysis.

# 1. Time-Based Cross-Validation

In many recommendation applications, user preferences and interaction patterns change over time.

For example, a user may prefer pop music during the summer and develop a stronger interest in jazz during the winter.

Similarly, product popularity may change following seasonal events or new product releases.

A random train–test split may not adequately reflect these temporal changes.

**Time-Based Cross-Validation** addresses this limitation by preserving the chronological ordering of interactions.

### How Time-Based Cross-Validation Works

The procedure generally involves the following steps.

1. Sort the interaction data chronologically.
2. Select a temporal boundary separating older and newer observations.
3. Train the recommendation model using interactions that occurred before the boundary.
4. Evaluate the model using interactions that occurred after the boundary.
5. Repeat the procedure using additional temporal boundaries.

For example, the oldest 80% of interactions may be used for training, while the newest 20% are reserved for evaluation.

Unlike random splitting, this procedure more closely resembles a deployment scenario in which past behavior is used to predict future behavior.

### Rolling or Expanding Evaluation Windows

Time-based validation can also be repeated using multiple evaluation windows.

For example:

| Experiment | Training Period | Evaluation Period |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | January–March | April |
| 2 | January–April | May |
| 3 | January–May | June |

This approach allows the evaluator to examine whether recommendation quality remains stable as time progresses.

It is especially useful for applications such as Spotify, Netflix, and e-commerce platforms, where preferences and item popularity may change.

Time-based cross-validation helps determine whether the system can adapt to evolving user behavior.

# 2. A/B Testing

**A/B Testing** is an online experimental methodology in which two versions of a recommendation system are evaluated simultaneously using different groups of users.

The objective is to determine whether a proposed change improves performance under real-world conditions.

For example, a platform may compare an existing hybrid recommendation model with a new version that uses different combination weights.

### How A/B Testing Works

A typical experiment follows four stages.

1. Randomly assign eligible users to two groups.
2. Expose Group A to the existing recommendation system.
3. Expose Group B to the modified recommendation system.
4. Compare the measured outcomes using appropriate statistical methods.

Suppose the existing hybrid recommender uses:

$$
w_{CF}=0.70,\qquad w_{CBF}=0.30
$$

The new system uses:

$$
w_{CF}=0.50,\qquad w_{CBF}=0.50
$$

The experiment evaluates whether changing the weights improves user engagement.

### Numerical Example

Consider an online movie platform conducting an A/B test.

The experiment involves two groups:

| Group | Users | CF Weight | CBF Weight | Click-Through Rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | 70% | 30% | 60% |
| B | 5,000 | 50% | 50% | 68% |

After one week, the measured click-through rates are:

$$
CTR_A=0.60
$$

$$
CTR_B=0.68
$$

The absolute difference is:

$$
\Delta CTR=0.68-0.60=0.08
$$

Therefore, Group B achieves an increase of **8 percentage points**.

The relative increase is:

$$
\frac{0.68-0.60}{0.60}\times100\approx13.33\%
$$

Thus, the new system produces an approximately 13.33% relative increase in click-through rate.

### Statistical Significance

Observed differences between experimental groups may arise partly from random variation.

Statistical hypothesis testing helps determine whether the observed difference provides sufficient evidence against the null hypothesis.

For example, an appropriate statistical test may produce:

$$
p<0.05
$$

This indicates that the observed result meets a conventional 5% statistical significance threshold under the assumptions of the test.

Depending on the measurement and experimental design, possible methods include a t-test or a test for differences between proportions.

The experiment described here assumes that statistical analysis confirms a significant improvement for Group B.

However, statistical significance should be interpreted alongside effect size, uncertainty intervals, and practical business relevance.

A statistically significant difference is not necessarily large enough to justify deployment.

### Why A/B Testing Matters

A/B testing allows recommendation system developers to assess the practical effects of changes such as:

- Adjusting hybrid model weights.
- Introducing a new recommendation component.
- Modifying candidate generation.
- Changing ranking algorithms.
- Increasing recommendation diversity.

It provides evidence about whether improvements observed during offline development translate into measurable benefits for real users.

# 3. User Interaction Log Analysis

User interaction logs record how users behave while interacting with a recommendation system.

These logs may contain events such as:

- Recommendation impressions.
- Clicks.
- Product views.
- Add-to-cart actions.
- Purchases.
- Viewing duration.
- Session activity.

Analyzing these records provides valuable information about actual recommendation performance.

### Log Analysis Procedure

A typical analysis includes the following steps.

**Step 1: Collect Interaction Events**

The system records user interactions with recommended items.

**Step 2: Calculate Behavioral Metrics**

Metrics such as click-through rate, conversion rate, and average engagement duration are calculated.

**Step 3: Compare Behavioral and Offline Performance**

The results are compared with offline metrics, such as Precision@K and Recall@K.

This comparison helps identify discrepancies between predicted relevance and actual user behavior.

### Example: E-Commerce Recommendations

Suppose an online store collects recommendation interaction logs over one month.

The observed click-through rate is:

$$
CTR=0.55
$$

This means that clicks occurred for 55% of recommendation impressions under the example's measurement definition.

Suppose that 10% of those clicks resulted in purchases.

The click-to-purchase conversion rate is:

$$
ConversionRate=0.10
$$

Further analysis reveals different conversion rates for the recommendation components.

| Recommendation Method | Conversion Rate |
|---|---:|
| Content-Based Filtering | 12% |
| Collaborative Filtering | 8% |

Under comparable conditions, the content-based component appears to generate more purchase conversions.

This information may support increasing the contribution of content-based filtering within the hybrid model.

However, before changing the weights, the system should examine possible differences in traffic composition, item exposure, and user characteristics.

A higher observed conversion rate does not automatically establish that one component would perform better for every user or recommendation context.

### Advantages of Log Analysis

Interaction log analysis provides insight into user behavior that may not be visible through offline evaluation.

For example, the logs may reveal that highly accurate recommendations are frequently ignored because they are too repetitive or insufficiently novel.

They may also indicate that users respond more positively to recommendations that introduce new product categories.

Such insights can guide the adjustment of model weights, ranking objectives, and diversity strategies.

## Combining Advanced Evaluation Methods

The evaluation techniques described above can be used together to produce a more comprehensive understanding of a hybrid recommender system.

Consider a weighted hybrid architecture that initially assigns:

$$
w_{CF}=0.60
$$

and:

$$
w_{CBF}=0.40
$$

Different evaluation methods may reveal different aspects of its performance.

### Time-Based Cross-Validation

A chronological evaluation indicates that Precision@5 has decreased over recent months:

$$
Precision@5:0.70\rightarrow0.60
$$

This suggests that the model's ability to identify relevant items may have deteriorated as user preferences or interaction patterns changed.

### A/B Testing

An online experiment evaluates a new weighting configuration:

$$
w_{CF}=0.50,\qquad w_{CBF}=0.50
$$

The click-through rate increases from:

$$
CTR_A=0.60
$$

to:

$$
CTR_B=0.65
$$

This suggests that the revised weighting strategy may provide a better recommendation experience under the experimental conditions.

### User Interaction Log Analysis

Further analysis indicates that more diverse recommendations, including items from previously unexplored genres, are associated with a 20% increase in user engagement time.

This provides additional evidence that recommendation diversity may contribute positively to the user experience.

Taken together, these findings support a more informed approach to adjusting hybrid recommendation weights.

Rather than optimizing only offline prediction accuracy, the system can consider temporal stability, actual user engagement, and recommendation diversity.

## Evaluating Different Hybrid Recommendation Architectures

Hybrid recommendation systems can differ substantially in how they combine their component algorithms.

Consequently, their evaluation should account for the characteristics of the selected hybridization strategy.

Three important categories are:

- Switching Hybrid Systems.
- Weighted Hybrid Systems.
- Cascade Hybrid Systems.

### 1. Switching Hybrid Systems

A switching hybrid selects one recommendation algorithm according to the current conditions.

For example, a system may use content-based filtering for users with limited interaction histories and collaborative filtering for more established users.

Evaluation should examine both the correctness of the selection mechanism and the performance of the selected algorithm.

#### Switching Decision Quality

The evaluator should determine whether the switching mechanism selects an appropriate model for each recommendation situation.

For example, if the system correctly identifies that content-based filtering is preferable for most new users, its switching mechanism may be considered effective.

#### Component Performance

Each algorithm should be evaluated under the conditions in which it is activated.

For example, Precision@K can be calculated separately for:

- New users assigned to content-based filtering.
- Established users assigned to collaborative filtering.

This reveals whether the switching strategy successfully exploits the strengths of the component models.

#### Example

Suppose the system activates content-based filtering for users with fewer than five historical ratings.

Among 20 new users, 18 report satisfaction with the recommendations.

The observed satisfaction proportion is:

$$
SatisfactionRate=\frac{18}{20}=0.90
$$

Therefore:

$$
SatisfactionRate=90\%
$$

This indicates favorable outcomes for the selected user group.

However, user satisfaction alone does not establish that the switching decision was optimal.

To evaluate switching accuracy directly, the selected model should ideally be compared with alternative models or a suitable reference selection strategy.

### 2. Weighted Hybrid Systems

Weighted hybrid systems combine predictions using numerical weights.

For example:

$$
\hat{R}_{u,i}=0.60\hat{R}_{u,i}^{CF}+0.40\hat{R}_{u,i}^{CBF}
$$

Evaluation focuses on the performance of the final combined predictions.

Possible metrics include:

- RMSE.
- MAE.
- Precision@K.
- Recall@K.
- NDCG@K.

#### Sensitivity Analysis

An important additional procedure is **weight sensitivity analysis**.

This involves changing the combination weights and observing how performance responds.

For example:

| CF Weight | CBF Weight | RMSE |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.70 | 0.30 | 0.85 |
| 0.50 | 0.50 | 0.75 |

Because lower RMSE indicates better rating prediction accuracy, the second configuration performs better on the evaluated dataset.

The difference is:

$$
0.85-0.75=0.10
$$

This illustrates how changing model weights can improve hybrid prediction quality.

Weight optimization should be performed using validation data, while the test set should remain reserved for final evaluation.

### 3. Cascade Hybrid Systems

A cascade hybrid applies recommendation models sequentially.

For example, the first stage may identify candidate items, while the second stage ranks those candidates.

Because the stages perform different functions, they should be evaluated using different metrics.

#### First Stage: Candidate Generation

The objective of candidate generation is to retrieve a sufficiently large proportion of relevant items.

Therefore, **Recall** is especially important.

A candidate generator should avoid excluding relevant items that a later ranking stage might otherwise prioritize.

#### Second Stage: Ranking

The ranking stage attempts to place the most relevant candidates near the top of the recommendation list.

Suitable evaluation metrics include:

- Precision@K.
- NDCG@K.

These metrics assess the quality of the final ranked recommendations.

#### Example

Suppose a cascade recommender operates on MovieLens.

The first stage selects 100 candidate movies using a high-recall retrieval strategy.

The second stage ranks these candidates and returns the ten highest-scoring movies.

The final ranking is evaluated using:

$$
NDCG@10
$$

This measures whether the most relevant movies appear near the beginning of the final recommendation list.

Evaluating the two stages separately is important because a high-quality ranking model cannot recover relevant items that were excluded by candidate generation.

## Evaluating Cold-Start Performance

The **cold-start problem** occurs when insufficient interaction information is available for new users or items.

Hybrid recommender systems can mitigate this problem by combining interaction-based and content-based information.

However, their cold-start performance must be evaluated explicitly.

### New User Cold Start

A new user may have very few historical ratings.

In this situation, collaborative filtering often lacks sufficient information to identify reliable similarities.

A content-based model may instead exploit:

- Explicitly stated preferences.
- User profile information.
- Interests selected during registration.
- Early interaction signals.

A hybrid system can use these alternative information sources to generate initial recommendations.

#### Evaluation

One possible approach is to evaluate Precision@K specifically for users with fewer than five historical ratings.

This assesses recommendation quality for users with limited interaction histories.

However, users with no historical interactions require a separate evaluation strategy because their observed preferences may be unavailable.

### New Item Cold Start

A newly introduced item may not have received any ratings or interactions.

Interaction-only collaborative filtering cannot reliably learn its relationships with other items or users from nonexistent interaction data.

Content-based recommendation can help when descriptive item metadata is available.

For example, a new movie may have information about its genre, director, cast, or synopsis even before users begin rating it.

A hybrid system can exploit these features to generate recommendations.

#### Evaluation

Cold-start item evaluation can be performed by excluding selected items entirely from the training interactions.

The model is then evaluated on its ability to recommend these previously unseen items.

Recall@K may be used to measure whether relevant cold-start items appear among the recommendations.

This experimental design provides a direct assessment of the system's ability to generalize to new items.

# Practical Implementation

We now implement a simple hybrid recommender using Python and the **Surprise** library.

The objective is to demonstrate how two collaborative filtering algorithms can be combined and evaluated using:

- Mean Absolute Error (MAE).
- Precision@K.

The example uses:

1. **SVD**, a latent-factor collaborative filtering model.
2. **KNNBasic**, a neighborhood-based collaborative filtering model.

Although both components are collaborative filtering algorithms, combining them still constitutes an ensemble recommendation approach because they use different modeling strategies.

The example focuses on evaluating the combined predictions.

## Prerequisites

Install the Surprise library:

```bash
pip install scikit-surprise
```

We use the **MovieLens 100K** dataset, which is available through Surprise's built-in dataset interface.

## Implementation Steps

### 1. Loading the Dataset and Training the Component Models

First, we load the dataset and divide it into training and test subsets.

We then train the SVD and KNNBasic models independently.

```python
from surprise import Dataset, Reader, SVD, KNNBasic, accuracy
from surprise.model_selection import train_test_split

# Upload MovieLens 100k dataset
data = Dataset.load_builtin('ml-100k')

# Splitting the data into training and test sets (80% training, 20% test)
trainset, testset = train_test_split(data, test_size=0.2)

# SVD model training
svd = SVD()
svd.fit(trainset)

# Training the KNNBasic model (based on cosine similarity between items)
knn = KNNBasic(sim_options={'name': 'cosine', 'user_based': False})
knn.fit(trainset)
```

The `SVD()` model learns latent-factor representations from observed user–item ratings.

The `KNNBasic()` model uses a neighborhood-based recommendation approach.

Its configuration specifies:

```python
sim_options={'name': 'cosine', 'user_based': False}
```

This means that the model uses cosine similarity and constructs neighborhoods based on items rather than users.

Thus, the two component algorithms capture different relationships:

- SVD learns latent user and item representations.
- KNNBasic exploits similarities between observed item rating patterns.

### 2. Implementing a Simple Hybrid Prediction Function

Next, we define a function that combines the predictions of the two models.

The default configuration assigns equal weights to SVD and KNNBasic.

```python
def hybrid_predict(user_id, item_id, svd_model, knn_model, weight_svd=0.5, weight_knn=0.5):
    # SVD prediction
    pred_svd = svd_model.predict(user_id, item_id).est
    # KNN prediction
    pred_knn = knn_model.predict(user_id, item_id).est
    # Weighted combination of predictions
    return weight_svd * pred_svd + weight_knn * pred_knn
```

The function accepts:

- `user_id`: The target user identifier.
- `item_id`: The target item identifier.
- `svd_model`: The trained SVD model.
- `knn_model`: The trained KNNBasic model.
- `weight_svd`: The weight assigned to the SVD prediction.
- `weight_knn`: The weight assigned to the KNN prediction.

The predicted rating is:

$$
\hat{R}_{u,i}^{Hybrid}=w_{SVD}\hat{R}_{u,i}^{SVD}+w_{KNN}\hat{R}_{u,i}^{KNN}
$$

With equal weights:

$$
\hat{R}_{u,i}^{Hybrid}=0.5\hat{R}_{u,i}^{SVD}+0.5\hat{R}_{u,i}^{KNN}
$$

This produces a weighted average of the two component predictions.

### 3. Calculating Mean Absolute Error

We now evaluate the hybrid model using MAE.

First, we generate predictions for the test observations.

Then, we compare these predictions with the actual test ratings.

```python
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

# Generate predictions for the test set
predictions = [hybrid_predict(uid, iid, svd, knn) for (uid, iid, _) in testset]
true_ratings = [rating for (_, _, rating) in testset]

# MAE calculation
mae = mean_absolute_error(true_ratings, predictions)
print(f'MAE hybrid system: {mae:.3f}')
```

The list comprehension:

```python
predictions = [hybrid_predict(uid, iid, svd, knn) for (uid, iid, _) in testset]
```

generates a hybrid prediction for every user–item pair in the test set.

The corresponding actual ratings are extracted using:

```python
true_ratings = [rating for (_, _, rating) in testset]
```

Finally, Scikit-learn's `mean_absolute_error()` function calculates the average absolute difference between the actual and predicted ratings.

The lower the resulting MAE, the more accurate the hybrid rating predictions are under this metric.

### 4. Calculating Precision@K

We next evaluate the quality of the Top-K recommendations.

For this example, an item is considered relevant if its observed rating is at least 3.5.

Thus, the relevance condition is:

$$
r_{u,i}\ge3.5
$$

We calculate Precision@10, which measures the proportion of relevant items among the ten highest-ranked recommendations.

The following function evaluates a recommendation model using the held-out rating observations.

```python
from collections import defaultdict

def precision_at_k(model, testset, k=10, threshold=3.5):
    # # Save predictions and actual scores for each user
    user_est_true = defaultdict(list)
    for uid, iid, true_r in testset:
        est = model.predict(uid, iid).est
        user_est_true[uid].append((est, true_r))

    precisions = []
    for uid, ratings in user_est_true.items():
        # Sorting predictions from highest to lowest
        ratings.sort(key=lambda x: x[0], reverse=True)
        # Choose K Top Offer
        top_k = ratings[:k]
        # Count the number of related items (actual score >= threshold)
        relevant = sum(1 for est, true_r in top_k if true_r >= threshold)
        precisions.append(relevant / k)

    # Average accuracies
    return sum(precisions) / len(precisions)

# Precision@10 calculation (for simplicity, SVD is used as a representative of the hybrid system)
precision = precision_at_k(svd, testset, k=10)
print(f'Precision@10 hybrid system: {precision:.3f}')
```

The function performs several operations.

**Step 1: Group Predictions by User**

The function iterates through test observations and records each item's predicted and actual rating.

**Step 2: Sort Predictions**

For each user, the predicted ratings are sorted from highest to lowest.

**Step 3: Select the Top-K Items**

The function selects the first $K$ items from the ranked list.

**Step 4: Count Relevant Items**

An item is treated as relevant if its actual rating satisfies:

$$
r_{u,i}\ge3.5
$$

**Step 5: Calculate Precision**

The number of relevant items is divided by $K$.

**Step 6: Average Across Users**

The function returns the arithmetic mean of the user-level precision values.

### Interpreting the Implementation

The code demonstrates a basic approach to Precision@K evaluation.

However, two methodological details are important.

First, the final call evaluates the **SVD component**, not the weighted hybrid predictor.

Therefore, the printed label `Precision@10 hybrid system` does not accurately describe the model being evaluated.

To evaluate the actual hybrid recommender, the evaluation function must use `hybrid_predict()` instead of relying exclusively on the SVD model.

Second, the function ranks only items present in each user's test observations.

This is a restricted candidate-set evaluation rather than a complete ranking over all unseen items in the catalog.

Furthermore, dividing by $K$ assumes that at least $K$ candidate items are available for each evaluated user.

These limitations should be considered when interpreting the resulting Precision@K values.

## Comparison of Evaluation Metrics and Methods

The following table summarizes the principal metrics and methodologies discussed in this section.

| Category | Metric or Method | Primary Application | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy Metrics | MAE / RMSE | Rating prediction | Simple, quantitative, and widely applicable | Do not directly evaluate recommendation ranking |
| Accuracy Metrics | Precision / Recall / F1 / NDCG | Top-N recommendation | Standard metrics for relevance and ranking quality | Sensitive to relevance definitions, candidate sets, and the choice of K |
| Beyond-Accuracy Metrics | Coverage / Diversity / Novelty / Serendipity | User experience and recommendation quality | Capture dimensions not reflected by predictive accuracy alone | Often require additional information or more complex calculations |
| Evaluation Method | Offline Evaluation | Historical interaction data | Relatively inexpensive, fast, and reproducible | May not accurately reflect user behavior after deployment |
| Evaluation Method | Online Evaluation | Real users and deployed systems | Measures actual user responses and practical outcomes | Requires production infrastructure, monitoring, and experimental controls |
| Evaluation Method | Time-Based Cross-Validation | Temporally ordered interactions | Evaluates changes in behavior and temporal generalization | More complex to implement and may require repeated training |

Each evaluation method provides a different perspective on recommendation performance.

Accuracy metrics quantify how closely predictions correspond to observed preferences.

Beyond-accuracy metrics assess additional aspects of recommendation quality, including variety, discovery, and user experience.

Offline evaluation supports efficient model development, while online evaluation provides evidence about actual user responses.

Time-based evaluation adds an important temporal perspective by measuring whether recommendation quality remains stable as user behavior changes.

For hybrid recommender systems, combining these evaluation perspectives is particularly valuable because the system's effectiveness depends not only on the quality of its constituent algorithms but also on how those algorithms interact, how their contributions are selected or weighted, and how well the resulting recommendations serve users under real-world conditions.
