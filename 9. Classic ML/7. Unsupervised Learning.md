
So far in this chapter, we have examined machine learning algorithms designed to predict a **target variable**, whether a numerical value or a categorical label.

For example, consider predicting the rating that a particular user would assign to a specific item. Using available user and item features, we attempt to estimate a predefined target variable. The model learns relationships between input features and their corresponding labels so that it can predict outcomes for previously unseen observations.

This approach is known as **Supervised Learning**.

However, labeled data is not always available in real-world applications. In many situations, we have only a collection of observations and want to discover the underlying patterns, structures, or relationships without knowing in advance which patterns exist.

This is the central objective of **Unsupervised Learning**.

Unlike supervised learning, which learns a mapping between inputs and known targets, unsupervised learning analyzes the structure of the input data itself.

The following table summarizes the principal differences between these two approaches.

| Characteristic | Supervised Learning | Unsupervised Learning |
|---|---|---|
| **Input data** | Features (`X`) and corresponding targets (`Y`) | Features (`X`) without target labels |
| **Objective** | Predict numerical values or class labels for new observations | Discover patterns, structures, hidden relationships, or lower-dimensional representations |
| **Common techniques** | Regression and classification | Clustering, dimensionality reduction, association rule mining |
| **Examples** | Rating prediction, predicting whether a user likes an item | Customer segmentation, image compression |
| **Applications in recommender systems** | Rating prediction, click prediction, purchase prediction | User and item clustering, latent feature discovery, association rule mining |

## 1. Unsupervised Learning in Recommender Systems

Unsupervised learning encompasses a broad range of techniques, each designed to discover a particular type of structure in data.

In recommender systems, these techniques can help us understand users, organize items, compress high-dimensional feature representations, discover relationships between products, and identify unusual interaction patterns.

The following sections examine four important applications:

1. Clustering.
2. Dimensionality Reduction.
3. Association Rule Mining.
4. Anomaly Detection.

## 2. Clustering: Grouping Similar Observations

**Clustering** is an unsupervised learning technique that groups observations according to their similarity.

The objective is to divide a dataset into groups called **clusters**, such that observations within the same cluster are relatively similar, while observations belonging to different clusters are relatively dissimilar.

For example, suppose we have a dataset containing information about customers, including:

- Age.
- Income.
- Purchasing behavior.
- Product preferences.
- Browsing activity.

A clustering algorithm can analyze these characteristics and identify groups of customers with similar profiles.

One cluster might contain customers primarily interested in books, another might represent customers who frequently purchase electronics, and another might consist of customers who prefer sports equipment.

Importantly, these groups are discovered from the data rather than defined in advance using labeled examples.

### 2.1. Applications of Clustering in Recommender Systems

Clustering is particularly useful in large-scale recommendation environments containing diverse users and items.

Consider an online marketplace such as Amazon, which offers products across many categories and serves customers with widely varying interests.

Developing recommendation models for such an environment may require large amounts of heterogeneous information.

However, not every feature is relevant to every user or item.

For example, a feature describing the sound output of a musical instrument may be useful when recommending audio equipment, but it is unlikely to contribute meaningfully to recommending books.

Furthermore, some user or item features may be unavailable or incomplete.

Clustering can help address these challenges by identifying meaningful groups and allowing the recommendation system to use more appropriate features and models for each group.

Two important applications are **User Segmentation** and **Item Categorization**.

#### User Segmentation

User segmentation divides users into groups based on similarities in their characteristics or behavior.

For example, users may be grouped according to:

- Preferred product categories.
- Historical purchase patterns.
- Browsing behavior.
- Price sensitivity.
- Content consumption preferences.

These groups can support personalized recommendation strategies.

A system might prioritize educational books for a cluster of users interested in academic content, while emphasizing sports products for another cluster.

Such strategies should be based on observed behavioral patterns rather than assuming that every member of a cluster has identical preferences.

#### Item Categorization

Item clustering groups products or content according to their similarities.

For example, movies might be clustered based on genre, descriptions, audience characteristics, or learned feature representations.

Similarly, products may be grouped according to their attributes and purchasing patterns.

These clusters can help a recommender system identify related items and restrict candidate generation to relevant regions of the item space.

Clustering may also support specialized recommendation models that operate on different product categories.

### 2.2. Clustering and the Cold-Start Problem

Clustering can also help mitigate the **Cold-Start Problem**.

A new user may have little or no historical interaction data, making traditional collaborative filtering difficult.

However, the system may still have access to information collected during registration or the user's initial interactions.

For example, a new user might provide preferred categories or interact with several products during an initial browsing session.

Using these observations, the system can assign the user to an existing cluster or estimate the user's similarity to several clusters.

The recommendation system can then use the preferences of similar users to generate initial recommendations.

As additional interactions become available, the user's cluster assignment or recommendation profile can be updated.

This approach does not eliminate cold start entirely, because accurate clustering still depends on the availability of informative features. Nevertheless, it can provide a useful initial personalization strategy.

### 2.3. How Does Clustering Work?

Clustering algorithms differ in how they define similarity and construct groups.

Some algorithms assign every observation to exactly one cluster, while others allow overlapping memberships or identify observations that do not belong to any well-defined cluster.

One widely used approach is **K-Means Clustering**.

K-Means partitions observations into a predefined number of clusters, denoted by $k$.

The algorithm attempts to minimize the total squared distance between each observation and the center of its assigned cluster.

Its objective function is:

$$
J=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_{i}-\mu_{c_{i}}\rVert^{2}
$$

Where:

- $n$ is the number of observations.
- $x_{i}$ is the feature vector representing observation $i$.
- $c_{i}$ is the cluster assigned to observation $i$.
- $\mu_{c_{i}}$ is the centroid of that cluster.
- $\lVert x_{i}-\mu_{c_{i}}\rVert^{2}$ is the squared Euclidean distance between the observation and its assigned centroid.

The algorithm typically follows these steps:

1. Select the number of clusters, $k$.
2. Initialize $k$ cluster centroids.
3. Assign each observation to its nearest centroid.
4. Recalculate each centroid using the mean of its assigned observations.
5. Repeat the assignment and centroid-update steps until the algorithm converges or reaches a stopping condition.

Because K-Means relies on distances between feature vectors, feature scaling can strongly influence its results.

For example, an income feature measured in thousands may dominate an age feature measured in decades unless the features are appropriately transformed.

The choice of $k$ is also important. An unsuitable number of clusters can produce groups that are too broad or unnecessarily fragmented.

Other clustering algorithms, such as hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN, use different assumptions and may be more appropriate for particular data distributions.

## 3. Dimensionality Reduction: Compressing Information

In machine learning, datasets may contain hundreds, thousands, or even millions of features.

As dimensionality increases, storing, processing, and analyzing observations can become increasingly expensive.

High-dimensional data may also make it difficult to measure similarity reliably or train models efficiently.

These difficulties are commonly associated with the **Curse of Dimensionality**.

The curse of dimensionality refers to several phenomena that arise as the number of dimensions increases, including the rapid growth of the space in which observations are represented.

As a result, data can become increasingly sparse, and substantially more observations may be required to estimate patterns reliably.

There is no universal threshold, such as 200 features, beyond which the curse of dimensionality automatically becomes problematic. Its effects depend on the dataset, feature representation, sample size, and learning algorithm.

### 3.1. The Fundamental Idea

**Dimensionality Reduction** aims to represent data using fewer features while preserving as much important information as possible.

Suppose each item is represented by a vector containing 1,000 features.

A dimensionality reduction algorithm might transform these vectors into representations containing only 50 features.

The resulting vectors are smaller and may be easier to store, process, visualize, or use as inputs to other models.

The challenge is to preserve the information that matters for the intended task.

### 3.2. Connection to Matrix Factorization

Matrix factorization, which we examined in previous chapters, is closely related to dimensionality reduction.

Recall that a user–item interaction matrix can be approximated by the product of two lower-dimensional matrices:

$$
R\approx PQ^{T}
$$

Where:

- $R$ is the user–item interaction matrix.
- $P$ contains latent representations of users.
- $Q$ contains latent representations of items.
- The number of latent dimensions is generally much smaller than the number of users or items.

Instead of representing each user using a vector whose length equals the total number of items, we can represent the user using a smaller latent feature vector.

Similarly, each item can be represented by a compact vector in the same latent space.

These latent representations preserve information that is useful for reconstructing observed interactions and predicting missing ones.

Thus, matrix factorization provides a powerful form of low-dimensional representation learning in recommender systems.

### 3.3. Applications in Feature Engineering

Dimensionality reduction is also useful when a recommender system contains extensive user or item metadata.

For example, item descriptions may be transformed into high-dimensional numerical vectors.

Likewise, users may be represented using hundreds of behavioral statistics.

Dimensionality reduction can transform these representations into a smaller set of features.

The resulting features can then be used as inputs to:

- Supervised learning models, such as Logistic Regression.
- Clustering algorithms.
- Similarity-based recommendation methods.
- Other components of a hybrid recommender system.

This can reduce computational requirements and sometimes improve model generalization by eliminating redundant or noisy dimensions.

However, reducing dimensionality may also remove useful information, so its effect should be evaluated empirically.

### 3.4. Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

**Principal Component Analysis (PCA)** is a classical linear dimensionality reduction technique.

PCA transforms the original features into a new coordinate system whose axes are called **principal components**.

These components are constructed so that:

- The first principal component captures the greatest possible variance in the data.
- The second captures the greatest remaining variance while being orthogonal to the first.
- Subsequent components capture progressively smaller amounts of variance.

By retaining only the first few principal components, we obtain a lower-dimensional representation that preserves as much variance as possible for that number of linear components.

For example, a dataset containing three numerical features can be projected onto a two-dimensional plane.

If most of the data's variation lies near that plane, the resulting two-dimensional representation may preserve much of the original structure.

PCA is particularly useful for numerical feature compression, exploratory data analysis, and visualization.

Because PCA maximizes retained variance rather than recommendation accuracy directly, the components with the greatest variance are not necessarily the most predictive for a downstream recommendation task.

### 3.5. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

**Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** is a fundamental matrix factorization technique in linear algebra.

For a matrix $A$, its SVD can be written as:

$$
A=U\Sigma V^{T}
$$

Where:

- $U$ contains the left singular vectors.
- $\Sigma$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values.
- $V$ contains the right singular vectors.

The singular values describe the relative importance of different directions in the matrix representation.

By retaining only the largest singular values and their corresponding singular vectors, we obtain a lower-rank approximation of the original matrix.

This is known as **Truncated SVD**.

SVD is closely related to PCA. In particular, PCA can be computed using SVD on an appropriately centered data matrix.

SVD is also related to matrix factorization methods used in recommender systems.

However, the SVD used in classical linear algebra should be distinguished from some recommendation algorithms informally called SVD, which learn latent factors from partially observed interaction matrices using optimization methods rather than directly applying a full matrix decomposition.

## 4. Association Rule Mining: Discovering Relationships Between Items

Recommendation systems frequently use observations such as:

> Customers who purchased bread also frequently purchased milk.

Or:

> Users who viewed this item also viewed another related item.

These patterns describe relationships between items that frequently occur together.

**Association Rule Mining** is a classical unsupervised learning technique designed to discover such relationships in large datasets.

It is particularly useful for transactional data, such as:

- Shopping baskets.
- Product purchase histories.
- Lists of movies watched by users.
- Groups of items viewed during browsing sessions.

The central objective is to discover rules of the form:

$$
A\rightarrow B
$$

Where $A$ and $B$ represent itemsets.

The rule suggests that transactions containing $A$ frequently also contain $B$.

For example, an association rule might indicate that users who watched one television series are also likely to watch another.

Such rules can help identify related content and support targeted recommendations.

Importantly, an association rule describes a statistical relationship, not necessarily a causal relationship.

### 4.1. Support

**Support** measures how frequently an itemset occurs in the transaction dataset.

For an itemset $A$:

$$
Support(A)=\frac{\text{Number of transactions containing }A}{\text{Total number of transactions}}
$$

For a rule $A\rightarrow B$, its support is the proportion of transactions containing both itemsets:

$$
Support(A\rightarrow B)=Support(A\cup B)
$$

Suppose a store has 1,000 transactions and 200 contain both bread and milk.

Then:

$$
Support(\text{Bread}\rightarrow\text{Milk})=\frac{200}{1000}=0.2
$$

The rule has a support of 20%.

Support is useful for identifying patterns that occur frequently enough to be meaningful for the application.

### 4.2. Confidence

**Confidence** measures the conditional frequency with which $B$ occurs among transactions containing $A$.

It is defined as:

$$
Confidence(A\rightarrow B)=\frac{Support(A\cup B)}{Support(A)}
$$

This corresponds to the empirical conditional probability:

$$
Confidence(A\rightarrow B)=P(B\mid A)
$$

Suppose 250 transactions contain bread, and 200 of those transactions also contain milk.

Then:

$$
Confidence(\text{Bread}\rightarrow\text{Milk})=\frac{200}{250}=0.8
$$

The rule has a confidence of 80%.

In other words, 80% of transactions containing bread also contain milk.

However, high confidence does not necessarily imply a strong association.

If milk is purchased in nearly every transaction, high confidence may simply reflect its overall popularity.

### 4.3. Lift

**Lift** measures the strength of an association relative to what would be expected if the two itemsets occurred independently.

It is defined as:

$$
Lift(A\rightarrow B)=\frac{Confidence(A\rightarrow B)}{Support(B)}
$$

Equivalently:

$$
Lift(A\rightarrow B)=\frac{Support(A\cup B)}{Support(A)\cdot Support(B)}
$$

Lift has three important interpretations:

- **Lift greater than 1:** The itemsets occur together more frequently than expected under independence.
- **Lift equal to 1:** Their co-occurrence is consistent with independence.
- **Lift less than 1:** They occur together less frequently than expected under independence.

For example, suppose the confidence of the rule Bread $\rightarrow$ Milk is 0.8, while the overall support of Milk is 0.5.

Then:

$$
Lift(\text{Bread}\rightarrow\text{Milk})=\frac{0.8}{0.5}=1.6
$$

This indicates that milk occurs 1.6 times as frequently among transactions containing bread as it does across all transactions.

Lift therefore provides additional information beyond confidence by accounting for the baseline frequency of the consequent itemset.

Nevertheless, a high lift value does not by itself establish causality or guarantee that a rule is reliable. Support and sample size must also be considered.

### 4.4. The Apriori Algorithm

**Apriori** is a classical algorithm for discovering frequent itemsets and generating association rules.

Its central principle is that if an itemset is frequent, all of its subsets must also be frequent.

Conversely, if an itemset is infrequent, none of its supersets can satisfy the same minimum support threshold.

This property allows Apriori to eliminate many candidate itemsets without evaluating every possible combination.

The algorithm generally proceeds as follows:

1. Identify individual items satisfying a minimum support threshold.
2. Generate larger candidate itemsets from the frequent itemsets found previously.
3. Count their occurrences in the transaction dataset.
4. Remove candidates that do not satisfy the minimum support threshold.
5. Repeat until no additional frequent itemsets can be discovered.
6. Generate association rules and evaluate them using metrics such as confidence and lift.

Association rules discovered through Apriori can be used to recommend complementary products, identify frequently co-consumed content, and support cross-selling strategies.

## 5. Anomaly Detection: Identifying Unusual Observations

In some recommendation environments, unusual interactions may indicate errors, fraudulent activity, or attempts to manipulate recommendation rankings.

Consider a movie that suddenly receives an unusually large number of extremely positive ratings.

At first glance, the movie may appear highly popular.

However, closer inspection might reveal that many ratings were submitted within a short period or originated from accounts exhibiting suspiciously similar behavior.

One possible explanation is an attempt to manipulate the recommendation system by artificially increasing the movie's apparent popularity.

For example, automated accounts might submit fraudulent ratings to make the movie more likely to appear in recommendations.

This type of manipulation is often associated with **Shilling Attacks** or **Profile Injection Attacks**.

### 5.1. What Is Anomaly Detection?

**Anomaly Detection** is the process of identifying observations or patterns that differ substantially from expected behavior.

These observations are called **Anomalies** or **Outliers**.

Anomalies may arise from:

- Data collection errors.
- Fraudulent activity.
- Malicious users.
- Unexpected behavioral changes.
- Legitimate but unusual events.

The objective is to identify observations that warrant further examination.

An anomaly is not necessarily evidence of fraud. A sudden increase in ratings might also result from a legitimate marketing campaign, a viral event, or renewed interest in a movie.

### 5.2. Applications in Recommender Systems

Two important applications are:

#### Detecting Fraudulent Ratings

Anomaly detection can help identify suspicious rating patterns.

Examples include:

- Large numbers of extreme ratings submitted within a short period.
- Multiple accounts exhibiting nearly identical rating behavior.
- Ratings that differ substantially from established interaction patterns.

Detecting such patterns can help protect recommendation quality from manipulation.

#### Identifying Malicious Users

Anomaly detection can also be used to identify accounts exhibiting unusual behavior.

Examples include:

- Excessive automated interactions.
- Coordinated rating activity.
- Abnormal browsing or purchasing patterns.
- Repeated attempts to influence item rankings.

These observations can be investigated further before corrective actions are taken.

Anomaly detection therefore contributes not only to recommendation quality but also to the reliability and security of the recommendation platform.

## 6. Why Use Unsupervised Learning?

Although many unsupervised learning techniques are based on relatively simple principles, they provide substantial practical value in recommender systems.

### 6.1. No Requirement for Target Labels

Many datasets contain observations without explicit target labels.

For example, browsing sessions and interaction logs may record what users viewed or clicked without providing explicit preference ratings.

Unsupervised learning can extract useful patterns from these observations without requiring a predefined prediction target.

It is important to distinguish between unlabeled data and implicit feedback: clicks and views can also serve as training signals in supervised or implicit-feedback recommendation models when an appropriate target is defined.

### 6.2. Mitigating Cold Start

Clustering can help assign new users or items to existing groups using the limited information available about them.

These group assignments can support initial recommendations before sufficient interaction history has accumulated.

### 6.3. Improving Recommendation Diversity

Item clustering can help increase the diversity of recommended lists.

For example, instead of selecting all recommendations from a single cluster of highly similar items, the system can include relevant items from several clusters.

This can expose users to a broader range of content and reduce excessive concentration on a narrow set of item types.

However, diversity must be balanced against relevance to avoid recommending items that are varied but uninteresting to the user.

### 6.4. Supporting Feature Engineering

Outputs from unsupervised learning algorithms can be incorporated into supervised recommendation models.

Examples include:

- Cluster membership labels.
- Distances to cluster centroids.
- Low-dimensional latent feature vectors.
- Dimensionality-reduced representations.

These derived features may provide useful information that is not directly represented by the original input variables.

For example, a Logistic Regression model could use a user's cluster membership as an additional feature when predicting purchase probability.

### 6.5. Discovering Hidden Patterns

Unsupervised learning can reveal structures that may not be immediately apparent through manual inspection.

Examples include:

- Previously unknown customer segments.
- Groups of related products.
- Hidden relationships among user preferences.
- Unusual interaction patterns.
- Frequently co-occurring items.

These findings can support recommendation design, business analysis, and further machine learning development.

## 7. Limitations and Challenges of Unsupervised Learning

Despite its advantages, unsupervised learning introduces several important challenges.

### 7.1. Difficulty of Objective Evaluation

In supervised learning, predictions can be compared with known target values.

For example, a rating prediction model can be evaluated using RMSE because the actual ratings are available.

In unsupervised learning, there may be no ground-truth labels against which discovered patterns can be compared.

This makes evaluation more difficult.

For clustering, an important question is:

**What constitutes a good cluster?**

A mathematically compact cluster is not necessarily useful for generating recommendations.

Internal clustering metrics, such as the Silhouette Score, can evaluate certain structural properties, but they do not automatically establish the practical usefulness of the clusters.

Ultimately, the value of unsupervised representations may need to be assessed through downstream recommendation performance.

### 7.2. Hyperparameter Selection

Many unsupervised learning algorithms require hyperparameters that are not automatically determined from the data.

For example, K-Means requires the number of clusters, $k$, to be specified in advance.

Different values of $k$ may produce substantially different segmentations.

Likewise, dimensionality reduction methods often require selecting the number of dimensions to retain.

These choices influence the complexity and usefulness of the resulting representations.

Hyperparameters should therefore be selected using appropriate evaluation criteria and the requirements of the intended application.

### 7.3. Interpretability of Discovered Patterns

Unsupervised algorithms may discover structures that are difficult to interpret.

For example, a clustering algorithm may divide users into several groups without providing meaningful descriptions of those groups.

Similarly, latent features produced by matrix factorization or dimensionality reduction may not correspond directly to recognizable concepts such as genre, price sensitivity, or product category.

Additional analysis may be necessary to understand the characteristics of each cluster or latent dimension.

Interpretability becomes particularly important when the discovered representations are used to guide business decisions or explain recommendations to users.

Unsupervised learning also supports additional applications beyond those discussed here, including representation learning, behavioral pattern discovery, and the construction of candidate-generation components in large-scale recommendation pipelines.
