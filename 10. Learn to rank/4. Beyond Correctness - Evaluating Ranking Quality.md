In the previous sections, we examined the fundamental principles and approaches used to develop Learning-to-Rank (LTR) models. We introduced the Pointwise, Pairwise, and Listwise approaches and explored how each formulates the ranking problem.

Now suppose three teams in a company have developed three different ranking models: one using Pointwise learning, another using Pairwise learning, and the third using Listwise learning.

An important question arises: **Which model performs best?**

More specifically, how can we objectively measure the quality of an ordered recommendation list?

Answering this question requires **Ranking Evaluation Metrics**, which are specifically designed to assess the effectiveness of ranking models.

## 4.1. Why Are Traditional Metrics Such as Accuracy and RMSE Insufficient?

In classification problems, we commonly evaluate models using metrics such as **Accuracy**. In regression problems, we frequently use metrics such as **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)**.

Although these metrics are useful for their respective prediction tasks, they do not necessarily reflect the quality of an ordered recommendation list.

Consider the following example.

### 4.1.1. Example: Prediction Accuracy Versus Ranking Quality

Suppose a user has rated three movies:

- **Movie A:** 5 stars, indicating excellent relevance.
- **Movie B:** 4 stars, indicating good relevance.
- **Movie C:** 1 star, indicating poor relevance.

The ideal recommendation order is:

`[A, B, C]`

Now consider two Pointwise recommendation models that predict the user's ratings.

| Movie | Actual Rating | Model 1 Prediction | Model 2 Prediction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 5 | 4.8 | 4.6 |
| B | 4 | 3.9 | 4.7 |
| C | 1 | 1.2 | 1.1 |
| **RMSE** | | **0.173** | **0.469** |
| **Predicted Ranking** | `[A, B, C]` | `[A, B, C]` | `[B, A, C]` |

Model 1 produces the correct ranking, placing Movie A above Movie B and Movie C.

Model 2 incorrectly places Movie B above Movie A.

For the predictions shown, Model 1 also has the lower RMSE. Therefore, these particular numerical values do not demonstrate the intended disagreement between rating prediction accuracy and ranking quality.

Nevertheless, the underlying distinction remains important: **a model with lower numerical prediction error can still produce a worse ranking**.

This is because RMSE measures the magnitude of prediction errors, whereas ranking quality depends on the relative ordering of items.

A ranking metric must therefore account for two important properties.

**1. Ranking Position**

Placing a highly relevant item in the first position is not equivalent to placing it in the tenth position.

Items appearing near the beginning of a recommendation list generally receive greater attention.

**2. Graded Relevance**

Not all relevant items are equally valuable.

For example, an item with a relevance grade of 5 may be substantially more useful than an item with a relevance grade of 3.

A suitable ranking metric should ideally account for both the positions and relevance levels of recommended items.

## 4.2. Ranking Evaluation Metrics

To compare different ranking metrics, we will use a consistent example throughout this section.

Suppose an LTR model generates the following recommendation list containing five movies.

Human evaluators have assigned each movie a relevance grade between 0 and 3:

- **0:** Irrelevant.
- **1:** Slightly relevant.
- **2:** Relevant.
- **3:** Highly relevant.

| Rank | Movie | Actual Relevance |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | A | 3 |
| 2 | B | 0 |
| 3 | C | 2 |
| 4 | D | 3 |
| 5 | E | 0 |

The model's predicted ranking is:

`[A, B, C, D, E]`

We will evaluate this list using three metrics:

1. Precision@K.
2. Mean Average Precision (MAP).
3. Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG).

## 4.3. Precision@K: Precision Among the Top K Results

**Precision@K** is one of the simplest and most interpretable ranking evaluation metrics.

It answers the following question:

> What proportion of the first K recommended items are relevant?

This metric is particularly useful when the primary objective is to evaluate the relevance of items displayed in a limited number of prominent positions.

For example, an e-commerce platform may want to know what percentage of the first ten recommended products are relevant to a user.

### 4.3.1. Mathematical Definition

Precision@K is defined as:

$$
Precision@K=\frac{\text{Number of relevant items among the top }K\text{ results}}{K}
$$

Before calculating this metric, we must define what constitutes a relevant item.

For our example, we assume that an item is relevant whenever its relevance grade is greater than zero.

Thus:

- Movie A is relevant.
- Movie B is irrelevant.
- Movie C is relevant.
- Movie D is relevant.
- Movie E is irrelevant.

### 4.3.2. Calculating Precision@1

The first recommendation is Movie A, which has a relevance grade of 3.

Therefore, the first item is relevant.

$$
Precision@1=\frac{1}{1}=1.0
$$

The result indicates that 100% of the first recommendation is relevant.

### 4.3.3. Calculating Precision@3

The first three recommendations are:

`[A, B, C]`

Among these items:

- Movie A is relevant.
- Movie B is irrelevant.
- Movie C is relevant.

Therefore, two of the first three recommendations are relevant.

$$
Precision@3=\frac{2}{3}\approx 0.667
$$

Approximately 66.7% of the first three recommendations are relevant.

### 4.3.4. Calculating Precision@5

The complete recommendation list contains three relevant movies: A, C, and D.

Therefore:

$$
Precision@5=\frac{3}{5}=0.6
$$

The result indicates that 60% of the five recommended movies are relevant.

### 4.3.5. Advantages of Precision@K

The principal advantage of Precision@K is its simplicity.

It provides a direct and easily interpretable measure of the proportion of relevant items within the first K recommendations.

This makes it particularly suitable for:

- Product performance reports.
- High-level recommendation quality monitoring.
- Communication with nontechnical stakeholders.
- Evaluating the relevance of items displayed on the first page.

### 4.3.6. Limitations of Precision@K

Despite its simplicity, Precision@K has two important limitations.

#### Limitation 1: Insensitivity to Ordering Within the Top K

Consider two recommendation lists:

**List 1:** `[Relevant, Relevant, Irrelevant]`

**List 2:** `[Irrelevant, Relevant, Relevant]`

Both lists have the same Precision@3:

$$
Precision@3=\frac{2}{3}
$$

However, List 1 is generally preferable because it places relevant items in more prominent positions.

Precision@K cannot distinguish between these two rankings because it considers only the number of relevant items within the first K positions.

#### Limitation 2: Insensitivity to Relevance Grades

Precision@K treats all relevant items equally.

For example, an item with relevance grade 3 and an item with relevance grade 1 both contribute one relevant item to the numerator.

Consequently, Precision@K does not distinguish between highly relevant and slightly relevant recommendations.

## 4.4. Mean Average Precision (MAP)

**Mean Average Precision (MAP)** is a ranking metric designed to account for the positions of relevant items.

Unlike Precision@K, MAP rewards rankings that place relevant items earlier in the recommendation list.

MAP is calculated by averaging **Average Precision (AP)** across multiple queries or users.

To understand MAP, we must first examine Average Precision.

### 4.4.1. Average Precision (AP)

Average Precision evaluates a single ranked list.

The calculation proceeds as follows:

1. Identify the positions containing relevant items.
2. Calculate Precision@K at each of those positions.
3. Average the resulting precision values.

A standard definition is:

$$
AP=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Precision@k\cdot rel(k)
$$

Where:

- $N$ is the number of evaluated results.
- $R$ is the total number of relevant items for the query.
- $rel(k)$ equals 1 if the item at rank $k$ is relevant and 0 otherwise.
- $Precision@k$ is the precision among the first $k$ results.

When the evaluated list contains all relevant items, this formula is equivalent to averaging Precision@K over the positions of relevant items.

If evaluation is truncated and additional relevant items exist outside the evaluated list, the denominator should still account for the relevant items defined by the evaluation protocol.

### 4.4.2. Calculating AP for the Example

Recall the recommendation list:

| Rank | Movie | Relevance | Relevant? |
|---:|---|---:|---|
| 1 | A | 3 | Yes |
| 2 | B | 0 | No |
| 3 | C | 2 | Yes |
| 4 | D | 3 | Yes |
| 5 | E | 0 | No |

Relevant items occur at ranks 1, 3, and 4.

**Step 1: Precision at Rank 1**

The first item is relevant.

$$
Precision@1=\frac{1}{1}=1.0
$$

**Step 2: Precision at Rank 3**

Two of the first three items are relevant.

$$
Precision@3=\frac{2}{3}\approx 0.667
$$

**Step 3: Precision at Rank 4**

Three of the first four items are relevant.

$$
Precision@4=\frac{3}{4}=0.75
$$

**Step 4: Calculate Average Precision**

There are three relevant items in the evaluated collection.

Therefore:

$$
AP=\frac{1+\frac{2}{3}+\frac{3}{4}}{3}
$$

$$
AP=\frac{29}{36}\approx 0.806
$$

Thus, the Average Precision of the recommendation list is approximately **0.806**, or 80.6%.

### 4.4.3. From AP to MAP

Average Precision evaluates one query or recommendation list.

Mean Average Precision extends this calculation across multiple queries.

For $Q$ queries, MAP is defined as:

$$
MAP=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}AP(q)
$$

Where:

- $Q$ is the number of queries.
- $AP(q)$ is the Average Precision for query $q$.

For example, suppose a recommendation system is evaluated on three users with the following AP values:

| User | Average Precision |
|---|---:|
| User 1 | 0.90 |
| User 2 | 0.75 |
| User 3 | 0.80 |

Then:

$$
MAP=\frac{0.90+0.75+0.80}{3}\approx 0.817
$$

The system's MAP is approximately 0.817.

### 4.4.4. Advantages of MAP

The principal advantage of MAP is that it is sensitive to the ordering of relevant items.

A ranking that places relevant items near the beginning generally receives a higher AP than one that places those same items farther down.

This makes MAP more informative than Precision@K when the positions of relevant recommendations matter.

### 4.4.5. Limitations of MAP

MAP generally assumes binary relevance.

Items are classified as either relevant or irrelevant.

Consequently, MAP does not distinguish between different positive relevance grades.

For example, a movie with relevance grade 3 and a movie with relevance grade 2 are both treated as relevant.

This limitation becomes important when the evaluation dataset contains graded relevance judgments.

## 4.5. Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)

**Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)** is one of the most widely used metrics for evaluating ranked lists with graded relevance judgments.

It addresses two limitations of the previous metrics:

1. It accounts for the **degree of relevance** of each item.
2. It accounts for the **position** at which each item appears.

NDCG is based on two fundamental principles.

**Gain**

More relevant items provide greater value.

For example, an item with relevance grade 3 should contribute more to ranking quality than an item with relevance grade 1.

**Discount**

The value of a relevant item decreases as its ranking position becomes lower.

A highly relevant item appearing in the first position should generally contribute more than the same item appearing in the fifth position.

NDCG combines these principles to evaluate the quality of a ranked recommendation list.

### 4.5.1. Step One: Cumulative Gain (CG)

The simplest measure of total relevance is **Cumulative Gain (CG)**.

CG is calculated by summing the relevance grades of the first K items.

$$
CG@K=\sum_{i=1}^{K}rel_{i}
$$

Where $rel_{i}$ is the relevance grade of the item at position $i$.

For our example:

| Rank | Movie | Relevance |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | A | 3 |
| 2 | B | 0 |
| 3 | C | 2 |
| 4 | D | 3 |
| 5 | E | 0 |

The cumulative gain is:

$$
CG@5=3+0+2+3+0=8
$$

Therefore:

**CG@5 = 8**

However, CG does not consider the ordering of items.

Any permutation of these five movies produces the same cumulative gain.

Thus, CG alone is insufficient for evaluating ranking quality.

### 4.5.2. Step Two: Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)

**Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)** introduces a position-dependent discount.

The idea is to reduce the contribution of items appearing at lower ranks.

Using the linear-gain formulation presented here, DCG is defined as:

$$
DCG@K=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{rel_{i}}{\log_{2}(i+1)}
$$

Where:

- $K$ is the number of evaluated positions.
- $rel_{i}$ is the relevance grade at position $i$.
- $\log_{2}(i+1)$ is the logarithmic discount factor.

The discount factor increases with rank, reducing the contribution of items appearing farther down the list.

At rank 1:

$$
\log_{2}(1+1)=1
$$

Therefore, the first item receives no discount.

At rank 2:

$$
\log_{2}(2+1)\approx 1.585
$$

The contribution of the second item is divided by approximately 1.585.

At rank 3:

$$
\log_{2}(3+1)=2
$$

The contribution of the third item is divided by 2.

This pattern continues for subsequent positions.

### 4.5.3. Calculating DCG@5

Recall the relevance grades:

`[3, 0, 2, 3, 0]`

We calculate the discounted contribution of each item.

| Rank | Relevance | Discount Factor | Discounted Gain | Cumulative DCG |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 3 | 1.000 | 3.000 | 3.000 |
| 2 | 0 | 1.585 | 0.000 | 3.000 |
| 3 | 2 | 2.000 | 1.000 | 4.000 |
| 4 | 3 | 2.322 | 1.292 | 5.292 |
| 5 | 0 | 2.585 | 0.000 | 5.292 |

The complete calculation is:

$$
DCG@5=\frac{3}{\log_{2}(2)}+\frac{0}{\log_{2}(3)}+\frac{2}{\log_{2}(4)}+\frac{3}{\log_{2}(5)}+\frac{0}{\log_{2}(6)}
$$

$$
DCG@5\approx 5.292
$$

Therefore:

**DCG@5 ≈ 5.29**

This value reflects both the relevance grades and their positions.

However, DCG alone does not indicate how close the ranking is to the best possible ordering.

To make this comparison, we introduce Ideal Discounted Cumulative Gain.

### 4.5.4. Step Three: Ideal Discounted Cumulative Gain (IDCG)

**Ideal Discounted Cumulative Gain (IDCG)** is the maximum DCG achievable by arranging the available items in descending order of their actual relevance grades.

For our example, the original ranking is:

`[A(3), B(0), C(2), D(3), E(0)]`

The ideal ranking is:

`[A(3), D(3), C(2), B(0), E(0)]`

The corresponding relevance sequence is:

`[3, 3, 2, 0, 0]`

We now calculate DCG for this ideal ordering.

| Rank | Relevance | Discount Factor | Discounted Gain | Cumulative IDCG |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 3 | 1.000 | 3.000 | 3.000 |
| 2 | 3 | 1.585 | 1.893 | 4.893 |
| 3 | 2 | 2.000 | 1.000 | 5.893 |
| 4 | 0 | 2.322 | 0.000 | 5.893 |
| 5 | 0 | 2.585 | 0.000 | 5.893 |

The ideal discounted cumulative gain is:

$$
IDCG@5=\frac{3}{\log_{2}(2)}+\frac{3}{\log_{2}(3)}+\frac{2}{\log_{2}(4)}
$$

$$
IDCG@5\approx 5.893
$$

Therefore:

**IDCG@5 ≈ 5.89**

This represents the highest possible DCG for the five available movies under the selected gain and discount functions.

### 4.5.5. Step Four: Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain

NDCG is obtained by dividing the DCG of the predicted ranking by the DCG of the ideal ranking.

$$
NDCG@K=\frac{DCG@K}{IDCG@K}
$$

For our example:

$$
NDCG@5=\frac{5.292}{5.893}\approx 0.898
$$

Therefore:

**NDCG@5 ≈ 0.898**

The generated ranking achieves approximately 89.8% of the maximum discounted cumulative gain available for this set of items.

An NDCG value of 1 indicates an ideal ranking under the selected evaluation formulation.

A lower value indicates that the ranking differs from the ideal ordering.

For nonnegative relevance grades and a positive IDCG, NDCG lies between 0 and 1.

When IDCG equals zero, the evaluation protocol must define how that query is handled, commonly by assigning NDCG a value of zero or excluding the query from aggregation.

### 4.5.6. Advantages of NDCG

**Sensitivity to Ranking Positions**

NDCG assigns greater importance to relevant items appearing near the beginning of the list.

**Support for Graded Relevance**

Unlike Precision@K and conventional MAP, NDCG can distinguish between different levels of relevance.

For example, relevance grades of 1, 2, and 3 contribute different amounts of gain.

**Normalization**

Dividing by IDCG makes it possible to compare ranking quality across different queries or users, even when their ideal DCG values differ.

**Compatibility with Learning-to-Rank Objectives**

Many LTR methods are designed to optimize ranking objectives related to NDCG.

This makes NDCG particularly useful for evaluating models trained with ranking-oriented losses.

### 4.5.7. Limitations of NDCG

NDCG is more difficult to interpret than simpler metrics such as Precision@K.

Its value depends on several design choices, including:

- The relevance grading scale.
- The gain function.
- The position discount function.
- The cutoff value K.

Furthermore, different implementations may use different gain functions.

For example, a common alternative uses exponential gain:

$$
DCG@K=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{2^{rel_{i}}-1}{\log_{2}(i+1)}
$$

This formulation assigns disproportionately greater importance to highly relevant items.

Consequently, evaluation results should always be interpreted in the context of the chosen DCG definition.

NDCG also measures ranking quality relative to relevance labels. It does not automatically capture every aspect of user experience, such as recommendation diversity, novelty, fairness, or long-term satisfaction.

## 4.6. Comparison of Ranking Evaluation Metrics

The three metrics differ in their treatment of relevance grades and ranking positions.

| Metric | Supports Graded Relevance? | Sensitive to Item Ordering? | Primary Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Precision@K** | No | No, within the top K | Simple evaluation of the proportion of relevant recommendations |
| **MAP** | No | Yes | Evaluating rankings with binary relevance judgments |
| **NDCG** | Yes | Yes | Evaluating rankings with graded relevance and position-dependent importance |

### 4.6.1. Choosing an Appropriate Metric

The selection of an evaluation metric is an important part of developing a Learning-to-Rank system.

**Precision@K** is useful when the primary concern is the proportion of relevant items among the first K recommendations.

Its simplicity makes it appropriate for high-level reporting and straightforward relevance analysis.

**MAP** is useful when relevance judgments are binary and the objective is to reward systems that place relevant items earlier in the ranked list.

**NDCG** is particularly appropriate when relevance has multiple levels and the positions of highly relevant items are important.

Because NDCG incorporates both graded relevance and position-dependent discounts, it is widely used in Learning-to-Rank research and practice.

However, no single metric is universally optimal.

The most appropriate evaluation metric depends on the recommendation task, available relevance judgments, and the objectives of the system.

For example, a recommendation platform primarily concerned with purchase conversion may need additional evaluation measures beyond relevance-based ranking metrics.

Similarly, a content recommendation system may need to consider diversity, novelty, and long-term user satisfaction.

The central principle is that **the evaluation metric should reflect the behavior that the ranking system is intended to optimize**.

This alignment is particularly important for Listwise Learning-to-Rank methods, which use training objectives designed to approximate or improve list-level ranking metrics such as NDCG.
