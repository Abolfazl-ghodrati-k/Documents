In the previous section, we introduced **Learning to Rank (LTR)** and examined its role in the architecture of modern recommender systems. We learned that an LTR model acts as a specialized ranking component, transforming an initial collection of candidate items into a final, optimized recommendation list.

However, an important question remains: **Where does a ranking model acquire the information needed to make effective ranking decisions?**

Regardless of how sophisticated its underlying algorithm may be, an LTR model cannot produce reliable rankings without informative training data and meaningful features.

In practice, the success of an LTR system often depends as much on **data preparation and feature engineering** as on the selection of the learning algorithm itself.

The objective of LTR is to rank items, such as products, movies, or documents, according to their relevance to a particular user or query. To accomplish this, the model requires information describing users, items, their relationships, and the relevance or preference signals associated with their interactions.

## 2.1. Categories of Training Data for Learning to Rank

Before constructing an LTR training dataset, we must understand the types of information available in recommender systems.

User preferences and relevance signals are commonly represented through two forms of feedback: **explicit feedback** and **implicit feedback**. In addition, recommendation tasks may operate without an explicit search query, requiring the user and their current context to define the ranking request.

### 2.1.1. Explicit Feedback Data

Explicit feedback consists of information that users deliberately provide to express their preferences or opinions about items.

Common examples include:

- Star ratings, such as assigning a movie a score between 1 and 5.
- Likes and dislikes.
- Explicit product reviews.
- Direct preference selections.

Explicit feedback provides a relatively direct indication of user preferences because users consciously express their evaluations.

A typical explicit-feedback record can be represented as:

`(user_id, item_id, rating)`

When additional information is available, the record may also contain contextual attributes or features:

`(user_id, item_id, features, rating)`

For example:

| User ID | Movie ID | Genre | Rating |
|---|---|---|---:|
| 123 | A | Action | 5 |
| 123 | B | Comedy | 3 |
| 123 | C | Drama | 1 |

These ratings indicate that User 123 preferred Movie A over Movies B and C.

An LTR model can use this information to learn the relative ordering of movies for that user.

Although explicit feedback is informative, it has several limitations. Users may rate only a small fraction of the items they consume, different users may interpret rating scales differently, and ratings may be influenced by individual biases.

Consequently, explicit feedback is often sparse and may not fully represent users' actual behavior.

### 2.1.2. Implicit Feedback Data

Implicit feedback is derived from users' observed behavior rather than from direct evaluations.

Examples include:

- Clicking a product.
- Watching a video.
- Listening to a song until completion.
- Adding an item to a shopping cart.
- Purchasing a product.
- Saving or sharing content.

In these situations, the system does not directly ask users whether they like an item. Instead, it infers preferences from their interactions.

Implicit-feedback data is commonly stored in event logs with a structure such as:

`(user_id, item_id, interaction_type, context)`

For example:

| User ID | Item ID | Interaction | Device |
|---|---|---|---|
| 123 | Movie A | Watch | Smart TV |
| 123 | Movie B | Click | Mobile |
| 456 | Movie C | Like | Desktop |

Implicit feedback is particularly valuable because large-scale platforms continuously collect substantial quantities of behavioral data.

However, implicit signals must be interpreted carefully.

A click does not necessarily indicate satisfaction, and the absence of an interaction does not necessarily imply that a user dislikes an item. The user may simply never have encountered it.

Furthermore, different interactions may indicate different levels of interest. For example, completing a movie may provide a stronger positive signal than merely clicking its thumbnail.

These considerations become important when constructing relevance labels for ranking models.

### 2.1.3. Recommendation Data Without an Explicit Query

In traditional information retrieval, ranking is usually performed in response to an explicit query.

For example, a user enters a search phrase, and the system ranks the documents relevant to that phrase.

However, many recommender systems operate without an explicit search query.

Examples include:

- The Netflix home page.
- The Instagram feed.
- Personalized product recommendations on an e-commerce home page.
- Automatically generated music recommendations.

In these scenarios, **the user and their current context collectively define the query**.

Instead of responding to a search phrase, the system attempts to answer:

> Given everything currently known about this user, which items should be displayed, and in what order?

The ranking context may include:

- User identity.
- Historical interactions.
- Recent preferences.
- Time of day.
- Geographic location.
- Device type.
- Current session activity.

For example, a user may prefer documentaries during weekday evenings but watch comedies on weekends.

An effective ranking model should be able to incorporate such contextual differences.

This is not a separate feedback mechanism. Rather, it is a particular formulation of the ranking problem in which the query is constructed from user and contextual information, while relevance labels may still originate from explicit or implicit feedback.

## 2.2. From Raw Interactions to Structured Training Data

The development of an LTR training dataset typically begins with **User Interaction Logs**.

These logs record events generated when users interact with a platform.

A raw event might look like the following:

```none
{ "timestamp": "2025-06-17T09:00:00Z", "user_id": "user-123", "item_id": "movie-857", "action": "watch", "duration_watched": 5400, "device": "SmartTV" }
```

This event indicates that:

- The interaction occurred on June 17, 2025, at 09:00 UTC.
- The user identifier was `user-123`.
- The item identifier was `movie-857`.
- The recorded action was `watch`.
- The user watched the movie for 5,400 seconds, equivalent to 90 minutes.
- The interaction occurred on a Smart TV.

Although this information is useful, a raw event is not necessarily a complete training example for an LTR model.

The data must first be transformed into a structured representation that includes features and a meaningful relevance label.

### 2.2.1. Constructing Relevance Labels

One of the most important steps in preparing LTR data is defining the **Relevance Label**.

A relevance label indicates how appropriate or valuable an item was for a particular user or query.

When explicit ratings are available, they may be used directly or converted into discrete relevance grades.

However, explicit ratings are often unavailable in production recommender systems.

In such cases, relevance labels must be derived from implicit interactions.

For example, a video recommendation system might distinguish between:

| Observed Behavior | Possible Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Item displayed but not clicked | No observed engagement |
| Item clicked | Initial interest |
| Video partially watched | Some engagement |
| Video watched to completion | Stronger engagement |
| Video explicitly liked | Positive preference |

These interpretations can guide the construction of binary or graded relevance labels.

The precise mapping depends on the application and the behavior being optimized.

For example, watching a short video to completion may be less informative than watching a long video to completion. Therefore, the interpretation of watch duration should consider the video's total length.

It is also important to distinguish between items that were displayed but not selected and items that were never displayed. The latter should not automatically be treated as negative examples.

### 2.2.2. Organizing Training Examples into Ranking Groups

LTR datasets differ from conventional classification and regression datasets because ranking is performed over collections of candidate items.

Each training example generally contains:

- A query or ranking-request identifier.
- A candidate item.
- A feature vector describing the candidate in the query context.
- A relevance label.

For example:

| Query ID | User ID | Item ID | Feature Vector | Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| Q1 | 123 | Movie A | Features A | 3 |
| Q1 | 123 | Movie B | Features B | 1 |
| Q1 | 123 | Movie C | Features C | 0 |
| Q2 | 456 | Movie A | Features D | 0 |
| Q2 | 456 | Movie D | Features E | 2 |

The items associated with `Q1` form one ranking group, while the items associated with `Q2` form another.

The model learns how to order candidates within each group according to their relevance.

This grouping becomes especially important for Pairwise and Listwise Learning-to-Rank approaches, which explicitly consider relationships among items belonging to the same ranking request.

## 2.3. Feature Engineering: Transforming Data into Knowledge

After identifying the available data and defining relevance labels, the next step is to convert raw information into features that machine learning algorithms can process.

This process is known as **Feature Engineering**.

Feature engineering is one of the most influential stages in developing an LTR model because the model's ability to distinguish between relevant and irrelevant items depends heavily on the information represented by its input features.

LTR features can be organized into three principal categories:

1. User features.
2. Item features.
3. Interaction and contextual features.

### 2.3.1. User Features: Who Is the User?

User features describe the characteristics, preferences, and historical behavior of the individual receiving recommendations.

They can be divided into several groups.

#### Static User Features

Static features represent attributes that remain relatively stable over time.

Examples include:

- `age`: The user's age.
- `gender`: The user's gender, when available and appropriate to use.
- `country`: The user's country.
- `language`: The user's preferred language.
- `subscription_level`: The user's subscription tier, such as Free, Silver, or Gold.

These features provide general information about the user.

However, demographic characteristics alone rarely capture the full complexity of individual preferences.

#### Behavioral User Features

Behavioral features summarize historical interactions and can provide a more direct representation of user interests.

**Activity Features**

Examples include:

- Number of visits during the previous month.
- Average session duration.
- Number of interactions during the previous week.
- Frequency of purchases or content consumption.

These features describe how actively a user interacts with the platform.

**Content Preference Features**

Examples include:

- Favorite movie genre, determined by total viewing time.
- Most frequently purchased brand.
- Preferred product categories.
- Historical engagement with particular content types.

These features help identify recurring patterns in the user's preferences.

**Temporal Behavior Features**

Examples include:

- Typical hours of activity.
- Preference for morning, afternoon, or evening usage.
- Higher activity during weekends or weekdays.

Such features allow the model to capture patterns associated with the user's habitual behavior.

#### User Embeddings

A **User Embedding** is a numerical vector representing a user in a latent feature space.

User embeddings can be obtained from previously trained recommendation models, including collaborative filtering and matrix factorization methods.

For example, a matrix factorization model may represent each user using a vector of latent factors.

These factors capture patterns in the user's interactions that may not correspond directly to explicit attributes such as age or favorite genre.

The resulting embedding can be incorporated into an LTR model as an additional feature representation.

This allows the ranking model to benefit from preference information learned by earlier recommendation components.

### 2.3.2. Item Features: What Is the Item?

Item features describe the characteristics of the products, movies, documents, or other entities being ranked.

These features allow the ranking model to distinguish between candidates based on their intrinsic properties and historical performance.

#### Content Features

Content features describe the semantic or descriptive characteristics of an item.

Examples include:

- `category`: Product category or content genre.
- `brand`: Product brand.
- `release_year`: Year of production or publication.
- `director`: Movie director.
- `actors`: Movie cast.
- Keywords appearing in the item's title or description.

For example, a movie may be represented by its genres, production year, cast, and textual description.

These features help the model learn relationships between item characteristics and user preferences.

#### Popularity Features

Popularity features summarize how frequently users interact with an item.

Examples include:

- Number of views during the previous 24 hours.
- Total number of purchases.
- Global click-through rate.
- Average user rating.

The **Global Click-Through Rate (CTR)** measures the proportion of recorded impressions that result in clicks.

For example, an item receiving 200 clicks from 2,000 impressions has a CTR of 10%.

Popularity features can provide useful information about general user interest.

However, popularity does not necessarily imply relevance to a particular user. Therefore, these features are most useful when combined with personalized information.

#### Monetary Features

For commercial recommendation systems, monetary attributes may be important.

Examples include:

- `price`: The item's price.
- `discount_percentage`: The percentage discount currently offered.

Such features can help the ranking model identify products that are compatible with a user's purchasing preferences.

#### Item Embeddings

An **Item Embedding** represents an item as a numerical vector in a latent feature space.

Like user embeddings, item embeddings may be obtained from matrix factorization or other representation-learning methods.

These vectors can encode relationships among items based on their interaction patterns or content characteristics.

For example, two movies frequently preferred by similar users may have nearby representations in the latent space.

Item embeddings can be used directly as LTR features or combined with user embeddings to construct interaction features.

### 2.3.3. Interaction and Contextual Features

Interaction and contextual features describe the relationship between a particular user and a particular item under the current circumstances.

These features are especially valuable because relevance is rarely determined by user characteristics or item characteristics in isolation.

Instead, it depends on how well the item matches the user's preferences at a particular moment.

#### Contextual Features

Contextual features describe the conditions under which a recommendation is generated.

Examples include:

**Time of Day**

A user's preferences may differ between morning and evening.

For example, a user might prefer short videos during a morning commute but full-length movies in the evening.

**Day of the Week**

Users may exhibit different consumption patterns on weekdays and weekends.

**Device Type**

A user accessing a platform through a mobile phone may prefer different content from the same user accessing it through a Smart TV.

For example, short videos may be more convenient on a mobile device, while full-length movies may be more suitable for television viewing.

These features allow ranking models to account for situational differences in user behavior.

#### Cross-Features

**Cross-Features** are constructed by combining information about the user and the candidate item.

They explicitly describe relationships that cannot be represented adequately by considering user and item attributes independently.

Several examples illustrate their importance.

**Example 1: User–Genre Affinity**

Consider the feature:

`number_of_movies_watched_by_user_in_candidate_genre_last_month`

This feature measures how many movies belonging to the candidate item's genre the user watched during the previous month.

For example, if a candidate movie belongs to the action genre and the user recently watched 15 action movies, the feature value would be 15.

This provides an indication of whether the candidate matches the user's recent interests.

**Example 2: Previous Brand Purchases**

Consider the binary feature:

`has_user_purchased_from_this_brand_before`

Possible values are:

- `1`: The user has previously purchased a product from the candidate item's brand.
- `0`: No previous purchase from that brand has been recorded.

This feature captures an aspect of the user's historical relationship with the brand.

**Example 3: Price Compatibility**

Consider the feature:

`candidate_price_minus_user_average_purchase_price`

This measures the difference between the candidate item's price and the user's average historical purchase price.

For example, if a user's average purchase price is $50 and the candidate product costs $65, the feature value is $15.

The feature helps the model learn whether a product falls within the user's typical purchasing range.

**Example 4: Embedding Similarity**

A particularly useful cross-feature is the **Cosine Similarity** between the user embedding and the item embedding.

Let the user embedding be represented by vector $\mathbf{p}_{u}$ and the item embedding by vector $\mathbf{q}_{i}$.

Their cosine similarity is:

$$
Sim(u,i)=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{i}}{\lVert\mathbf{p}_{u}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{q}_{i}\rVert}
$$

This measure captures the angular similarity between the two vectors.

A larger cosine similarity generally indicates stronger alignment between the user's latent preferences and the item's latent representation.

However, its interpretation depends on how the embeddings were trained.

For cosine similarity to be computed directly, both embeddings must belong to compatible vector spaces with the same dimensionality.

This feature allows an LTR model to incorporate preference relationships learned by a collaborative filtering or representation-learning model.

## 2.4. Constructing the Final Feature Vector

After extracting the relevant features, they must be combined into a numerical representation suitable for the ranking model.

For each user–item pair, the system constructs a **Feature Vector** containing the values of the selected features.

Consider a recommendation task in which we want to determine whether **Movie A** is a suitable recommendation for **User 123**.

Suppose the user previously liked the movie, so the observed relevance label is `1`.

The training example is conceptually represented as:

`(User 123, Movie A, Relevance Label = 1)`

The associated features may include:

| Feature | Value | Description |
|---|---:|---|
| User age | 34 | Age of User 123 |
| Action movie viewing ratio | 0.80 | Proportion of the user's watched movies belonging to the action genre |
| Movie release year | 2022 | Production year of Movie A |
| Global average rating | 4.5 | Average rating assigned to the movie |
| Action movies watched | 15 | Number of action movies previously watched by the user |
| User–item cosine similarity | 0.92 | Similarity between the user's and movie's latent representations |
| Is weekend | 1 | Indicates that the recommendation context corresponds to a weekend |

The resulting numerical feature vector can be represented as:

`[34, 0.80, 2022, 4.5, 15, 0.92, 1, ...]`

The ellipsis indicates that the complete representation may contain dozens or hundreds of additional features.

A similar feature vector is constructed for every candidate item associated with a ranking request.

The training dataset therefore consists of candidate items represented by feature vectors, grouped according to their corresponding users or queries, together with their relevance labels.

The LTR model uses these representations to learn how feature patterns relate to item relevance and relative ordering.

At prediction time, the trained model evaluates the feature vectors of the available candidate items and produces the information required to rank them.

Depending on the learning approach, this may involve predicting an individual relevance score, estimating pairwise preferences, or optimizing the ordering of an entire candidate list.

These alternative formulations form the basis of the Pointwise, Pairwise, and Listwise Learning-to-Rank methods examined in the following sections.
