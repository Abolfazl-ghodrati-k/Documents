## A Need for a New Approach

Return to the earlier example: suppose you enjoy science-fiction films. A content-based system recommends more science-fiction films, while UBCF recommends films liked by users whose ratings resemble yours. Both approaches can be useful, but UBCF faces important practical challenges. There may be an enormous number of users whose preferences change continually, making it computationally expensive to calculate and maintain similarity among millions of users. In addition, when a new film enters the system, UBCF may struggle to recommend it until enough like-minded users have interacted with it; this is the item cold-start problem.

Now consider a very large online store such as Amazon, with millions of users and billions of products. Users continually change their interests and purchase diverse products. Finding similar users for every user, and continually updating those relationships at this scale, becomes a computational burden. More importantly, is it essential that Ali's preferences resemble Mohammad's? Or is the more useful relationship that, if Ali views a particular mobile phone, he may also be interested in a protective case for that phone? This is a relationship between two items—the phone and the case—regardless of which users purchased them.

This is where **Item-Based Collaborative Filtering (IBCF)** changes the perspective. Instead of asking, “Which users are similar to this user?”, it asks, “Which items are similar to this item?”

## Introducing Item-Based Collaborative Filtering

Item-Based Collaborative Filtering is a form of collaborative filtering that focuses on similarity among items rather than similarity among users. The core idea is that if a user has shown interest in, or interacted with, an item—for example, by purchasing it, assigning it a high rating, or viewing it—the user may also be interested in items similar to that item. Item similarity is inferred from historical patterns of user interaction.

### How IBCF Works

An IBCF workflow usually consists of the following stages:

1. **Construct a user-item matrix.** As in UBCF, we create a matrix that records user interactions with items, including ratings, purchases, clicks, or other signals. This matrix is the raw input.
2. **Compute an item-item similarity matrix.** This is the central stage. From the user-item matrix, the system calculates similarity for each item pair. Two items are considered similar when they are associated with similar patterns of user interaction. Similarity may be based on co-occurrence—for example, how many users purchased or rated both items—or on similarity between their rating vectors across users.
3. **Generate recommendations for a target user.** For a particular user, the system considers items with which that user has already interacted, such as highly rated or purchased items. It then uses the item-item similarity matrix to find items most similar to those previously preferred items. Unseen items with the highest resulting scores are recommended.

## Advantages of IBCF Compared with UBCF

IBCF offers important advantages over UBCF, particularly for large-scale and dynamic data.

### 1. Better Scalability

This is IBCF's primary advantage. In UBCF, we calculate similarity among $N$ users, which has all-pairs complexity on the order of:

$$O(N^{2})$$

In IBCF, we calculate similarity among $M$ items, with all-pairs complexity on the order of:

$$O(M^{2})$$

In many recommender systems, the number of users $N$ is much greater than the number of items $M$:

$$N\gg M$$

Consequently, $O(M^{2})$ can be substantially smaller and more manageable than $O(N^{2})$. This can make IBCF more computationally efficient on platforms with millions of users. The advantage depends on the sizes of the user base and catalog; when the item catalog is extremely large, item-item computation also requires careful optimization.

### 2. More Stable Similarities and Less Frequent Updating

The relationship between two items—for example, two books by the same author and genre, or two complementary tools—is often more stable over time than an individual user's preferences.

This stability means that the item-item similarity matrix may not require frequent recomputation. The matrix can often be calculated offline at longer intervals, such as weekly or monthly, and its results can be stored. Although a person's musical taste may differ from one day to the next, the relationship between two tracks from the same album or between two related products in a category often changes more slowly. Because the costly computation is performed in advance, the system can produce online recommendations much more quickly.

Item similarity is not permanently fixed: trends, seasonality, changing inventories, and new interaction data can alter item relationships. The appropriate update frequency should therefore be determined empirically.

### 3. Better Adaptation to Dynamic User Preferences in Real Time

IBCF naturally accommodates changing user preferences. If a user who previously bought science books begins to purchase gardening tools, the system can recommend items similar to the gardening tools based on precomputed item similarities. It does not need to rebuild a user-user neighborhood before responding to the new behavioral signal.

### 4. Earlier Incorporation of New Items Relative to UBCF

In UBCF, a new item can be difficult to recommend until it has been rated or interacted with by enough like-minded users. In IBCF, once a small number of users interact with a new item in patterns similar to their interactions with existing items—for example, users who viewed item A also view new item B—the system can begin estimating item B's similarity to existing items.

This does not eliminate item cold start: an item with no interactions still lacks collaborative evidence. Nevertheless, IBCF can often incorporate an item earlier than UBCF once initial interactions are available. Content-based features and exploration strategies remain useful for completely new items.

### 5. Relatively Easy-to-Understand Recommendations

Explanations can improve user experience. In IBCF, an explanation is often intuitive:

> You liked item A, and item B is similar to item A; therefore, item B is recommended.

## The User-Item Matrix

As noted earlier, the starting point for both IBCF and UBCF is the **user-item matrix**. This matrix is a structured representation of interactions between users and items. Its rows usually represent users, and its columns represent items or products.

The entry at position $(u,i)$ represents the type and strength of user $u$'s interaction with item $i$. It may represent explicit feedback or implicit feedback.

Suppose we have four users—Ali, Sara, Reza, and Maryam—and five films. Users have rated the films on a scale where $1$ means very poor and $5$ means excellent. A value of $0$ in this illustrative table means that the user did not rate or watch the film.

| User | Film 1 | Film 2 | Film 3 | Film 4 | Film 5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Ali | 5 | 4 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| Sara | 4 | 0 | 5 | 0 | 4 |
| Reza | 0 | 5 | 4 | 0 | 5 |
| Maryam | 2 | 2 | 0 | 3 | 0 |

This matrix is generally sparse: most cells are zero or missing because each user interacts with only a small subset of the available items. In a real rating dataset, an unobserved rating should be represented as missing rather than interpreted as an actual rating of zero. The zero values in this table are only placeholders for unobserved interactions.

## The Core of IBCF: The Item-Item Matrix

After constructing the user-item matrix, the fundamental step in IBCF is to construct the **item-item similarity matrix**. This matrix indicates how similar every pair of items is according to user-interaction patterns.

Both its rows and columns are items. The entry at position $(i,j)$ represents the similarity between items $i$ and $j$. This value is often normalized to the interval $[0,1]$, although measures such as Pearson correlation can range from $-1$ to $1$. A value of $1$ represents complete similarity under the chosen measure.

The system first measures the similarity between items using an appropriate similarity measure; it can then construct the item-item matrix. For the user-item matrix above, an illustrative item-item matrix might be as follows. These values are examples only and may differ from values obtained through an actual calculation.

|  | Film 1 | Film 2 | Film 3 | Film 4 | Film 5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Film 1** | 1 | 0.7 | 0.2 | 0.8 | 0.1 |
| **Film 2** | 0.7 | 1 | 0.9 | 0.3 | 0.85 |
| **Film 3** | 0.2 | 0.9 | 1 | 0.15 | 0.95 |
| **Film 4** | 0.8 | 0.3 | 0.15 | 1 | 0.05 |
| **Film 5** | 0.1 | 0.85 | 0.95 | 0.05 | 1 |

By focusing on relatively stable and scalable relationships among items, IBCF addresses many of UBCF's fundamental difficulties in large and dynamic systems. The following sections examine item-similarity computation, IBCF implementations for different data types, and the subtleties behind the apparent simplicity of item similarity.

## Summary

IBCF recommends unseen items by using relationships among items rather than searching for similar users. Its central artifact is the item-item similarity matrix, which can often be computed offline and reused for fast online recommendations. Its practical benefits include improved scalability, relatively stable similarity relationships, responsiveness to new user interactions, and straightforward explanations. It still depends on interaction data and therefore does not fully solve cold start for items with no observed feedback.
