In the previous section, we introduced deep learning and examined why it can improve the performance of recommender systems. We now turn to one of the simplest yet most powerful architectures in deep learning: the **Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)**.

This section introduces the fundamental structure and operation of MLPs and explains how they can be applied to recommendation problems, particularly through **Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF)**.

## 3.1. An Overview of Multilayer Perceptrons

A **Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)** is a type of **feedforward neural network** consisting of three principal components:

1. **Input Layer:** Receives the input features.
2. **Hidden Layers:** Perform successive transformations of the input representations.
3. **Output Layer:** Produces the final prediction.

An MLP contains one or more hidden layers, each consisting of multiple computational units called *neurons*.

Each neuron computes a weighted combination of its inputs, adds a bias term, and applies an activation function to produce its output.

Common activation functions include:

- **ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit)**
- **Sigmoid**
- **Hyperbolic Tangent (tanh)**

For a neuron receiving an input vector $\mathbf{x}$, its output can be expressed as:

$$
a=\phi(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{x}+b)
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{x}$ is the input feature vector.
- $\mathbf{w}$ is the vector of learnable weights.
- $b$ is the bias term.
- $\phi$ is the activation function.
- $a$ is the neuron's output.

For an entire layer, the transformation can be written as:

$$
\mathbf{h}^{(l)}=\phi\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right)
$$

Where $\mathbf{h}^{(l)}$ represents the output of layer $l$, and $\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$ represents the output of the preceding layer.

### 3.1.1. Learning Through Successive Nonlinear Transformations

An MLP can be understood as an assembly line in which each processing station corresponds to a neural network layer.

Each layer receives information from the previous layer, transforms it, and passes the resulting representation to the next layer.

Through this sequence of transformations, the network progressively learns more complex patterns and **hierarchical representations** of the input data.

The use of nonlinear activation functions is particularly important. Without nonlinear transformations, a sequence of linear layers would be equivalent to a single linear transformation, regardless of the number of layers.

By introducing nonlinearity, MLPs can model relationships that cannot be represented by a simple linear function.

### 3.1.2. The Universal Approximation Theorem

The expressive power of MLPs is supported by the **Universal Approximation Theorem**.

Under appropriate conditions, a feedforward neural network with a sufficiently large hidden layer and a suitable nonlinear activation function can approximate any continuous function on a compact domain to arbitrary accuracy.

This theoretical result demonstrates that neural networks can represent a broad class of complex relationships between inputs and outputs.

However, the theorem does not guarantee that a particular network can be trained efficiently, that a small network will be sufficient, or that the learned function will generalize well to unseen data.

In recommendation problems, this expressive capacity is useful because user preferences often depend on complicated interactions among multiple features.

For example, an MLP may learn subtle nonlinear patterns from a user's movie-watching history that simpler models cannot easily capture.

## 3.2. Why Use MLPs in Recommender Systems?

Many traditional recommendation models, including standard **Matrix Factorization (MF)**, use relatively simple interaction functions.

In matrix factorization, users and items are represented by latent feature vectors, and their predicted interaction is commonly calculated using a dot product.

Although effective, this approach restricts the interaction function to a particular mathematical form.

MLPs can introduce nonlinear transformations into recommendation models, enabling them to learn more complex relationships between user and item representations.

One of the most established approaches based on this idea is **Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF)**.

## 3.3. Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF)

**Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF)** is a deep learning framework that extends traditional collaborative filtering by replacing or generalizing the interaction function used to model user–item preferences.

The central motivation is to overcome the limited expressiveness of the conventional dot-product interaction function.

### 3.3.1. Traditional Matrix Factorization

In Matrix Factorization, the user–item interaction matrix is approximated using two lower-dimensional matrices containing user and item latent factors.

For a user $u$ and an item $i$, the predicted interaction score is:

$$
\hat{r}_{ui}=\mathbf{p}_{u}^{T}\mathbf{q}_{i}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{p}_{u}$ is the latent representation of user $u$.
- $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ is the latent representation of item $i$.
- $\hat{r}_{ui}$ is the predicted interaction score.

The dot product calculates the sum of the elementwise products of the two vectors.

Although this operation captures relationships between corresponding latent factors, it imposes a fixed interaction structure.

As a result, it may not fully represent complex nonlinear dependencies between users and items.

### 3.3.2. Replacing the Dot Product with a Neural Network

NCF addresses this limitation by using a neural architecture to learn the user–item interaction function.

Instead of relying exclusively on a fixed dot product, the model can learn a more flexible mapping from user and item representations to a predicted interaction score.

In a simple MLP-based NCF architecture, the process consists of three stages:

1. **User Embedding:** A user identifier is mapped to a dense latent vector.
2. **Item Embedding:** An item identifier is mapped to another dense latent vector.
3. **Neural Interaction Modeling:** The user and item embeddings are combined and passed through an MLP to predict the interaction.

The general prediction function can be expressed as:

$$
\hat{y}_{ui}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{q}_{i})
$$

Where:

- $\hat{y}_{ui}$ is the predicted interaction score.
- $\mathbf{p}_{u}$ and $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ are the learned user and item embeddings.
- $f_{\theta}$ is a neural network parameterized by $\theta$.

In an MLP-based implementation, the embeddings are commonly concatenated before being passed through the hidden layers.

This enables the model to learn nonlinear combinations of the latent user and item features.

### 3.3.3. Example: Personalized Movie Recommendation

Consider a movie recommendation platform such as Netflix.

In traditional matrix factorization, a user's preferences and a movie's characteristics may be represented by two simple latent vectors.

For illustration, suppose a user's preferences are:

`[Action: 0.8, Comedy: 0.2]`

And an action movie is represented as:

`[Action: 0.9, Comedy: 0.1]`

The predicted interaction score is calculated using the dot product:

$$
\hat{r}=0.8(0.9)+0.2(0.1)=0.74
$$

This produces a score of $0.74$.

NCF replaces this fixed interaction calculation with a learned neural function.

For example, the network may discover that a user who enjoys action and thriller movies is also likely to enjoy science-fiction movies featuring actors who appeared in the user's favorite action films.

Such relationships may be difficult to represent through a simple linear interaction between latent vectors.

An MLP-based model can potentially learn these more complex patterns when the relevant information is represented in its input features or training interactions.

## 3.4. Implementing MF and NCF with PyTorch

The following example uses **PyTorch**, one of the most widely used deep learning libraries, to illustrate the difference between conventional Matrix Factorization and an MLP-based Neural Collaborative Filtering model.

The example demonstrates how the fixed interaction function used in MF can be replaced with a more expressive nonlinear neural network.

### 3.4.1. Creating User and Item Embeddings

We begin by defining a small hypothetical recommendation environment containing 100 users and 200 items.

Each user and item is represented by a 16-dimensional embedding vector.

These embeddings are initialized randomly and can subsequently be learned through model training.

In other applications, vector representations may also be obtained using different feature extraction methods.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

# تعداد کاربران، آیتم‌ها و ابعاد تعبیه هر کدام

num_users = 100
num_items = 200
embedding_dim = 16

# تعریف تعبیه نشان دهنده کاربر و آیتم (انواع ویژگی‌های استخراج شده و به بردار تبدیل شده)
user_embedding = nn.Embedding(num_users, embedding_dim)
item_embedding = nn.Embedding(num_items, embedding_dim)

# یک مثال از کاربر و آیتم
user_id = torch.tensor([10])
item_id = torch.tensor([50])

# استخراج بردارهای نهفته
user_vector = user_embedding(user_id)  # shape: [1, 16]
item_vector = item_embedding(item_id)  # shape: [1, 16]

```

The `nn.Embedding` layer maintains a learnable lookup table that maps integer identifiers to dense vectors.

In this example:

- `num_users = 100` specifies the number of users.
- `num_items = 200` specifies the number of items.
- `embedding_dim = 16` determines the dimensionality of each embedding.
- `user_id = 10` identifies the example user.
- `item_id = 50` identifies the example item.

Both extracted vectors have shape `[1, 16]`, where the first dimension represents the batch size.

### 3.4.2. Implementing Matrix Factorization

We first implement a conventional Matrix Factorization interaction function.

The predicted score is calculated using the dot product of the user and item embeddings.

```python
# یک پیاده‌سازی از مدل MF
class MatrixFactorization(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super(MatrixFactorization, self).__init__()

    def forward(self, user_vec, item_vec):
        # ضرب داخلی دو بردار
        prediction = torch.sum(user_vec * item_vec, dim=1)
        return prediction

# استفاده از مدل MF
mf_model = MatrixFactorization()
mf_prediction = mf_model(user_vector, item_vector)
print(f"MF Prediction: {mf_prediction.item()}")

```

The model performs elementwise multiplication between the user and item vectors and sums the resulting values along the embedding dimension.

Mathematically:

$$
\hat{r}_{ui}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{uk}q_{ik}
$$

Where $d$ is the embedding dimension.

The output is a scalar representing the predicted interaction score.

This model uses a fixed dot-product interaction function rather than learning a nonlinear transformation between the embeddings.

The numerical output depends on the embedding values. Because the embeddings are randomly initialized in this example, the prediction does not yet represent a meaningful learned preference.

### 3.4.3. Implementing MLP-Based Neural Collaborative Filtering

We now replace the dot-product interaction function with an MLP.

Instead of directly multiplying the embeddings, the model concatenates them into a single feature vector.

The combined vector is then passed through multiple fully connected layers with nonlinear activation functions.

```python
class NeuralCollaborativeFiltering(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, embedding_dim):
        super(NeuralCollaborativeFiltering, self).__init__()
        # ورودی MLP از الحاق بردارهای کاربر و آیتم تشکیل می‌شود
        self.mlp_layers = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(embedding_dim * 2, 64),  # لایه اول با تابع فعال‌سازی غیرخطی
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(64, 32),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(32, 1)  # لایه خروجی برای پیش‌بینی امتیاز
        )

    def forward(self, user_vec, item_vec):
        # الحاق بردارهای کاربر و آیتم
        combined_vector = torch.cat([user_vec, item_vec], dim=1)
        # عبور از MLP برای یادگیری روابط غیرخطی
        prediction = self.mlp_layers(combined_vector)
        return prediction.squeeze()

# استفاده از مدل NCF
ncf_model = NeuralCollaborativeFiltering(embedding_dim)
ncf_prediction = ncf_model(user_vector, item_vector)
print(f"NCF Prediction: {ncf_prediction.item()}")


```

The architecture contains three fully connected layers:

| Layer | Input Dimensions | Output Dimensions | Activation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| First hidden layer | 32 | 64 | ReLU |
| Second hidden layer | 64 | 32 | ReLU |
| Output layer | 32 | 1 | Linear |

Because each embedding contains 16 values, concatenating the user and item embeddings produces a 32-dimensional input vector.

The first hidden layer transforms this vector into a 64-dimensional representation.

The second hidden layer reduces the representation to 32 dimensions.

Finally, the output layer produces a single predicted interaction score.

The ReLU activation functions introduce nonlinearity between successive transformations.

As a result, the model can represent more complex user–item interaction functions than a conventional dot product.

The mathematical structure can be summarized as:

$$
\mathbf{z}_{0}=[\mathbf{p}_{u};\mathbf{q}_{i}]
$$

$$
\mathbf{z}_{1}=ReLU(\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{z}_{0}+\mathbf{b}_{1})
$$

$$
\mathbf{z}_{2}=ReLU(\mathbf{W}_{2}\mathbf{z}_{1}+\mathbf{b}_{2})
$$

$$
\hat{y}_{ui}=\mathbf{W}_{3}\mathbf{z}_{2}+\mathbf{b}_{3}
$$

Here, the notation $[\mathbf{p}_{u};\mathbf{q}_{i}]$ represents vector concatenation.

The resulting prediction is generated by a learned nonlinear function rather than a fixed dot product.

**Important:** The code demonstrates the forward computations of MF and NCF, but it does not include a training loop, loss function, or optimizer. Consequently, the example models have not yet learned meaningful recommendation patterns. Their purpose is to illustrate the architectural difference between linear interaction modeling and nonlinear neural interaction modeling.

## 3.5. Neural Matrix Factorization (NeuMF)

A further extension of Neural Collaborative Filtering is **Neural Matrix Factorization (NeuMF)**.

NeuMF combines two complementary architectures:

1. **Generalized Matrix Factorization (GMF)**
2. **Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)**

The objective is to combine the strengths of traditional latent-factor interaction modeling with the expressive power of nonlinear neural networks.

### 3.5.1. Generalized Matrix Factorization (GMF)

GMF extends conventional Matrix Factorization within the NCF framework.

Instead of directly calculating a dot product, GMF first performs elementwise multiplication between the user and item embeddings.

The resulting interaction vector is:

$$
\mathbf{z}_{GMF}=\mathbf{p}_{u}\odot\mathbf{q}_{i}
$$

Where $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication.

A learnable output layer can then transform this vector into a prediction.

This preserves the latent-factor interaction structure of conventional Matrix Factorization while allowing additional flexibility through learned output weights.

### 3.5.2. The MLP Branch

The MLP branch learns nonlinear relationships between user and item representations.

The embeddings are concatenated and passed through multiple fully connected layers.

Unlike GMF, which relies on elementwise interactions, the MLP can learn more complex transformations involving combinations of multiple latent features.

### 3.5.3. Combining GMF and MLP

NeuMF combines the representations produced by the GMF and MLP branches.

The resulting representation is used to generate the final prediction.

Conceptually:

$$
\mathbf{z}_{NeuMF}=[\mathbf{z}_{GMF};\mathbf{z}_{MLP}]
$$

The combined representation is then passed to an output layer.

This architecture allows the model to exploit two different types of interaction patterns:

- **GMF:** Captures generalized latent-factor interactions.
- **MLP:** Captures nonlinear relationships between user and item representations.

By combining both branches, NeuMF aims to achieve stronger recommendation performance than either component alone.

## 3.6. Beyond NCF: MLPs in Sequential Recommendation

The applications of MLPs in recommender systems extend beyond Neural Collaborative Filtering.

Despite their relatively simple structure, MLPs can also serve as effective components in more advanced recommendation architectures.

An important example is **FMLP-Rec**, introduced in research published in 2022.

FMLP-Rec demonstrates that techniques inspired by signal processing can be combined with MLP-based architectures to achieve competitive performance in sequential recommendation tasks.

Sequential recommendation involves predicting future user interactions based on the order of previous interactions.

Many advanced approaches use architectures designed specifically for sequence modeling.

FMLP-Rec explores an alternative approach by combining MLP-based modeling with frequency-domain filtering techniques.

This design can achieve performance comparable to more complex sequential recommendation architectures while reducing certain computational and architectural complexities.

For additional details, see the original paper:

[*FMLP-Rec: Filter-enhanced MLP is All You Need for Sequential Recommendation*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13556)

## 3.7. The Role of MLPs in Modern Recommender Systems

Multilayer Perceptrons are among the most fundamental architectures in deep learning.

Their ability to perform successive nonlinear transformations makes them useful for modeling complex relationships between users and items.

In recommendation systems, MLPs can extend traditional collaborative filtering by replacing fixed interaction functions with learned nonlinear mappings.

Architectures such as **NCF** and **NeuMF** demonstrate how neural networks can enhance latent-factor models and capture interaction patterns that may be difficult to represent using a dot product alone.

Although the benefits of these architectures depend on the dataset, training procedure, and evaluation setting, they provide an important foundation for understanding more advanced deep learning-based recommendation methods.
