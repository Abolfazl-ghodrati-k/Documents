**Neural Autoregressive Models** are a class of neural architectures that use the probability chain rule to factorize the joint distribution of data into a sequence of conditional distributions. Each conditional distribution is parameterized by a neural network, allowing the model to capture complex dependencies among variables.

These architectures originated from traditional statistical autoregressive models. They gained particular prominence in machine learning with the introduction of the **Neural Autoregressive Distribution Estimator (NADE)**, which made it possible to calculate exact probabilities for high-dimensional data through a tractable factorization.

Subsequent developments included Deep NADE and Convolutional NADE. Autoregressive modeling also became a fundamental component of modern sequence-generation architectures, including Transformer-based models such as GPT.

In recommender systems, neural autoregressive modeling has been incorporated into collaborative filtering through approaches such as **CF-NADE** and **Implicit CF-NADE**. These methods use conditional probability distributions to model user preferences and generate personalized recommendations.

## 7.1. Review of Autoregressive Models

An **Autoregressive Model** predicts a variable using previously observed variables in a sequence.

In statistics and machine learning, autoregressive methods are particularly useful when the order of observations matters.

Examples include:

- Predicting the next value in a time series.
- Predicting the next word in a sentence.
- Estimating future user interactions from previous behavior.
- Modeling dependencies among item ratings.

The underlying principle is that previous observations provide information about subsequent outcomes.

For example, a language model may predict the next word based on the words already appearing in a sentence.

Similarly, a recommendation model may estimate a user's preference for an item using information from other items the user has rated.

Traditional autoregressive models often represent these dependencies through linear relationships. Neural autoregressive models extend this framework by using nonlinear neural networks.

## 7.2. Overview of Neural Autoregressive Models

Consider generating a story one word at a time.

Each new word should be consistent with the words already generated so that the resulting sequence remains meaningful.

Neural autoregressive models follow a similar principle when modeling probability distributions.

Instead of representing the joint probability of all variables directly, they decompose it into a product of conditional probabilities.

This decomposition is based on the **Chain Rule of Probability**.

### 7.2.1. Factorizing the Joint Probability Distribution

Let the input vector be:

$$
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})
$$

The joint probability distribution can be factorized as:

$$
p(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{d=1}^{N}p(x_{d}\mid\mathbf{x}_{<d})
$$

Where:

- $p(\mathbf{x})$ is the joint probability of the complete data vector.
- $N$ is the number of variables or dimensions.
- $\prod_{d=1}^{N}$ denotes multiplication over all conditional probabilities.
- $\mathbf{x}_{<d}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})$ represents the variables preceding position $d$.
- $p(x_{d}\mid\mathbf{x}_{<d})$ is the conditional probability of variable $x_{d}$ given the preceding variables.

For the first variable, there are no preceding observations, so:

$$
p(x_{1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{<1})=p(x_{1})
$$

For example, consider a vector containing three variables:

$$
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
$$

Its joint probability can be expressed as:

$$
p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=p(x_{1})p(x_{2}\mid x_{1})p(x_{3}\mid x_{1},x_{2})
$$

This decomposition is mathematically exact and does not require the variables to be independent.

The primary modeling challenge is estimating the individual conditional distributions.

### 7.2.2. Neural Parameterization of Conditional Probabilities

In neural autoregressive models, each conditional probability is estimated using a neural network.

The network receives information about previously observed variables and produces the parameters of the conditional distribution for the next variable.

For example, the model may estimate:

$$
p(x_{d}\mid x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d-1})
$$

Using neural networks allows these conditional probabilities to depend on complex, nonlinear relationships among preceding observations.

This distinguishes neural autoregressive models from traditional linear autoregressive approaches.

For instance, the meaning of a sentence can change substantially when a negation such as *not* appears.

A neural autoregressive language model can learn how such contextual information influences subsequent word probabilities.

Similarly, a neural recommendation model can learn that combinations of ratings provide more information about user preferences than individual ratings considered independently.

### 7.2.3. Neural Autoregressive Distribution Estimation

The **Neural Autoregressive Distribution Estimator (NADE)** is an influential architecture for modeling high-dimensional probability distributions.

NADE combines neural network representations with autoregressive probability factorization.

Its principal advantage is that it supports tractable evaluation of the probability assigned to an observed data vector.

This property distinguishes it from probabilistic models whose likelihoods are difficult to calculate exactly.

Later extensions, including Deep NADE and Convolutional NADE, introduced more expressive neural architectures for modeling conditional dependencies.

The autoregressive principle has also become central to Transformer-based language models, including GPT, where each token is predicted conditionally on preceding tokens.

The same general framework can be adapted to collaborative filtering.

## 7.3. Collaborative Filtering Neural Autoregressive Distribution Estimator (CF-NADE)

**Collaborative Filtering Neural Autoregressive Distribution Estimator (CF-NADE)** applies neural autoregressive modeling to collaborative filtering.

Instead of estimating a user's complete rating vector through a single direct prediction, CF-NADE factorizes its probability distribution into a sequence of conditional rating probabilities.

Each rating is modeled using information from other ratings that precede it in the selected ordering.

This allows the model to learn dependencies among a user's preferences for different items.

### 7.3.1. Intuitive Example: Movie Recommendation

Consider a movie recommendation system attempting to understand a user's preferences.

Suppose the user has rated several movies.

**First Movie: Forrest Gump**

The user watches *Forrest Gump* and gives it a favorable rating.

This interaction may provide evidence that the user enjoys emotionally engaging stories, drama, and character-driven narratives.

**Second Movie: Inception**

The user subsequently watches *Inception* and also rates it positively.

This provides additional information.

The user may appreciate complex narratives not only in conventional drama but also in science-fiction settings.

**Third Movie: Interstellar**

Based on the preceding ratings, the recommendation system may assign a high predicted preference to *Interstellar*.

This movie combines science-fiction themes with emotionally engaging storytelling.

The recommendation is therefore based on the relationship among the user's previously expressed preferences rather than on a single isolated characteristic.

CF-NADE provides a probabilistic framework for learning such relationships.

In this example, the movie ordering illustrates conditional prediction. In CF-NADE, the ordering of rated items need not correspond to the chronological order in which the user watched them.

### 7.3.2. Autoregressive Factorization of Rating Vectors

Let a user's rating vector be:

$$
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{M})
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{r}$ is the user's rating vector.
- $r_{i}$ is the rating assigned to item $i$.
- $M$ is the number of ratings included in the modeled vector.

CF-NADE represents the joint probability of the rating vector as:

$$
p(\mathbf{r})=\prod_{i=1}^{M}p(r_{i}\mid\mathbf{r}_{<i})
$$

Where:

- $p(\mathbf{r})$ is the joint probability of the complete rating vector.
- $\prod_{i=1}^{M}$ denotes the product of conditional probabilities.
- $\mathbf{r}_{<i}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{i-1})$ contains the preceding ratings.
- $p(r_{i}\mid\mathbf{r}_{<i})$ is the probability of rating $r_{i}$ given the preceding ratings.

The probability of each rating therefore depends on the ratings already included in the conditioning context.

For example, the probability that a user assigns five stars to a particular movie may depend on how the user rated other movies.

The model uses neural networks to learn these conditional relationships.

Unlike a conventional matrix factorization model that represents preferences primarily through interactions between user and item latent vectors, CF-NADE directly models dependencies among the ratings in a user's interaction history.

### 7.3.3. Modeling Rating Dependencies

The conditional distributions in CF-NADE allow the model to capture complex patterns in rating behavior.

For example, a user may consistently give high ratings to science-fiction movies with philosophical themes but lower ratings to science-fiction movies emphasizing action.

A neural autoregressive model can learn that combinations of previously observed ratings influence the probability distribution over ratings for another item.

This provides a flexible alternative to approaches that rely exclusively on linear interactions between latent factors.

Because the model estimates a probability distribution over possible ratings, it can represent uncertainty rather than producing only a single deterministic prediction.

### 7.3.4. Ordinal Properties of Ratings

One important characteristic of explicit ratings is that they have an **Ordinal Scale**.

For example, ratings may range from one to five stars:

$$
r_{i}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
$$

These values are ordered.

A prediction of three stars is closer to an actual rating of four stars than a prediction of one star.

A conventional categorical classification objective may treat all incorrect categories without explicitly accounting for their ordinal distance.

CF-NADE addresses this limitation through an additional **Ordinal Cost**.

The ordinal cost encourages predictions that respect the ordering of rating values.

Consequently, the model is trained not only to assign high probability to the correct rating but also to distinguish between errors of different magnitudes.

For example, if the actual rating is four stars, assigning probability to three stars is generally preferable to assigning the same probability to one star.

Incorporating ordinal information can improve rating prediction by reflecting the structure of the rating scale.

### 7.3.5. Factored CF-NADE

A limitation of neural autoregressive models is that their parameter requirements can become substantial when the number of items is large.

Large weight matrices may increase:

- Memory consumption.
- Computational requirements.
- Training time.
- The risk of overfitting.

To improve scalability, a **Factored Version of CF-NADE** was proposed.

In this approach, large weight matrices are represented through products of smaller, low-rank matrices.

For example, a matrix $\mathbf{W}$ may be factorized as:

$$
\mathbf{W}\approx\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{W}$ is the original weight matrix.
- $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are smaller matrices.
- Their intermediate dimension is substantially smaller than the original matrix dimensions.

This factorization reduces the number of trainable parameters when the chosen rank is sufficiently small.

As a result, the model can be trained more efficiently on larger datasets and with more limited computational resources.

## 7.4. Implicit CF-NADE

The original CF-NADE approach focuses on explicit feedback, such as numerical ratings.

However, many real-world recommendation systems rely primarily on **Implicit Feedback**.

Implicit feedback is derived from user behavior rather than directly expressed preferences.

Examples include:

- The number of times a user watches a movie.
- The number of clicks on a product.
- Repeated visits to an item page.
- Listening frequency for a song.
- Purchase or consumption history.

Such observations provide indirect evidence about user preferences.

**Implicit CF-NADE** extends the neural autoregressive collaborative filtering framework to incorporate this type of information.

### 7.4.1. Representing Implicit Feedback

Instead of using explicit ratings, Implicit CF-NADE transforms interaction counts into two separate vectors:

1. A **Binary Preference Vector**.
2. A **Confidence Vector**.

These vectors represent different aspects of the observed behavior.

### 7.4.2. Binary Preference Vector

The binary preference vector indicates whether a user has interacted with an item.

Let:

$$
\mathbf{l}=(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{M})
$$

Where:

$$
l_{i}\in\{0,1\}
$$

A typical definition is:

$$
l_{i}=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if the user has interacted with item }i\\0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

For example:

- If a user has watched a movie at least once, the corresponding entry may be 1.
- If no interaction has been recorded, the corresponding entry may be 0.

This representation indicates whether an interaction has been observed.

However, a zero value does not necessarily mean that the user dislikes the item.

It may simply indicate that the item has not been encountered.

### 7.4.3. Confidence Vector

The confidence vector represents the strength or reliability of the interaction signal.

Let:

$$
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{M})
$$

Where $c_{i}$ represents the confidence associated with item $i$.

For example, repeatedly watching a movie may provide stronger evidence of interest than watching it only once.

Similarly, repeated clicks on a product may indicate stronger engagement than a single accidental click.

The confidence value allows the model to assign greater importance to interactions considered more informative.

Thus, the binary preference vector and confidence vector serve different purposes:

| Vector | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbf{l}$ | Whether an interaction has been observed | Watched = 1, not observed = 0 |
| $\mathbf{c}$ | Strength or confidence of the interaction signal | Higher confidence for repeated viewing |

The confidence values are used to weight the learning objective.

### 7.4.4. Confidence-Weighted Conditional Probability

Implicit CF-NADE models the binary preference vector conditionally on the confidence information.

The confidence-weighted autoregressive formulation can be written as:

$$
p(\mathbf{l}\mid\mathbf{c})=\prod_{i=1}^{M}p(l_{i}\mid\mathbf{l}_{<i},\mathbf{c})^{c_{i}}
$$

Where:

- $p(\mathbf{l}\mid\mathbf{c})$ is the confidence-weighted conditional likelihood expression for the preference vector.
- $M$ is the number of items.
- $l_{i}$ is the binary preference indicator for item $i$.
- $\mathbf{l}_{<i}$ contains preceding preference indicators.
- $\mathbf{c}$ contains the confidence information.
- $c_{i}$ is the confidence weight associated with item $i$.

Raising each conditional probability to the power $c_{i}$ increases the contribution of higher-confidence observations to the training objective.

More precisely, this expression is a confidence-weighted likelihood criterion; without an additional normalization factor, it should not generally be interpreted as a normalized probability distribution over all possible binary preference vectors.

The key idea is that interactions supported by stronger behavioral evidence should have a greater influence during learning.

### 7.4.5. Confidence-Weighted Loss Function

Taking the negative logarithm of the confidence-weighted likelihood produces the training objective:

$$
\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}\log p(l_{i}\mid\mathbf{l}_{<i},\mathbf{c})
$$

Where:

- $\mathcal{L}$ is the confidence-weighted negative log-likelihood loss.
- $c_{i}$ is the confidence weight for item $i$.
- $p(l_{i}\mid\mathbf{l}_{<i},\mathbf{c})$ is the conditional probability assigned to the observed preference indicator.
- The summation covers the modeled items.

The confidence weights determine how strongly each observation contributes to the loss.

For example, suppose a user has watched one movie 100 times and another movie only once.

If repeated viewing is interpreted as stronger evidence of preference, the first movie receives a larger confidence weight.

An incorrect prediction for that movie therefore contributes more heavily to the loss.

By contrast, an incorrect prediction for an item with a smaller confidence weight produces a smaller penalty.

This mechanism encourages the model to focus more strongly on reliable behavioral signals while reducing the relative influence of weaker or potentially noisy observations.

However, interaction frequency is not always a perfect indicator of preference. Repeated interactions may also reflect availability, habit, or other contextual factors. Confidence weighting therefore depends on how behavioral signals are interpreted and transformed.

### 7.4.6. Advantages of Implicit CF-NADE

Implicit CF-NADE combines neural autoregressive modeling with confidence-weighted implicit feedback.

This provides several potential advantages.

**Learning from Behavioral Data**

The model does not require users to provide explicit numerical ratings.

Instead, it can learn from naturally occurring interactions such as views, clicks, and repeated consumption.

**Modeling Complex Preference Dependencies**

The autoregressive architecture learns conditional relationships among item preferences.

This allows the model to capture nonlinear dependencies that may be difficult to represent using simpler collaborative filtering approaches.

**Accounting for Interaction Confidence**

The confidence-weighted objective distinguishes between stronger and weaker behavioral signals.

This can improve the model's ability to learn from noisy implicit feedback.

**Handling Sparse Interaction Data**

By learning dependencies across observed interactions, the model can estimate preferences even when users have interacted with only a subset of available items.

**Improving Personalized Recommendation**

The resulting conditional predictions can be used to rank candidate items and generate personalized recommendations.

Experimental evaluations on real-world datasets, including television streaming and viewing histories, have reported substantial improvements over standard implicit matrix factorization methods.

These results suggest that confidence-aware neural autoregressive models can effectively exploit repeated behavioral signals while reducing the influence of weaker observations.

Their effectiveness nevertheless depends on the quality of the interaction data, confidence-weighting strategy, and experimental setting.
