
This section examines another important deep learning architecture: **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)**. We first review their structure, fundamental operations, and distinguishing characteristics. We then explore how convolutional networks can be applied to recommender systems, focusing on representation learning, Visual Bayesian Personalized Ranking (VBPR), and Convolutional Neural Collaborative Filtering (ConvNCF).

## 5.1. Convolutional Neural Networks

Imagine examining an image through several different lenses, each designed to detect a particular type of visual pattern. One lens might identify edges, another might detect textures, and a third might recognize particular shapes. By combining the information extracted through these lenses, increasingly complex visual structures can be identified.

This is the fundamental principle underlying **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)**.

CNNs are a class of artificial neural networks designed to learn meaningful patterns from data with a regular spatial or sequential structure.

Consider the problem of teaching a computer to recognize cats in photographs.

A traditional approach might involve manually designing features corresponding to characteristics such as cat ears, eyes, or whiskers.

A CNN, however, learns relevant features automatically from training data. Instead of requiring developers to explicitly specify which visual characteristics are important, the network learns filters that respond to useful patterns.

CNNs are particularly effective for data organized into regular structures, including:

- **Images:** Two-dimensional grids of pixels arranged into rows and columns.
- **Audio:** One-dimensional sequences of samples over time, or two-dimensional time-frequency representations such as spectrograms.
- **Text:** Sequences of words, tokens, or characters represented numerically through embeddings.

### 5.1.1. Convolutional Filters

The central operation in a CNN is **convolution**.

A convolutional filter, also called a *kernel*, is a small collection of learnable weights applied repeatedly across different regions of the input.

For an image, a filter may move across the spatial dimensions and respond to particular local patterns.

Different filters may learn to detect:

- Horizontal or vertical edges.
- Corners and curves.
- Textures.
- Repeated visual structures.
- More complex combinations of lower-level features.

The result of applying a filter is called a **Feature Map**.

For a two-dimensional input $\mathbf{X}$ and a kernel $\mathbf{K}$, the operation commonly implemented by CNN libraries can be written as:

$$
Y_{i,j}=\sum_{a=0}^{k_{h}-1}\sum_{b=0}^{k_{w}-1}K_{a,b}X_{i+a,j+b}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{X}$ is the input feature map.
- $\mathbf{K}$ is the convolutional kernel.
- $k_{h}$ and $k_{w}$ are the kernel's height and width.
- $Y_{i,j}$ is the output value at spatial position $(i,j)$.

This expression describes the cross-correlation operation commonly referred to as convolution in deep learning frameworks.

As the filter moves across the input, the same set of weights is applied to different regions, enabling the network to recognize similar patterns at different positions.

### 5.1.2. Pooling Operations

After convolutional filters identify important patterns, **Pooling** operations may be used to summarize the resulting feature maps.

Pooling reduces the spatial dimensions of the representation while preserving selected information.

One common operation is **Max Pooling**, which retains the maximum value within each local region.

For a pooling region $\mathcal{N}_{i,j}$:

$$
Y_{i,j}=\max_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{N}_{i,j}}X_{a,b}
$$

For example, a $2\times2$ max-pooling operation with a stride of two typically reduces the height and width of a feature map by approximately half.

Pooling can provide several benefits:

- Reducing the size of intermediate representations.
- Decreasing computational requirements in subsequent layers.
- Preserving prominent feature responses.
- Introducing some tolerance to small shifts in feature positions.

Not every modern CNN architecture requires explicit pooling layers. Some networks achieve dimensionality reduction through strided convolutions.

### 5.1.3. Nonlinear Activation Functions

**Nonlinear Activation Functions** allow CNNs to learn relationships that cannot be represented through linear transformations alone.

Without nonlinear activations, a sequence of linear convolutional layers would remain equivalent to a single linear transformation.

A commonly used activation function is the **Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)**:

$$
ReLU(x)=\max(0,x)
$$

ReLU preserves positive values and replaces negative values with zero.

By introducing nonlinear transformations between convolutional layers, CNNs can learn increasingly complex patterns.

For example, early layers may detect simple edges, intermediate layers may identify textures or object parts, and deeper layers may recognize more abstract visual structures.

### 5.1.4. Weight Sharing and Parameter Efficiency

One of the principal advantages of CNNs is **Weight Sharing**.

In a fully connected neural network, different input positions generally have separate connection weights.

In a convolutional layer, the same kernel is applied repeatedly across different regions of the input.

This substantially reduces the number of learnable parameters compared with a fully connected layer operating on the same input.

For example, consider a convolutional layer with:

- A kernel size of $3\times3$.
- Three input channels.
- Sixteen output channels.

Ignoring biases, the number of learnable parameters is:

$$
P=3\times3\times3\times16=432
$$

The parameter count does not depend directly on the spatial height and width of the input image.

Weight sharing also allows a filter to detect a similar pattern at different locations.

For instance, a filter trained to detect a particular edge pattern can respond to that pattern whether it appears near the top, center, or bottom of an image.

This property contributes to the translation equivariance of convolutional layers.

Through weight sharing and local connectivity, CNNs can achieve relatively efficient representation learning while retaining the ability to model complex patterns.

A typical CNN used for handwritten digit recognition may contain several convolutional layers, nonlinear activation functions, pooling operations, and a final classification layer.

The network receives an image of a handwritten digit and predicts one of the ten classes, from 0 through 9.

Readers unfamiliar with CNN architectures may consult introductory deep learning resources. For a detailed explanation of convolution dimensions, padding, stride, and related calculations, the tutorial *A Guide to Convolution Arithmetic for Deep Learning* is particularly useful.

## 5.2. Using CNNs for Representation Learning in Recommender Systems

CNNs can extract informative features from structured data such as images. This capability is especially valuable in recommender systems because user preferences are influenced by more than historical interactions.

For example, a user's decision to purchase a product may depend on:

- The product's visual appearance.
- Its textual description.
- Its brand or category.
- The characteristics of associated audio or video content.

Consequently, recommendation models can benefit from incorporating information from multiple modalities.

CNNs can be used to learn feature representations from images, text, audio, video, and other structured inputs.

These representations can then be integrated with collaborative filtering methods to improve preference modeling.

### 5.2.1. Visual Representation Learning

Consider a clothing recommendation system.

Two products may belong to the same category but differ substantially in color, shape, texture, and design.

A traditional collaborative filtering model primarily learns from observed user–item interactions.

However, a CNN can extract visual features directly from product images.

These features provide additional information about the products and may help the recommendation system understand visual preferences.

For example, a user may consistently prefer:

- Brightly colored clothing.
- Minimalist designs.
- Particular patterns or textures.
- Specific silhouettes or shapes.

A visual representation can encode characteristics relevant to these preferences.

One influential model that incorporates this idea is **Visual Bayesian Personalized Ranking (VBPR)**.

## 5.3. Visual Bayesian Personalized Ranking (VBPR)

**Visual Bayesian Personalized Ranking (VBPR)** extends traditional matrix factorization by incorporating visual features extracted from product images.

The model combines collaborative interaction information with visual representations to learn personalized rankings.

Rather than relying exclusively on latent factors learned from user–item interactions, VBPR introduces an additional visual component.

### 5.3.1. Extracting Visual Features

Suppose a CNN extracts a high-dimensional feature vector from an item's image.

For example, the CNN may produce a vector containing 4,096 features.

Let this vector be:

$$
\mathbf{f}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{f}_{i}$ is the visual feature vector for item $i$.
- $D$ is the dimensionality of the CNN representation.

Because the original visual representation may be high-dimensional, VBPR projects it into a lower-dimensional latent space.

This projection is performed using a learnable embedding matrix:

$$
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}
$$

The resulting visual embedding is:

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{f}_{i}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{E}$ is the trainable visual embedding matrix.
- $\mathbf{f}_{i}$ is the CNN-derived visual feature vector.
- $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}$ is the lower-dimensional visual representation of item $i$.
- $K$ is the dimensionality of the visual latent space.

This projection allows the model to learn which visual characteristics are most relevant to user preferences.

For example, the visual embedding may capture patterns associated with a user's preference for bright colors or particular clothing designs.

### 5.3.2. The VBPR Prediction Function

VBPR combines nonvisual latent factors with visual preference factors.

A simplified prediction function can be expressed as:

$$
\hat{x}_{ui}=\alpha+\beta_{u}+\beta_{i}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
$$

Where:

- $\hat{x}_{ui}$ is the predicted preference score for user $u$ and item $i$.
- $\alpha$ is a global bias representing the overall baseline score.
- $\beta_{u}$ is a user-specific bias.
- $\beta_{i}$ is an item-specific bias.
- $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{u}$ is the nonvisual latent-factor vector for user $u$.
- $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}$ is the nonvisual latent-factor vector for item $i$.
- $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}$ is the visual preference vector for user $u$.
- $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}$ is the visual representation of item $i$.

The prediction function contains two important interaction terms.

**Nonvisual Preference Interaction**

$$
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}
$$

This term models compatibility between the user and item in the conventional collaborative latent space.

It may capture preferences associated with factors such as product category, brand, or functionality.

**Visual Preference Interaction**

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
$$

This term models how well the item's visual characteristics match the user's learned visual preferences.

For example, a user who frequently interacts with brightly colored clothing may receive higher scores for visually similar products.

The original VBPR formulation also includes a visual bias term. The equation above presents the simplified formulation used to explain the interaction between collaborative and visual latent factors.

### 5.3.3. Training with Bayesian Personalized Ranking

VBPR is trained using **Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR)**, a pairwise learning-to-rank approach introduced previously in the section on learning approaches for ranking.

BPR is particularly suitable for recommendation datasets containing implicit feedback.

Instead of predicting an exact numerical rating, BPR learns to rank observed interactions above unobserved interactions.

For a user $u$, suppose:

- Item $i$ has been observed as a positive interaction.
- Item $j$ has not been observed as a positive interaction.

The model aims to satisfy:

$$
\hat{x}_{ui}>\hat{x}_{uj}
$$

A common BPR optimization objective is:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{BPR}=-\sum_{(u,i,j)\in\mathcal{D}}\log\sigma(\hat{x}_{ui}-\hat{x}_{uj})+\lambda\lVert\Theta\rVert^{2}
$$

Where:

- $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of training triplets.
- $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function.
- $\Theta$ denotes the model parameters.
- $\lambda$ controls regularization.

The model is encouraged to assign higher preference scores to items with which the user has interacted than to sampled unobserved items.

This directly supports personalized ranking.

An unobserved interaction is not necessarily a negative preference; it is used as a relative comparison during training.

### 5.3.4. Advantages of VBPR

By incorporating visual information, VBPR can improve recommendation accuracy compared with standard matrix factorization in settings where product appearance strongly influences user preferences.

One particularly important application is the **Item Cold-Start Problem**.

Suppose a new clothing product is added to an online store.

Because the product has not yet been purchased or rated, a purely collaborative filtering model has little interaction information from which to learn its preferences.

VBPR can nevertheless extract visual features from the product image.

The model can compare these features with users' learned visual preferences and estimate which users are likely to find the product attractive.

Consequently, visual information can partially compensate for limited interaction history.

VBPR can also provide a degree of interpretability through its learned visual preference factors.

For example, the model may reveal that a particular recommendation is associated with visual characteristics preferred by the user.

However, learned visual factors are not necessarily directly interpretable as specific human-readable attributes without additional analysis.

### 5.3.5. Extensions of VBPR

Since its introduction in 2016, VBPR has inspired several multimodal recommendation approaches.

One example is **Multi-View Bayesian Personalized Ranking (MVBPR)**.

MVBPR extends personalized ranking by combining multiple sources of side information, such as visual and textual features.

By incorporating complementary representations, multimodal recommendation models can capture aspects of items that may not be evident from interaction histories alone.

## 5.4. Convolutional Neural Networks in Collaborative Filtering

Deep learning has also enabled convolutional architectures to be applied directly to user–item interaction modeling.

One such approach is **Convolutional Neural Collaborative Filtering (ConvNCF)**.

ConvNCF extends neural collaborative filtering by introducing a convolutional architecture designed to learn complex interactions between user and item latent factors.

Its central idea is to represent user–item relationships using an interaction matrix rather than reducing them immediately to a single scalar value.

### 5.4.1. From Inner Products to Outer Products

Traditional matrix factorization commonly estimates user–item compatibility through the inner product of two latent vectors.

Let:

$$
\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
$$

$$
\mathbf{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{p}_{u}$ is the embedding of user $u$.
- $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ is the embedding of item $i$.
- $K$ is the embedding dimensionality.

The inner-product prediction is:

$$
\hat{x}_{ui}=\mathbf{p}_{u}^{\top}\mathbf{q}_{i}
$$

Expanding the expression gives:

$$
\hat{x}_{ui}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{u,k}q_{i,k}
$$

The inner product produces a single scalar value.

Although this approach is computationally efficient, it combines corresponding embedding dimensions and does not explicitly represent every possible pairwise interaction between different dimensions.

ConvNCF addresses this limitation by using the **Outer Product**.

### 5.4.2. Constructing the Interaction Map

Instead of computing an inner product, ConvNCF calculates the outer product of the user and item embeddings.

The resulting interaction map is:

$$
\mathbf{M}_{ui}=\mathbf{p}_{u}\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\top}
$$

Because both embeddings contain $K$ dimensions:

$$
\mathbf{M}_{ui}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
$$

Each entry of the interaction map is:

$$
M_{ui}^{a,b}=p_{u,a}q_{i,b}
$$

Where:

- $p_{u,a}$ is dimension $a$ of the user embedding.
- $q_{i,b}$ is dimension $b$ of the item embedding.
- $M_{ui}^{a,b}$ represents the interaction between these two dimensions.

The interaction map explicitly contains all pairwise products between user and item embedding dimensions.

This provides a richer intermediate representation than a single inner product.

For example, consider a user who enjoys metal music.

Their preferences may depend not only on the genre itself but also on combinations of characteristics such as:

- Fast tempo.
- Strong bass guitar.
- Symphonic elements.
- Particular instrumental arrangements.

A simple inner product may not explicitly capture all cross-dimensional relationships associated with such preferences.

An outer-product interaction map, however, exposes pairwise combinations between latent dimensions, allowing subsequent neural layers to learn more complex relationships.

### 5.4.3. Applying CNNs to the Interaction Map

After constructing the two-dimensional interaction map, ConvNCF processes it using a multilayer convolutional neural network.

The interaction map is treated as a structured matrix.

Convolutional filters operate on local regions of this matrix and learn patterns associated with relationships among latent dimensions.

As information passes through successive convolutional layers, the network can combine local patterns into increasingly complex representations.

This process resembles feature extraction from images, although the interaction map represents latent-factor relationships rather than visual pixels.

The CNN learns transformations that can capture higher-order patterns among user and item factors.

The resulting representation is then used to calculate a final user–item preference score.

### 5.4.4. Modeling Higher-Order Correlations

The principal motivation behind ConvNCF is to model interactions that are more complex than those represented by conventional matrix factorization.

An inner product aggregates corresponding latent-factor interactions into one scalar.

An outer product preserves pairwise cross-dimensional interactions in a matrix.

A convolutional network can subsequently learn nonlinear combinations of these interactions.

This enables ConvNCF to model higher-order correlations among user and item latent factors.

Such modeling capacity may be particularly useful when preferences depend on combinations of several characteristics rather than isolated factors.

However, convolutional processing introduces an architectural assumption: neighboring positions in the interaction map should contain useful local patterns.

Unlike pixels in an image, latent embedding dimensions do not necessarily have an inherent spatial ordering. Consequently, the effectiveness of convolutional filters depends on whether the learned embedding arrangement supports useful local structure.

### 5.4.5. Training ConvNCF with BPR

ConvNCF can be trained using the **Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR)** objective.

As discussed previously, BPR optimizes the relative ranking of observed and unobserved interactions.

For each training triplet $(u,i,j)$:

- $u$ represents a user.
- $i$ represents an observed positive item.
- $j$ represents a sampled unobserved item.

The model learns to assign a higher score to item $i$ than to item $j$.

The ranking objective is:

$$
\hat{x}_{ui}>\hat{x}_{uj}
$$

The corresponding BPR loss can be expressed as:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{BPR}=-\sum_{(u,i,j)\in\mathcal{D}}\log\sigma(\hat{x}_{ui}-\hat{x}_{uj})+\lambda\lVert\Theta\rVert^{2}
$$

This training approach is particularly appropriate for personalized recommendation tasks involving implicit feedback.

### 5.4.6. Advantages and Applications of ConvNCF

ConvNCF was evaluated on recommendation datasets such as **Yelp** and **Gowalla**.

In the reported experiments, the architecture demonstrated improvements over several collaborative filtering baselines, including neural collaborative filtering approaches.

Its principal advantages include:

**Modeling Complex Interactions**

The outer-product interaction map preserves cross-dimensional relationships that are not explicitly represented by a conventional inner product.

**Learning Higher-Order Patterns**

Convolutional layers can learn nonlinear combinations of latent-factor interactions.

**Parameter Efficiency**

Through weight sharing, convolutional layers may model complex interaction patterns with fewer parameters than certain deep fully connected architectures.

**Handling Sparse Interaction Data**

Learning more expressive interaction representations may improve recommendation performance when observed interactions are limited.

**Improved Personalized Ranking**

Training with BPR enables the model to optimize the relative ordering of candidate items.

These properties make ConvNCF a useful architecture for studying how convolutional operations can be incorporated into collaborative filtering.

Nevertheless, the ability to model complex interactions does not automatically solve the cold-start problem.

When a completely new user or item has no interaction history and no additional side information, a purely collaborative ConvNCF model still lacks sufficient information to learn a reliable embedding.

Similarly, improvements over conventional matrix factorization or MLP-based approaches depend on the dataset, architecture, training procedure, and evaluation methodology.
