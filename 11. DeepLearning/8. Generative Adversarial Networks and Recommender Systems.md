In previous sections, we examined several important deep learning architectures and explored their applications in recommender systems. This section introduces another influential approach: **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)**.

We first examine the fundamental principles and training mechanisms of GANs, then explore their applications in information retrieval and recommendation through two important approaches: **Information Retrieval Generative Adversarial Networks (IRGAN)** and **Adversarial Personalized Ranking (APR)**.

## 8.1. Overview of Generative Adversarial Networks

Imagine a skilled artist who produces counterfeit paintings and an experienced art expert whose responsibility is to distinguish authentic paintings from forgeries.

The artist continuously improves the quality of the counterfeit paintings to make them increasingly difficult to detect. Meanwhile, the expert improves their ability to identify subtle differences between authentic and counterfeit works.

As both participants improve through competition, the artist becomes more capable of producing realistic paintings, while the expert becomes increasingly effective at identifying counterfeits.

This competitive process illustrates the fundamental idea behind **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)**.

Introduced in 2014, GANs are a class of deep generative models that learn to produce synthetic samples resembling observations from a real data distribution.

Their introduction represented an important development in generative modeling because they provided a framework for learning complex data distributions through adversarial training, without requiring explicit probability density estimation.

A GAN consists of two neural networks:

1. **Generator (G)**
2. **Discriminator (D)**

These networks are trained through a competitive optimization process.

### 8.1.1. Generator

The **Generator**, denoted by $G$, is responsible for producing synthetic data.

It typically receives a random noise vector:

$$
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}(\mathbf{z})
$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{z}$ is a randomly sampled latent vector.
- $p_{z}(\mathbf{z})$ is the prior distribution from which the noise is sampled.

The noise distribution is commonly Gaussian or uniform.

The generator transforms this vector into a synthetic sample:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=G(\mathbf{z})
$$

Where $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ is the generated observation.

For example, depending on the application, the generator may produce:

- Synthetic images.
- Artificial audio signals.
- Generated data records.
- Samples resembling observations from a target distribution.

The generator's objective is to produce samples that resemble real data closely enough to deceive the discriminator.

In other words, it attempts to make the discriminator assign a high probability of authenticity to generated samples.

### 8.1.2. Discriminator

The **Discriminator**, denoted by $D$, is responsible for distinguishing real observations from generated samples.

It acts as a binary classifier.

The discriminator receives two types of input:

- **Real samples**, drawn from the actual data distribution.
- **Generated samples**, produced by the generator.

For an input sample $\mathbf{x}$, the discriminator outputs:

$$
D(\mathbf{x})\in[0,1]
$$

This value represents the discriminator's estimated probability that the sample originates from the real data distribution.

Ideally:

- For a real sample, $D(\mathbf{x})$ should approach 1.
- For a generated sample, $D(G(\mathbf{z}))$ should approach 0.

The discriminator therefore attempts to maximize its ability to distinguish authentic data from generated observations.

The generator, in contrast, attempts to produce samples for which:

$$
D(G(\mathbf{z}))\rightarrow 1
$$

These opposing objectives create the adversarial training process.

### 8.1.3. The Minimax Game

GAN training is formulated as a **Minimax Game** between the generator and discriminator.

The discriminator attempts to maximize its classification performance, while the generator attempts to minimize the discriminator's ability to distinguish generated samples from real ones.

The original GAN objective is:

$$
\min_{G}\max_{D}V(D,G)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{data}(\mathbf{x})}[\log D(\mathbf{x})]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}(\mathbf{z})}[\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))]
$$

Where:

- $V(D,G)$ is the value function of the adversarial game.
- $G$ is the generator, which attempts to minimize the objective.
- $D$ is the discriminator, which attempts to maximize the objective.
- $p_{data}(\mathbf{x})$ is the real data distribution.
- $p_{z}(\mathbf{z})$ is the generator's input noise distribution.
- $D(\mathbf{x})$ is the discriminator's estimated probability that a real sample is authentic.
- $G(\mathbf{z})$ is the synthetic sample produced from random noise.
- $D(G(\mathbf{z}))$ is the discriminator's estimated probability that the generated sample is real.
- $\mathbb{E}$ denotes mathematical expectation.

The objective contains two terms.

**Real-Data Classification Term**

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{data}(\mathbf{x})}[\log D(\mathbf{x})]
$$

This term rewards the discriminator for correctly identifying real observations.

As $D(\mathbf{x})$ approaches 1, the logarithmic term increases toward 0, its maximum possible value.

**Generated-Data Classification Term**

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}(\mathbf{z})}[\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))]
$$

This term rewards the discriminator for correctly identifying generated samples as artificial.

The discriminator attempts to make $D(G(\mathbf{z}))$ approach 0.

The generator has the opposing objective: it attempts to produce samples that the discriminator classifies as real.

### 8.1.4. Adversarial Training and Equilibrium

During training, the generator and discriminator are typically updated alternately.

A simplified training cycle consists of the following steps:

1. Sample real observations from the training dataset.
2. Sample random noise vectors and generate synthetic observations.
3. Train the discriminator to distinguish real observations from generated ones.
4. Update the generator to produce samples that are more likely to deceive the discriminator.
5. Repeat the process.

As training progresses, the generator ideally learns to approximate the real data distribution.

Under the theoretical equilibrium of the original GAN formulation, when the generated distribution matches the real distribution, the optimal discriminator cannot distinguish the two.

At this equilibrium:

$$
D(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}
$$

for samples in the relevant distributional support.

A discriminator output of approximately 0.5 indicates that it cannot reliably distinguish real samples from generated samples.

However, achieving this equilibrium in practice is not guaranteed. GAN training can be unstable, and problems such as mode collapse may prevent the generator from adequately representing the full data distribution.

The adversarial principle can also be applied to problems other than synthetic image generation.

One important application is information retrieval.

## 8.2. Information Retrieval Generative Adversarial Networks (IRGAN)

**Information Retrieval Generative Adversarial Networks (IRGAN)** extend adversarial learning to information retrieval tasks, including:

- Web search.
- Document retrieval.
- Item recommendation.
- Question answering.

IRGAN introduces a competitive framework in which a generative retrieval model and a discriminative retrieval model improve through adversarial training.

Consider a large digital library containing thousands of books.

A user submits a query describing the information they need.

One model attempts to select books that are likely to be relevant to the query.

Another model evaluates whether the selected books are genuinely relevant.

The first model improves its selection strategy by learning from the second model's evaluations, while the second model improves by distinguishing relevant documents from less relevant candidates.

This interaction captures the central idea of IRGAN.

### 8.2.1. Generator in IRGAN

In IRGAN, the generator is a probabilistic retrieval model.

It is represented by:

$$
p_{\theta}(d\mid q)
$$

Where:

- $q$ is a query.
- $d$ is a candidate document or item.
- $\theta$ denotes the generator's parameters.
- $p_{\theta}(d\mid q)$ is the probability of selecting document $d$ for query $q$.

The generator attempts to approximate the true relevance distribution:

$$
p_{true}(d\mid q)
$$

For a search engine, $q$ may represent a textual search query, and $d$ may represent a webpage or document.

For a recommender system, $q$ may represent a user or the user's preference context, while $d$ represents a candidate item.

The generator learns to select candidates that are likely to be relevant.

As training progresses, it should assign higher probabilities to more relevant documents or items.

### 8.2.2. Discriminator in IRGAN

The discriminator evaluates the relevance of a candidate document to a query.

It is represented by:

$$
D(d\mid q)=\sigma(f_{\phi}(q,d))
$$

Where:

- $D(d\mid q)$ is the discriminator's estimated probability that document $d$ is relevant to query $q$.
- $f_{\phi}(q,d)$ is a learnable relevance scoring function.
- $\phi$ denotes the discriminator's parameters.
- $\sigma$ is the sigmoid activation function.

The sigmoid function is defined as:

$$
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}
$$

It maps an arbitrary real-valued score into the interval $(0,1)$.

The discriminator attempts to assign high probabilities to relevant documents drawn from the observed relevance distribution and low probabilities to documents sampled by the generator.

The generator, in contrast, attempts to select candidates that the discriminator considers relevant.

### 8.2.3. Adversarial Training in IRGAN

IRGAN alternates between updating the discriminator and updating the generator.

**Discriminator Update**

The discriminator learns to distinguish:

- Relevant documents sampled from the true relevance distribution.
- Candidate documents sampled from the generator.

Its parameters $\phi$ are updated to improve this distinction.

**Generator Update**

The generator learns to select documents that receive favorable evaluations from the discriminator.

Because document selection is a discrete sampling operation, gradients cannot generally be propagated directly through the sampled document identities.

IRGAN therefore uses a **Policy Gradient** approach to optimize the generator.

The discriminator's feedback provides a reward signal that guides the generator toward selecting more relevant candidates.

### 8.2.4. IRGAN Minimax Objective

The joint optimization objective is:

$$
\min_{\theta}\max_{\phi}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\mathbb{E}_{d\sim p_{true}(\cdot\mid q_{n})}[\log D(d\mid q_{n})]+\mathbb{E}_{d\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q_{n})}[\log(1-D(d\mid q_{n}))]\right]
$$

Where:

- $\theta$ represents the generator parameters.
- $\phi$ represents the discriminator parameters.
- $N$ is the number of queries in the dataset.
- $q_{n}$ is the $n$-th query.
- $p_{true}(d\mid q_{n})$ is the true relevance distribution for query $q_{n}$.
- $p_{\theta}(d\mid q_{n})$ is the generator's candidate-selection distribution.
- $D(d\mid q_{n})$ is the discriminator's relevance estimate.

The generator minimizes the objective, while the discriminator maximizes it.

The first expectation encourages the discriminator to recognize genuinely relevant documents.

The second expectation encourages the discriminator to identify generated candidates as artificial, while the generator attempts to make those candidates increasingly difficult to distinguish from true relevant documents.

Through alternating optimization, the two models can improve their retrieval capabilities.

### 8.2.5. Generator-Based Ranking with Softmax

For ranking tasks, the generator must assign probabilities to candidate documents or items.

A common approach is to apply the **Softmax Function** to relevance scores.

The generator distribution can be expressed as:

$$
p_{\theta}(d_{i}\mid q)=\frac{\exp(g_{\theta}(q,d_{i}))}{\sum_{j}\exp(g_{\theta}(q,d_{j}))}
$$

Where:

- $p_{\theta}(d_{i}\mid q)$ is the probability of selecting candidate $d_{i}$ for query $q$.
- $g_{\theta}(q,d_{i})$ is the generator's relevance score.
- The denominator sums the exponentiated scores across all candidate documents.

Softmax converts relevance scores into a normalized probability distribution.

Consequently:

$$
\sum_{i}p_{\theta}(d_{i}\mid q)=1
$$

Candidates receiving higher relevance scores are assigned higher sampling probabilities.

For recommendation tasks, the generator can use these probabilities to sample candidate items or rank them according to predicted relevance.

### 8.2.6. Scoring Functions and Hard Negative Sampling

The generator and discriminator use scoring functions:

$$
g_{\theta}(q,d)
$$

$$
f_{\phi}(q,d)
$$

These functions may share a similar mathematical structure, although they generally have distinct trainable parameters.

For example, both may be implemented using matrix factorization when the task involves item recommendation.

One important benefit of IRGAN is **Adaptive Hard Negative Sampling**.

In conventional recommendation training, negative examples may be sampled randomly.

However, randomly sampled negatives are often easy to distinguish from positive interactions and may provide limited training information.

IRGAN addresses this limitation by allowing the generator to select increasingly informative candidates.

As the generator improves, it can produce candidates that resemble relevant items more closely.

These candidates become challenging negative examples for the discriminator.

The discriminator must therefore learn more precise relevance boundaries.

This adversarial sampling process can improve learning efficiency and ranking performance.

IRGAN also provides a framework for exploiting information from unlabeled candidate documents.

Its applications include web search, item recommendation, and question answering, where adversarial training can improve retrieval quality under suitable conditions.

## 8.3. Adversarial Personalized Ranking for Recommender Systems

In previous sections, we examined **Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR)**, which learns to rank preferred items above less-preferred or unobserved items.

Although BPR is an effective learning-to-rank framework, its performance may be sensitive to small changes in learned model parameters.

This sensitivity can affect generalization and robustness.

**Adversarial Personalized Ranking (APR)** addresses this limitation by incorporating adversarial training into the conventional BPR framework.

Unlike a standard GAN, which uses a generator to produce synthetic samples, APR introduces an adversarial perturbation mechanism that deliberately challenges the recommendation model.

The objective is to train a recommender that maintains good ranking performance even when its parameters are subjected to small, targeted perturbations.

### 8.3.1. The Standard BPR Objective

Consider a training dataset containing triplets:

$$
(u,i,j)\in\mathcal{D}
$$

Where:

- $u$ is a user.
- $i$ is an item associated with an observed positive interaction.
- $j$ is an unobserved or sampled negative item.
- $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of training triplets.

The model attempts to assign a higher score to item $i$ than to item $j$.

The BPR loss is:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{BPR}=-\sum_{(u,i,j)\in\mathcal{D}}\ln\sigma(\hat{y}_{ui}-\hat{y}_{uj})
$$

Where:

- $\mathcal{L}_{BPR}$ is the BPR loss.
- $\hat{y}_{ui}$ is the predicted preference score for user $u$ and item $i$.
- $\hat{y}_{uj}$ is the predicted preference score for user $u$ and item $j$.
- $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function.
- $\ln$ denotes the natural logarithm.

The term:

$$
\sigma(\hat{y}_{ui}-\hat{y}_{uj})
$$

represents the model's estimated probability that user $u$ prefers item $i$ over item $j$.

The objective encourages this probability to approach 1 for correctly ordered training pairs.

By minimizing the loss, the model learns to rank observed positive interactions above sampled unobserved interactions.

### 8.3.2. Adversarial Perturbations

APR introduces a second participant into the optimization process: an **Adversarial Attacker**.

The attacker attempts to identify weaknesses in the recommendation model by introducing small, targeted perturbations into its parameters.

Let:

$$
\Theta
$$

denote the current model parameters.

The attacker constructs a perturbation:

$$
\Delta
$$

The perturbed parameters become:

$$
\Theta+\Delta
$$

The attacker seeks a perturbation that maximizes the recommendation model's ranking loss.

However, the perturbation magnitude is constrained:

$$
\lVert\Delta\rVert\le\epsilon
$$

Where:

- $\lVert\Delta\rVert$ is the norm of the perturbation.
- $\epsilon$ is a small positive threshold controlling the maximum permitted perturbation magnitude.

The perturbation is designed to be small but strategically selected to increase the model's prediction error.

This process identifies directions in parameter space to which the model is particularly sensitive.

### 8.3.3. The Adversarial Attacker's Objective

The attacker searches for the perturbation that produces the greatest increase in BPR loss.

The optimal adversarial perturbation is defined as:

$$
\Delta_{adv}=\arg\max_{\lVert\Delta\rVert\le\epsilon}\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta+\Delta)
$$

Where:

- $\Delta_{adv}$ is the adversarial perturbation.
- $\arg\max$ identifies the perturbation that maximizes the objective.
- $\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta+\Delta)$ is the BPR loss evaluated using perturbed model parameters.
- $\epsilon$ limits the perturbation magnitude.

The attacker therefore attempts to make the model perform as poorly as possible within the allowed perturbation region.

This is not necessarily an attack on the system by an external malicious actor.

Instead, the adversarial mechanism is deliberately introduced during training to expose weaknesses in the model.

### 8.3.4. The Minimax Optimization Problem

APR trains the recommendation model to minimize both:

1. Its ordinary BPR loss.
2. Its BPR loss under adversarial perturbations.

The combined objective is:

$$
\min_{\Theta}\max_{\lVert\Delta\rVert\le\epsilon}\left[\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta)+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta+\Delta)\right]
$$

Where:

- $\Theta$ represents the recommendation model parameters.
- $\Delta$ represents the adversarial perturbation.
- $\epsilon$ is the perturbation magnitude constraint.
- $\lambda$ controls the relative importance of the adversarial loss.
- $\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta)$ is the ordinary BPR loss.
- $\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta+\Delta)$ is the BPR loss under perturbed parameters.

The optimization involves two opposing objectives.

**Attacker Objective**

The attacker maximizes the loss by selecting a perturbation that exploits the model's weaknesses.

**Recommendation Model Objective**

The recommendation model minimizes the combined loss by updating its parameters to improve both ordinary ranking accuracy and resistance to adversarial perturbations.

This process encourages the model to learn parameter configurations that are less sensitive to small, harmful changes.

### 8.3.5. Adversarial Training Procedure

APR training can be described through the following sequence.

**Step 1: Train or Initialize the Recommendation Model**

A recommendation model is initialized or pretrained using the conventional BPR objective.

**Step 2: Calculate the Adversarial Perturbation**

The attacker examines the current model parameters and identifies a perturbation that increases the BPR loss.

The perturbation must satisfy the norm constraint:

$$
\lVert\Delta\rVert\le\epsilon
$$

**Step 3: Evaluate the Adversarial Loss**

The BPR loss is calculated using the perturbed parameters:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta+\Delta_{adv})
$$

**Step 4: Update the Recommendation Model**

The model parameters are updated to minimize the combined ordinary and adversarial losses.

**Step 5: Repeat the Process**

The attacker and recommendation model continue alternating their optimization steps.

Over successive iterations, the model learns to maintain better ranking performance under targeted perturbations.

### 8.3.6. Efficient Perturbation Approximation

Finding the exact adversarial perturbation can be computationally expensive.

Therefore, practical implementations often use gradient-based approximations.

The gradient of the BPR loss with respect to the perturbation indicates the direction in which the loss increases most rapidly.

A commonly used approximation is:

$$
\Delta_{adv}=\epsilon\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert}
$$

Where:

$$
\mathbf{g}=\nabla_{\Delta}\mathcal{L}_{BPR}(\Theta+\Delta)
$$

The gradient is typically evaluated at a specified initial perturbation, often $\Delta=0$.

This approximation selects a perturbation in the gradient direction and scales it to satisfy the norm constraint.

For a nonzero gradient and an Euclidean norm constraint, the resulting perturbation has magnitude $\epsilon$.

The approximation makes adversarial training computationally practical without requiring an exhaustive search over all possible perturbations.

### 8.3.7. Adversarial Matrix Factorization

When APR is applied to a matrix factorization recommendation model, the resulting approach is known as **Adversarial Matrix Factorization (AMF)**.

AMF combines:

- Matrix factorization for learning user and item latent representations.
- BPR for personalized ranking.
- Adversarial perturbations for improving robustness.

The adversarial mechanism identifies parameter configurations that are sensitive to small changes.

The recommendation model then learns to reduce these vulnerabilities.

This can improve generalization and ranking accuracy compared with conventional BPR training.

### 8.3.8. Advantages of APR

APR introduces adversarial regularization into personalized ranking.

Its principal advantages include:

**Improved Robustness**

The model is explicitly trained to withstand small, targeted perturbations to its parameters.

**Better Generalization**

Adversarial training can discourage overly sensitive parameter configurations, potentially improving performance on previously unseen interactions.

**Improved Ranking Accuracy**

By optimizing both ordinary and adversarial BPR losses, the model may learn more reliable preference rankings.

**Reduced Sensitivity to Perturbations**

The model becomes less vulnerable to small changes in the parameter space considered during training.

**Compatibility with Existing Recommendation Models**

APR can be incorporated into conventional BPR-based recommendation frameworks, including matrix factorization.

Experimental evaluations have reported improvements over standard BPR and several contemporary neural and adversarial recommendation methods on established benchmark datasets.

These results indicate that adversarial training can improve the robustness and predictive performance of personalized recommendation models.

However, robustness to parameter perturbations does not automatically guarantee robustness against every form of noisy data, manipulated interactions, or adversarial attack.

The effectiveness of APR depends on the perturbation strategy, regularization strength, underlying recommendation model, and characteristics of the dataset.
