In the previous section, we introduced IBCF and saw that its central operation is computing similarity among items. How can this similarity be measured? Fortunately, many of the measures used in UBCF to compare users can also be used here after a change in perspective.

## The Essential Shift: From Users to Items

Before examining individual measures, recall the fundamental change in viewpoint.

In UBCF, we searched for users whose preferences resembled those of the target user. We compared rows of the user-item matrix. Each row was a vector of one user's interactions, such as ratings, with all items.

In IBCF, we search for items resembling an item of interest to the user, such as an item the user recently viewed or purchased. We compare columns of the user-item matrix. Each column is a vector of all users' interactions, such as ratings, with one particular item.

Thus, an item's vector is the collection of ratings or interactions that different users have expressed toward that item. Rather than asking, “How similar is User 1 to User 2?”, we ask, “How similar is Item A to Item B, given how users interacted with both?”

## Common Measures of Item Similarity

### 1. Cosine Similarity

Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors. Its range is $[-1,1]$. For nonnegative vectors, such as visit counts, purchases, or positive ratings, its practical range is usually $[0,1]$.

For items $i$ and $j$, we extract their interaction vectors from the user-item matrix: columns $i$ and $j$. Cosine similarity then measures the directional alignment of those vectors:

$$Cosine(i,j)=\frac{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}r_{ui}r_{uj}}{\sqrt{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}r_{ui}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}r_{uj}^{2}}}$$

Here, $U_{ij}$ is the set of users who interacted with both items. Restricting the calculation to this set avoids treating a missing explicit rating as an actual rating of zero.

Cosine similarity is influenced by the relative magnitudes of vector entries, but it normalizes vector length. It therefore emphasizes alignment in interaction patterns rather than raw total activity alone.

**Advantages in IBCF:**

- It is relatively simple and computationally efficient.
- It often works well with sparse data.
- It is a useful baseline for explicit ratings and is especially common for nonnegative implicit signals, including binary viewed/not-viewed data.

**Limitations in IBCF:**

- It does not directly account for user-specific rating bias. A consistently generous rater and a consistently strict rater can distort similarity when raw explicit ratings are used.
- It can overstate similarity between widely rated or popular items unless the implementation applies appropriate shrinkage, weighting, or filtering.

Use cosine similarity when a fast baseline based on general interaction patterns is needed, particularly for binary or other nonnegative implicit feedback.

### 2. Adjusted Cosine Similarity

Standard cosine similarity does not account for differences in users' rating scales. Some users tend to assign high ratings, whereas others tend to rate strictly. Adjusted cosine similarity addresses this issue by centering each rating around the corresponding user's mean rating before comparing items.

For each user $u$, instead of using $r_{ui}$ directly, it uses:

$$r_{ui}-\bar{r}_{u}$$

where $\bar{r}_{u}$ is the mean rating assigned by user $u$ across items they have rated. The adjusted cosine similarity between items $i$ and $j$ is:

$$sim(i,j)=\frac{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}(r_{ui}-\bar{r}_{u})(r_{uj}-\bar{r}_{u})}{\sqrt{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}(r_{ui}-\bar{r}_{u})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}(r_{uj}-\bar{r}_{u})^{2}}}$$

In this expression, $U_{ij}$ is the set of users who rated both items $i$ and $j$.

**Advantages in IBCF:**

- Subtracting each user's mean reduces the influence of individual rating bias, such as users who are consistently generous or consistently strict.
- It often provides a more appropriate comparison than standard cosine similarity for explicit-rating recommender systems.

For explicit ratings, adjusted cosine is often preferable to standard cosine similarity unless computational simplicity is the overriding concern.

### 3. Pearson Correlation Coefficient

The Pearson correlation coefficient measures the linear relationship between two variables. Its range is $[-1,1]$: $1$ indicates perfect positive correlation, $-1$ perfect negative correlation, and $0$ no linear correlation.

For items $i$ and $j$, Pearson correlation assesses whether users who rate item $i$ above or below its mean also tend to rate item $j$ above or below its mean. One item-based form is:

$$Pearson(i,j)=\frac{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}(r_{ui}-\bar{r}_{i})(r_{uj}-\bar{r}_{j})}{\sqrt{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}(r_{ui}-\bar{r}_{i})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{u\in U_{ij}}(r_{uj}-\bar{r}_{j})^{2}}}$$

Here, $\bar{r}_{i}$ and $\bar{r}_{j}$ are the mean ratings of items $i$ and $j$, respectively, calculated over the common-user set $U_{ij}$.

Adjusted cosine similarity and Pearson correlation are closely related in IBCF and can sometimes produce similar results. Their principal difference is the centering operation: adjusted cosine centers ratings using each **user's** mean, whereas this item-based Pearson form centers ratings using each **item's** mean among common users. Both methods reduce bias, but from different perspectives.

**Advantages in IBCF:**

- It controls for rating-scale effects through centering.
- It can represent negative relationships. For example, users who like one film may tend to dislike another.
- It is a strong option for explicit-rating data; the choice between Pearson and adjusted cosine should be evaluated empirically on the relevant dataset.

### 4. Jaccard Similarity and Other Set-Based Measures

The Jaccard index compares two sets. It is the size of their intersection divided by the size of their union:

$$Jaccard(A,B)=\frac{\lvert A\cap B\rvert}{\lvert A\cup B\rvert}$$

In IBCF, Jaccard similarity and related measures, such as the Dice coefficient, are well suited to implicit or binary feedback: purchased/not purchased, viewed/not viewed, or clicked/not clicked. The Jaccard value is the fraction of users who interacted with at least one of the two items and interacted with both.

**Advantages in IBCF:**

- It is simple and easy to interpret for implicit feedback.
- It can be computed efficiently.

**Limitations in IBCF:**

- It considers only the presence or absence of an interaction, not its frequency, duration, or value.
- It can yield misleading results for items with very different popularity levels. A highly popular item can co-occur with many other items even when the relationship is not substantively meaningful.

Jaccard similarity is most appropriate when interaction intensity is unavailable or unimportant and only the occurrence of an interaction matters.

## Example: Computing Item Similarity

Consider the following user-item rating matrix. Ratings range from 1 to 5, and a dash indicates no rating.

| User | Film 1 (F1) | Film 2 (F2) | Film 3 (F3) | User mean rating $\bar{r}_{u}$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Ali | 5 | 4 | 2 | $\frac{5+4+2}{3}\approx3.67$ |
| Sara | 4 | 5 | - | $\frac{4+5}{2}=4.5$ |
| Reza | 1 | 2 | 5 | $\frac{1+2+5}{3}\approx2.67$ |
| Maryam | - | 3 | 4 | $\frac{3+4}{2}=3.5$ |

We want to calculate the similarity between Film 1 and Film 2.

For explicit ratings, we use only users who rated both films: Ali, Sara, and Reza. The item vectors are therefore:

$$F1=(5,4,1)$$

$$F2=(4,5,2)$$

The dot product is:

$$F1\cdot F2=(5\cdot4)+(4\cdot5)+(1\cdot2)=42$$

The vector norms are:

$$\lVert F1\rVert=\sqrt{5^{2}+4^{2}+1^{2}}=\sqrt{42}$$

$$\lVert F2\rVert=\sqrt{4^{2}+5^{2}+2^{2}}=\sqrt{45}$$

Thus, the cosine similarity is:

$$Cosine(F1,F2)=\frac{42}{\sqrt{42}\cdot\sqrt{45}}=\frac{42}{\sqrt{1890}}\approx0.966$$

The result indicates a strong similarity in the raw rating patterns of the users who rated both films. It should nevertheless be interpreted carefully: the high value may partly reflect the fact that explicit ratings are positive-valued. Mean-centering with adjusted cosine or Pearson correlation can better distinguish relative preference patterns.

## Example: Jaccard Similarity for Implicit Feedback

Now suppose that the interaction matrix is binary, where $1$ means that a user watched a film and $0$ means that they did not.

| User | Film 1 (F1) | Film 2 (F2) | Film 3 (F3) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Ali | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Sara | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| Reza | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Maryam | 0 | 1 | 1 |

The users who watched Film 1 are:

$$U_{F1}=\{\text{Ali},\text{Sara},\text{Reza}\}$$

The users who watched Film 2 are:

$$U_{F2}=\{\text{Ali},\text{Sara},\text{Reza},\text{Maryam}\}$$

Their intersection and union are:

$$U_{F1}\cap U_{F2}=\{\text{Ali},\text{Sara},\text{Reza}\},\quad\lvert U_{F1}\cap U_{F2}\rvert=3$$

$$U_{F1}\cup U_{F2}=\{\text{Ali},\text{Sara},\text{Reza},\text{Maryam}\},\quad\lvert U_{F1}\cup U_{F2}\rvert=4$$

Therefore:

$$Jaccard(F1,F2)=\frac{\lvert U_{F1}\cap U_{F2}\rvert}{\lvert U_{F1}\cup U_{F2}\rvert}=\frac{3}{4}=0.75$$

This means that 75% of users who watched at least one of F1 and F2 watched both films.

## The Dice Similarity Coefficient

Another important set-based measure is the **Dice similarity coefficient**, also called the Sørensen-Dice coefficient. For two sets $A$ and $B$, it is defined as:

$$Dice(A,B)=\frac{2\cdot\lvert A\cap B\rvert}{\lvert A\rvert+\lvert B\rvert}$$

The terms $\lvert A\rvert$ and $\lvert B\rvert$ are the sizes of sets $A$ and $B$, while $\lvert A\cap B\rvert$ is the number of shared members. The coefficient ranges from $0$ to $1$. A value closer to $1$ indicates greater similarity; a value of $0$ indicates no overlap.

Suppose:

$$A=\{1,2,3,4\}$$

$$B=\{3,4,5,6\}$$

Their intersection is:

$$A\cap B=\{3,4\},\quad\lvert A\cap B\rvert=2$$

and both sets have four members:

$$\lvert A\rvert=4,\quad\lvert B\rvert=4$$

The Dice coefficient is therefore:

$$Dice(A,B)=\frac{2\cdot2}{4+4}=\frac{4}{8}=0.5$$

Thus, the Dice similarity between these sets is $0.5$.

## Choosing a Similarity Measure for IBCF

- **Data type:** Are the data explicit ratings or implicit interactions? For explicit ratings, adjusted cosine similarity or Pearson correlation is often appropriate. For implicit feedback, Jaccard or Dice can be suitable.
- **Extreme sparsity:** When only one or two users have rated both items, an estimated similarity can be noisy and unreliable. Significance weighting can give greater confidence to similarities supported by more common users; alternatively, a system can impose a minimum co-rater threshold.
- **Popularity bias:** Highly popular items tend to appear similar to many other items, especially under co-occurrence measures, even when the semantic relationship is weak. Approaches such as lift or inverse-frequency weighting can reduce this bias.
- **Business objective:** The meaning of similarity may depend on the objective. A retailer may want to identify complementary items, such as a phone and its protective case, rather than only substitute items, such as two competing phones.

The next section uses the item-item similarity matrix created by these measures to examine the precise process of rating prediction and recommendation-list generation in IBCF.

## Summary

Item similarity is the central signal in IBCF. Cosine similarity provides a fast normalized comparison, adjusted cosine reduces user-rating bias, Pearson measures centered linear association, and Jaccard and Dice compare sets of implicit interactions. The most appropriate measure depends on the feedback type, data sparsity, popularity distribution, and recommendation objective.
